🎯 Real-world scenario

What we're building & where it's used

We're designing a global on-demand video-streaming service with a curated catalog — a service that ingests studio masters, transcodes them into hundreds of device-specific renditions, delivers those bytes to a viewer in Nairobi or Nagoya with sub-second startup, and personalizes every homepage row for every profile. This is the archetype for Netflix, and closely related to Amazon Prime Video, Disney+, Hulu, HBO Max, Apple TV+, and Hotstar/JioCinema. Netflix differs from YouTube in one critical way: the catalog is curated and finite, not user-uploaded — which changes the CDN math dramatically.

Companies build this because streaming has replaced broadcast television, and doing it well is the difference between a Sunday-night binge and an angry churn. The design touches every core distributed-systems primitive: a globally-distributed CDN (which Netflix famously operates itself as Open Connect), multi-region caching (EVCache on Memcached), distributed databases (Cassandra for viewing history, MySQL for billing), massive asynchronous pipelines (Kafka at 8 M events/sec peak), and a personalization stack that ranks the entire catalog per user per session. It's a favorite interview question because a good answer proves you can reason about the whole stack — not just a REST endpoint.

🧑‍🎓 In laymen terms

Explained without any tech words

The everyday problem. You come home, kick off your shoes, sit on the couch, and press play on a movie. It starts in about a second, looks crisp, and doesn't stutter — no matter whether you're in Tokyo, Toronto, or a small town in South Africa. That is genuinely magical, and the entire business of Netflix is making that one second feel effortless for hundreds of millions of people at the same time.

The story of one tap. When you press play, Netflix doesn't fly the movie to you from a warehouse in California — that would take too long. Instead, Netflix has quietly shipped little fridges of hard drives (yes, real physical boxes) into your internet provider's building down the road, and it fills them up overnight with the movies most people in your neighborhood are likely to watch. So when you press play, the movie is not travelling across the ocean — it's coming from a box that is maybe a mile from your couch. That's why it starts so fast.

The homepage magic. Before you even press play, Netflix has to decide which shows to show you. The homepage rows — "Trending Now," "Because You Watched…," "Top 10 in your country" — are not the same for anyone else. Netflix ran a giant matching game in the background all day, guessing which rows would tempt you the most, and even picked a different thumbnail image for the same show based on what it thinks you find eye-catching. All of that had to be ready the millisecond you opened the app.

The hard part. The tricky bit is Sunday evening in every timezone. Everyone finishes dinner, everyone wants to watch, and suddenly Netflix is the biggest single tenant on the entire internet — at peak, more than a tenth of the world's internet traffic is Netflix videos moving through the pipes. If they got it even slightly wrong — one crashed database, one overloaded cache — you'd see a spinning circle instead of a show, and you'd cancel your subscription. The whole design is built around never showing that spinning circle, even when small pieces break in the background.

📋 Requirements & scale

Requirements and back-of-the-envelope math

Functional requirements

  • Browse a curated catalog, with personalized rows and search.
  • Stream on 2 200+ device types with adaptive bitrate (HLS/DASH).
  • Resume exactly where you left off — on any device.
  • Recommend what to watch next, personalize every row and every thumbnail.
  • Manage profiles, subscriptions, billing.
  • Ingest studio masters, transcode into 1 100+ device-specific renditions per title.

Non-functional requirements

  • Startup latency < 1 second — the "TTFF" (time to first frame) target.
  • Availability > strong consistency everywhere except billing. A stale view count is fine; a stuck play button is fatal.
  • Global reach: viewers on every continent, on 3G to gigabit fiber.
  • Read-heavy: ~9:1 read/write ratio on viewing history alone; on catalog metadata, closer to 10 000:1.
  • Peak-heavy: evening prime-time in each region is ~5–8× the daily average.

🏗️ High-level architecture

How the pieces fit together

The whole system splits into three planes. The control plane runs on AWS: microservices behind the Zuul gateway, EVCache in front of Cassandra and MySQL, Kafka for events. Its job is to answer "who are you, what can you watch, where did you leave off, what should we recommend?" — and to hand the client a signed manifest URL. The data plane is Open Connect: Netflix's own CDN, with servers embedded directly inside ISPs and IXPs. It stores the transcoded video and serves 98%+ of playback bytes without ever touching AWS. The offline plane is the ingest+transcode pipeline plus the ML/recommendation training pipeline — everything that runs on Spark and EMR while you sleep.

Sync API call (control plane) Video bytes (data plane) Async / offline

The magic move is that the two planes only meet once, at manifest time. The client asks AWS "what should I play, and where's my personalized homepage?" — AWS returns metadata + a manifest URL. From that moment on, every byte of video comes from Open Connect, never from AWS. That's how Netflix keeps its AWS bill tractable and its startup latency low.

🔍 Component deep-dive

1. Open Connect — a private CDN inside your ISP

Netflix built its own CDN because the commercial CDNs of 2011 couldn't handle its egress economics. Open Connect deploys physical appliances — OCAs (Open Connect Appliances) — in two places: embedded inside ISP networks (free of charge to qualifying ISPs, over 1 000+ ISP partners), and clustered at internet exchange points (IXPs) via settlement-free interconnect (SFI). Each OCA is packed with HDDs and NVMe SSDs, and each is prefilled during the ISP's off-peak window (usually 2–6 AM local) with the titles most likely to be watched in that region the next day. When you press play in Tokyo, the bytes are already sitting on a box a mile from your couch. Netflix reports the CDN hit rate at ~98% — only ~2% of playback traffic escapes back toward AWS. This is the single biggest reason startup latency (TTFF) is under a second: the bytes never travel far.

2. Streaming — DASH/HLS adaptive bitrate at rendition-level extreme fan-out

Every title is transcoded into ~1 100–1 200 renditions: resolutions from 240p through 4K HDR, codecs H.264 (broad), H.265/HEVC (bandwidth-efficient), VP9 (older Android), AV1 (newer), audio in dozens of languages and multiple bitrates (stereo, 5.1, Atmos), plus subtitles. Netflix uses per-title encoding and per-shot encoding — running an optimization to find the best bitrate ladder for each individual title (a talky drama needs fewer bits than a Marvel action sequence). At playback, the client uses DASH or HLS adaptive bitrate: it measures its own bandwidth and buffer level and switches rendition between segments. This is why a viewer whose Wi-Fi drops from strong to weak sees the picture dim slightly instead of buffering.

3. Startup latency — how to make TTFF < 1 second

Reducing time-to-first-frame is a design theme, not one component. Key techniques: (a) the manifest is tiny — a few KB — and can be fetched from AWS in ~100 ms via anycast + regional caching; (b) segments are pre-warmed in the OCA closest to you before you even open the app — the "top 10 in your country" is on the local box; (c) parallel probes at manifest time — the client fires off connections to multiple OCAs and races them; (d) the first segment is intentionally short (2–4 s vs 6–8 s later segments) so the buffer fills fast; (e) the initial rendition is chosen conservatively — start at a bitrate the client is likely to sustain, then ratchet up. In an interview, name these five tactics: "manifest, prewarm, parallel probes, short-first-segment, conservative-first-bitrate."

4. Caching — EVCache and the "cold cache is worse than no cache" problem

EVCache is Netflix's open-source wrapper around Memcached. It's designed for the failure mode Netflix cares about: cold caches during a region failover. Writes are propagated to all replica nodes and cross-region; reads hit only the nearest cluster. This means the cache is full and warm on both coasts, so when a region evacuates, the incoming region doesn't stampede Cassandra. Metadata caches (title info, homepage rows) commonly hit > 99%. This pattern — write-fan-out, read-local — is the reason Netflix can survive an entire AWS region failure without visible impact to the user.

5. Distributed databases — Cassandra + MySQL, each for a specific reason

Cassandra holds the write-heavy, eventually-consistent workloads: viewing history (~9:1 write:read), user activity, playback events. Netflix runs 50+ Cassandra clusters, 500+ nodes, with the biggest cluster handling 250 000 writes per second. To keep viewing-history reads fast, Netflix splits it into a LiveVH (recent, uncompressed rows) and CompressedVH (older rows, compressed and denser) — a classic hot/cold tier. MySQL is reserved for the strongly-consistent workloads: billing, subscriptions, payment. Netflix runs MySQL master-master with InnoDB and cross-region read replicas; failover is DNS-driven via Route 53.

6. Event pipeline — Kafka, at eye-watering scale

Every play, pause, scrub, rating, and click flows into Kafka: ~500 billion events per day, peak 8 million events per second, ~24 GB/s, ~1.3 PB/day. Consumers include: the recommendation feature store (real-time signals), Elasticsearch (for on-call ops and CS tooling), the analytics warehouse (S3 → Spark → Redshift-equivalent), and the QoE (quality-of-experience) telemetry pipeline that measures whether that spinning circle ever appeared. Kafka being the "spinal cord" of the data plane is a common answer at Netflix-scale interviews.

7. Recommendations — two-stage ranking + contextual bandits + artwork personalization

The recommendation problem at Netflix is not just "what should the user watch next" — it's "which rows should be on the homepage, in what order, with what titles in each row, and which thumbnail image for each title." The high-level architecture is a two-stage funnel: (1) candidate generation narrows the ~15 000-title catalog to a few thousand per profile using collaborative filtering, embeddings, and content-based signals; (2) ranking — a heavier GBDT/neural model — orders those candidates using rich features (device, time of day, session context, freshness). On top of ranking sits a contextual bandit for row ordering and for thumbnail personalization: Netflix has multiple thumbnail images per title and learns which image drives clicks for which taste cluster. Training runs on Spark; online serving is a low-latency service in every region. Model retraining is daily; real-time signals (this session's clicks) feed a feature store so the recommendations adapt as you browse.

8. Handling peak traffic — autoscale, chaos-test, and cache-warm

Netflix does not scale reactively — it scales predictively. Traffic is highly seasonal (evenings in each region + weekends + release windows), so services are pre-scaled before evening prime time. Chaos engineering (Chaos Monkey, Simian Army, ChAP) constantly kills nodes and even entire regions in production to prove the failover paths still work. Regional evacuations are practiced monthly. The zone-based load balancer is two-tier — DNS round-robin across zones, then round-robin across instances within a zone — so a zone loss doesn't cascade. Combined with the full cross-region cache in EVCache, the failure of an entire AWS region is a non-event for the viewer.

Viewing history Wide-row Cassandra by userId; time-sorted; TTL on LiveVH Write-heavy 9:1 ratio; append-only + tombstones handled by compaction.
Candidate generation (recs) Approximate nearest neighbor over user+item embeddings (HNSW/FAISS) Sub-linear (~O(log N)) over the catalog vs O(N) brute force.

The one to remember: Zipf-driven CDN prefill. The reason Open Connect hits 98% is not clever caching — it's that 90% of watches concentrate on 10% of titles, and Netflix ships those bytes to your ISP overnight.

Deployment story: services run active-active across 3 AWS regions (Netflix uses us-east-1, us-west-2, eu-west-1). Route 53 latency-based routing pins users to the nearest region; a regional evacuation just flips the health check and cache-warm-elsewhere ensures no thundering-herd on the surviving regions. S3 replicates masters cross-region so a regional loss cannot destroy the catalog. On the data plane, either Open Connect appliances in ISPs (Netflix's actual setup) or CloudFront with Origin Shield (the AWS-native equivalent) serves 95–98% of playback bytes locally.

💻 C# implementation

Two pieces of the design in C#: (1) the playback authorization endpoint that reads user state from EVCache/Cassandra and returns a signed manifest URL, and (2) a compact ABR (adaptive bitrate) selector the player uses to pick the next segment. These two together are the seam between the control plane and the data plane — exactly what an interviewer wants you to code.

Playback authorization (ASP.NET Core minimal API)

using Amazon.S3;
using Amazon.S3.Model;
using Enyim.Caching;                // Memcached client (EVCache-equivalent)
using Cassandra;                    // Datastax C# driver

var app = WebApplication.CreateBuilder(args).Build();

// One request: user opens a title → we return the signed manifest URL
// and the "resume at" position. This is the LAST time AWS is in the loop
// for this playback session — every subsequent byte comes from Open Connect.
app.MapGet("/playback/{titleId}", async (
        string titleId,
        HttpContext ctx,
        IMemcachedClient cache,
        ICluster c) =>
{
    var userId = ctx.User.FindFirst("sub")!.Value;

    // 1) Hot path: EVCache lookup for "resume position" — 99%+ hit rate.
    var cacheKey = $"resume:{userId}:{titleId}";
    var resumeSec = await cache.GetValueAsync<int?>(cacheKey);

    if (resumeSec is null)
    {
        // 2) Cache miss → Cassandra. Wide-row on userId; latest viewing rows first.
        using var session = await c.ConnectAsync("viewing");
        var ps = await session.PrepareAsync(
            "SELECT position_sec FROM history WHERE user_id=? AND title_id=? LIMIT 1");
        var row = (await session.ExecuteAsync(ps.Bind(userId, titleId))).FirstOrDefault();
        resumeSec = row?.GetValue<int>("position_sec") ?? 0;

        // Write-through so the next request is a cache hit.
        await cache.SetAsync(cacheKey, resumeSec.Value, TimeSpan.FromMinutes(10));
    }

    // 3) Pick the OCA closest to the client. In Open Connect this is BGP + DNS
    //    steering; on AWS you'd let CloudFront do it automatically.
    var ocaHost = OcaRouter.PickHostFor(ctx.Connection.RemoteIpAddress!);

    // 4) Mint a short-lived signed URL for the manifest (segment URLs inside
    //    the manifest are also signed). This is the whole handoff to the CDN.
    var manifestUrl = SignedUrl.Build(
        host:   ocaHost,
        path:   $"/manifests/{titleId}/master.m3u8",
        ttl:    TimeSpan.FromHours(6),
        userId: userId);

    return Results.Ok(new {
        titleId,
        manifestUrl,
        resumeSec,
        drmLicenseUrl = "https://drm.netflix.example/widevine"
    });
});

app.Run();

Client-side ABR selector (segment-by-segment)

// Buffer-based ABR: choose the next segment's bitrate based on how much
// buffer we already have + measured throughput. This is the algorithm
// running inside every Netflix client to keep playback smooth without
// buffering. Similar in spirit to BOLA/BBA published algorithms.
public sealed class AbrSelector
{
    private readonly int[] _ladderKbps;         // e.g. [400, 1200, 3000, 6000, 12000]
    private double _emaKbps = 2000;              // EWMA of measured throughput
    private const double Alpha = 0.3;             // smoothing factor

    public AbrSelector(int[] ladderKbps) => _ladderKbps = ladderKbps;

    // Called after each segment download completes.
    public void ObserveSegment(long bytes, TimeSpan elapsed)
    {
        var kbps = (bytes * 8.0) / (elapsed.TotalSeconds * 1000);
        _emaKbps = Alpha * kbps + (1 - Alpha) * _emaKbps;
    }

    // Pick the bitrate for the next segment.
    public int PickBitrateKbps(double bufferSec)
    {
        // Safety headroom: never request a bitrate above 80% of measured throughput.
        var ceiling = _emaKbps * 0.8;

        // If buffer is tiny (< 8s), be very conservative — avoid the rebuffer cliff.
        // If buffer is fat (> 30s), we can afford to reach for the top of the ladder.
        var allowanceFactor = bufferSec switch {
            < 8   => 0.6,
            < 15  => 0.8,
            < 30  => 1.0,
            _         => 1.2
        };
        var target = ceiling * allowanceFactor;

        // Pick the highest rung of the ladder we can afford.
        var pick = _ladderKbps[0];
        foreach (var r in _ladderKbps)
            if (r <= target) pick = r;
        return pick;
    }
}

Two shapes to notice. The authorization endpoint is thin — cache first, DB fallback, mint a signed URL, return. That's it. The real complexity lives on the client: bitrate selection, segment prefetch, decoder buffer management. This is the correct division of labor for a streaming service — put the intelligence at the edge where the network conditions are actually known.

⚖️ Trade-offs, bottlenecks & likely follow-ups

Where it can break

The three real bottlenecks are evening prime-time egress in a single metro (solved by prefilled OCAs), Cassandra hot partitions from a global superstar profile (solved by wide-row bucketing and TTL'd LiveVH), and recommendation freshness lag — training is daily but users expect instant reactions to their behavior (solved by real-time features and contextual bandits on top of the stale ranker). A subtle failure mode: a rendition that's bad but shipped everywhere — Netflix mitigates this by rolling out new encodes gradually and A/B-testing quality metrics (VMAF) against real users.

Consistency vs availability

Everything user-facing chooses availability. Viewing history, resume positions, view counts, likes are all eventually consistent — you might see a title show up on two devices' "continue watching" rows a few seconds apart. Billing is the exception: it lives in MySQL with ACID semantics because charging you twice would be worse than a spinning circle. In an interview, name this split: "read paths are AP; the money path is CP."

Likely follow-up questions (with crisp answers)

Why did Netflix build its own CDN instead of using Akamai or CloudFront?

Egress economics at their scale. Netflix is ~10-15% of North American peak internet traffic; commercial CDN pricing on that volume was untenable. Open Connect also gives them tighter control over prefill, telemetry, and codec rollouts — they own the whole video path.

How does an OCA know what to prefill?

Netflix runs a daily popularity forecast per region using recent viewing patterns and release calendars. Each OCA gets a manifest of what to prefill overnight; content moves from the tier-2 regional cluster to the OCA during ISP off-peak hours.

What happens if a whole AWS region goes down?

Route 53 fails DNS over to the surviving region(s); Zuul + microservices are already running there active-active. The critical trick is EVCache is write-fan-out cross-region, so the surviving region's cache is already warm — no thundering herd against Cassandra.

How does Netflix personalize thumbnails?

Each title has several thumbnail candidates. A contextual multi-armed bandit per user cluster tests which image drives the most clicks. Explore vs exploit is tuned per session — you might see the same title with a different thumbnail depending on which taste cluster you're in.

Why is the first HLS segment shorter than later segments?

To fill the buffer fast and start playback in under a second. A short first segment costs a bit more manifest overhead but earns a huge TTFF win. Subsequent segments are longer (6–8s) for cache-hit efficiency and fewer HTTP requests.

Why Cassandra for viewing history — not DynamoDB?

Wide-partition user-keyed workloads with a 9:1 write:read ratio; also, historical reason — Netflix adopted Cassandra pre-DynamoDB maturity. On a fresh AWS build, DynamoDB with adaptive capacity would be a perfectly good substitute.

How does chaos engineering fit in?

Netflix continuously runs Chaos Monkey (kills instances), Chaos Kong (evacuates regions), and ChAP (chaos experiments in production) so failure paths never atrophy. Regional evacuation is practiced monthly; that's how they can promise no visible impact from an AWS region loss.
