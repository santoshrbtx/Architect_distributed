The Saga Pattern

The recommended alternative to distributed 2PC transactions across microservices — a sequence of local transactions coordinated by events or an orchestrator, with compensations instead of rollbacks. From Chris Richardson's Microservices Patterns and Sam Newman's Building Microservices.

🎯 Real-world scenario

Why sagas exist

A customer clicks "Place Order" on Amazon. Behind that one click, four services must all agree: Order Service creates the order, Payment Service charges the card, Inventory Service reserves stock, and Shipping Service schedules a courier. Each service owns its own database (the Database-per-Service pattern). If step three fails — the warehouse is out of stock — you cannot leave the customer's card charged. You need a way to either complete all four steps, or undo the ones already done.

In the monolith days you would wrap the whole thing in BEGIN TRANSACTION … COMMIT. In microservices that's off the table: each service has a different database, and even when they don't, two-phase commit (2PC) doesn't work well across services — it couples them, holds locks across network hops, and one hung participant blocks everyone. Chris Richardson's Microservices Patterns and Sam Newman's Building Microservices both name the same alternative: the Saga pattern.

A saga is a sequence of local transactions. Each service commits its own change and emits a message. The next service reacts. If any step fails, the saga runs compensating transactions — new local transactions that semantically undo the earlier ones. Amazon uses this pattern for orders. Uber uses it for rides. Netflix uses it for subscription lifecycles. Every SaaS billing system does something like it. This page explains it deeply enough that you can whiteboard it in an interview and implement it on a real team.

🧑‍🎓 In plain English

The story, told without any jargon

Imagine booking a foreign holiday. You want a hotel room in Paris, a plane ticket to get there, and a rental car when you arrive. Three separate companies, three separate systems. None of them talks to the others.

The old way (a distributed transaction) is to say: "everyone, hold your bookings — I'll confirm once I've heard back from all of you. If anyone can't do it, we all cancel." In the real world this is silly — you can't ask an airline to freeze a seat while you wait to hear back from a hotel. Airlines don't work that way, and neither do most software services.

The saga way is what a human actually does: book them one at a time, in order. Book the hotel. If confirmed, book the flight. If confirmed, book the car. If the car company says "sorry, out of cars", you don't undo them with a magic reversal — instead, you cancel the flight and cancel the hotel by phoning them back. That "phone call to cancel" is what the pattern calls a compensating transaction. It doesn't rewind the booking — it just makes a new booking that means "please undo the last one".

There are two ways to run the sequence. Either each service knows what to do next ("when the hotel confirms, the flight service should book automatically") — that's choreography. Or you can have a single conductor — a travel agent — who calls each company one by one and decides when to stop or compensate. That's orchestration. Both work; the trade-off is coupling versus centralisation.

The catch: while the booking is in flight, the world is in an in-between state. Your credit card has been charged for the hotel while you're waiting for the flight. If someone else looks at your card in that instant, they see a charge that might still be undone. Traditional database transactions hide this ("isolation"); sagas don't. So you have to design around it — for example, mark the hotel booking as "pending" until every step succeeds. That's the whole idea. Book one thing at a time; if something breaks, phone back and cancel; be honest that the world is briefly in-progress.

📋 Why 2PC isn't an option

What the saga replaces, and what it gives up

A distributed transaction (usually two-phase commit) is elegant on paper: a coordinator asks all participants "can you commit?", waits for a unanimous yes, then tells them "commit". Everyone commits atomically. Locks are held across the network. Beautiful — until a participant crashes with a prepared transaction still open, and now every other participant is blocked, holding locks, until an operator intervenes. This is why the microservices community rejects it:

Concern2PCSaga
CoordinationSynchronous, blockingAsynchronous, message-driven
LockingCross-service locks held during prepareNo cross-service locks; each service commits locally
CouplingTight — every service must implement XA / TransactionManager protocolLoose — services exchange domain events / commands
Failure toleranceA crashed prepared participant blocks everyoneFailed step triggers compensations; no blocking
ACIDFull — A, C, I, D across servicesACD only. Isolation is not guaranteed. Consistency is eventual.
RollbackAutomatic (undo log)Manual — developer writes compensating transactions
ComplexitySimple mental model, complex operationsComplex mental model, simple operations
LatencyLatency of slowest participant × 2 (prepare + commit)Latency of critical path only
Cloud-nativenessPoor — RDBMS + XA requiredExcellent — event-driven, works with any store

The trade the saga makes, in one line: you trade automatic rollback and isolation for loose coupling, availability, and cloud-scale. This is essentially never worth doing for a single service (use ACID). It is almost always worth doing across services.

🧩 Core concepts you must be able to name

These are the vocabulary items an interviewer will assume you know. Master them and you sound senior.

TermMeaning
Local transactionA normal ACID transaction inside one service. The atom of a saga.
Compensating transactionA new local transaction that semantically undoes a previously committed one. e.g. ChargeCard → RefundCard. Not a rollback; a business-level reversal.
Compensable transactionA step for which a compensation is possible (and defined).
Retryable transactionA step that follows the pivot; must be idempotent and will be retried until it succeeds.
Pivot transactionThe point of no return. Once it commits, compensations of earlier steps stop being business-valid. Everything after the pivot must be retried until it eventually succeeds.
ChoreographyCoordination style where services publish events; peers react. No central controller.
OrchestrationCoordination style where a central orchestrator commands each participant and decides the flow. Typically implemented as a state machine.
Saga logA durable record of what steps the saga has executed. Lets a crashed orchestrator resume.
Correlation IDAn ID stamped on every message in a saga so replies can be matched to the right saga instance.
ACD (not ACID)What sagas provide: Atomicity via compensations, Consistency eventually, Durability via local commits + reliable messaging. Isolation is dropped.
Outbox patternAtomically writing the DB update and the outgoing message to the same DB transaction, then publishing later. This is how you avoid "committed the row but never sent the event".
Idempotent handlerConsumer stores a de-dup key so a retried message doesn't apply twice. Non-negotiable.
🏗️ Architecture — the two flavours

Choreography vs Orchestration, side by side

Both approaches drive the same saga; the difference is where the flow logic lives. In a real system you may mix them: choreography for simple flows, orchestration for the ones that need audit and error recovery.

Domain event Command / reply Orchestrator
Choreography wins when
  • The flow is short (2–3 services).
  • Steps are naturally driven by domain events.
  • You want no central single point of failure.
  • Services already publish rich domain events for other reasons.
Orchestration wins when
  • The flow has 4+ services, branches, or retries.
  • You need a clear audit trail of the saga state.
  • You need to add new steps without touching every service.
  • Failure recovery / debugging must be centralised.
The failure modes to name: choreography suffers from cyclic dependencies (Service A listens for B's events, B for A's) and hidden workflow (no single place shows the full sequence). Orchestration introduces a single point of failure (the orchestrator must be highly-available) and God-service risk (business logic leaks into the orchestrator). Interviewers reward you for volunteering these before being asked.

🔍 Deep-dive — the details Chris Richardson & Sam Newman emphasise

Local transactions plus reliable messaging

Every saga step is a normal ACID transaction inside one service. The tricky bit is atomically committing the data change AND sending the next message. If you write to the DB and then the process dies before publishing, the saga stalls. If you publish first and then the DB write fails, you have a phantom event. The community answer, cited in both books, is the Transactional Outbox pattern: write the event to an outbox table in the same DB transaction as the business update, and have a separate polling process (or Debezium-style change-data-capture) publish outbox rows to the broker. Same for the reverse direction — the Inbox pattern deduplicates incoming messages by message-id.

Compensating transactions must be designed, not derived

There is no automatic compensation. A refund isn't a "reverse of the charge" — it's a new charge with an opposite sign, with different business rules (partial refund? fee? refund window?). Every step of a saga must ship with its designed compensation. Steps that cannot be compensated (email sent, missile launched) become the pivot — the point past which the saga must complete forward, not backward.

Retryable vs Compensable vs Pivot — the three step classes

Chris Richardson breaks each step into one of three classes:

  • Compensable — a step for which a compensating transaction is defined. Anything before the pivot.
  • Pivot — the go / no-go boundary. If it succeeds, the saga will complete forward, no matter how many retries it takes on later steps.
  • Retryable — a step after the pivot that is idempotent and will be retried until it eventually succeeds. It cannot fail permanently.

Identifying the pivot is the design decision that shapes everything. In "Place Order", the pivot is often ReserveStock — after stock is reserved you must ship it, refund becomes accounting-only.

Orchestrator as a state machine

The orchestrator is best modelled as an explicit state machine — PENDING → PAYMENT_APPROVED → STOCK_RESERVED → SHIPPED → APPROVED, with error transitions PAYMENT_FAILED, STOCK_FAILED, and each error mapping to the specific compensations to run. Frameworks like Eventuate Tram Sagas, Axon, MassTransit Saga State Machines, Temporal.io, and cloud-native AWS Step Functions exist for exactly this. Rolling your own is a rite of passage the community strongly recommends against beyond toy examples.

The saga log & correlation ID

Every saga instance writes to a durable saga log (in Postgres, DynamoDB, or a dedicated store) at each step boundary, so a crashed orchestrator can resume. Every message carries a correlation ID (typically the saga instance ID) that participants echo in replies. This is what makes debugging tractable: you can grep the correlation ID across every service's logs and reconstruct the whole story.

Client response strategies

A POST /orders starts a saga that may take seconds to finish. You have three options for responding to the client:

  1. Block until done — simple, but ties HTTP request lifetime to saga latency. Fine for <1 s sagas.
  2. Return an ID; client polls — POST /orders returns 202 Accepted { id }; client GETs /orders/{id}. This is the most common pattern.
  3. Return an ID; server pushes — websocket, SSE, or a webhook fires when the saga completes. Best UX; more infrastructure.

Testing a saga

Unit-test each step in isolation. Integration-test the orchestrator against contract-mocked participants. End-to-end test the happy path plus every compensation path in a real environment. Chaos-test by injecting failures at each step and verifying the system lands in a consistent state. If you skip the compensation tests you will ship a broken system — this is the biggest production hazard.

🚧 Isolation — the "I" the saga drops, and how to defend

ACID's Isolation means concurrent transactions look sequential. Sagas can't guarantee this — while a saga is in flight, other sagas can see and modify the same data. Chris Richardson lists three specific anomalies and six countermeasures that every saga-shipping architect must know.

The three anomalies

AnomalyWhat happens
Lost updatesTwo sagas write the same data; the second overwrites the first's write, unaware.
Dirty readsA saga reads data that another saga has changed and might still compensate (i.e., "unroll"). The read is of a value that will vanish.
Fuzzy / non-repeatable readsTwo reads within the same saga see different values because another saga wrote in between.

The six countermeasures

CountermeasureHow
Semantic lockMark the affected row as "pending / in a saga" (e.g. order.state = PENDING). Other operations see the flag and either wait or short-circuit. Released when the saga finishes or compensates.
Commutative updatesDesign updates so ordering doesn't matter — e.g. balance += 10 instead of balance = 110. Sagas can run in any interleaving and still converge.
Pessimistic viewReorder the saga so updates happen in retryable (post-pivot) steps, not compensable ones. Prevents dirty reads.
Reread valueBefore applying an update, re-read the row and confirm it hasn't changed. If it has, abort and restart the saga (optimistic concurrency).
Version fileKeep an ordered log of operations on a record. Apply them in the correct sequence to prevent conflicts.
By-value (risk-based)Choose a mechanism dynamically per risk. Low-value orders → saga; multi-million-dollar transfer → distributed transaction after all.
The most common in practice is semantic lock (an explicit PENDING state) combined with reread for optimistic concurrency. Together they cover 90% of real-world saga hazards.

💻 Code — a C# orchestrator saga sketch (MassTransit-style)

Here is a minimal, realistic C# orchestrator using explicit state, correlation-id, and compensations. This is close to what a MassTransit or Temporal.io implementation would look like in production.

// The saga's state — persisted after each step via the saga log.
public class OrderSagaState
{
    public Guid CorrelationId { get; set; }
    public string State { get; set; } = "PENDING";
    public int  OrderId { get; set; }
    public decimal AmountCharged { get; set; }
    public bool StockReserved { get; set; }
}

// The orchestrator — one method per state transition, one compensating handler each.
public class OrderSaga(ISagaRepository<OrderSagaState> repo, IBus bus, ILogger<OrderSaga> log)
{
    // ----- Happy path -----
    public async Task Handle(CreateOrderCommand cmd, CancellationToken ct)
    {
        var saga = new OrderSagaState { CorrelationId = Guid.NewGuid() };
        await repo.SaveAsync(saga, ct);                          // step 0 — saga log
        await bus.SendAsync(new CreateOrderRequested(saga.CorrelationId, cmd), ct);
    }

    public async Task On(OrderCreated ev, CancellationToken ct)
    {
        var saga = await repo.LoadAsync(ev.CorrelationId, ct);
        saga.OrderId = ev.OrderId; saga.State = "ORDER_CREATED";
        await repo.SaveAsync(saga, ct);
        await bus.SendAsync(new ChargeCardRequested(saga.CorrelationId, ev.OrderId, ev.Total), ct);
    }

    public async Task On(CardCharged ev, CancellationToken ct)
    {
        var saga = await repo.LoadAsync(ev.CorrelationId, ct);
        saga.AmountCharged = ev.Amount; saga.State = "PAYMENT_APPROVED";
        await repo.SaveAsync(saga, ct);
        await bus.SendAsync(new ReserveStockRequested(saga.CorrelationId, saga.OrderId), ct);
    }

    public async Task On(StockReserved ev, CancellationToken ct)   // PIVOT
    {
        var saga = await repo.LoadAsync(ev.CorrelationId, ct);
        saga.StockReserved = true; saga.State = "STOCK_RESERVED";   // no more compensations after this
        await repo.SaveAsync(saga, ct);
        await bus.SendAsync(new ScheduleShipmentRequested(saga.CorrelationId, saga.OrderId), ct);
    }

    public async Task On(ShipmentScheduled ev, CancellationToken ct)  // retryable — will retry forever
    {
        var saga = await repo.LoadAsync(ev.CorrelationId, ct);
        saga.State = "APPROVED";
        await repo.SaveAsync(saga, ct);
    }

    // ----- Failure path — compensations run in LIFO order -----
    public async Task On(StockReservationFailed ev, CancellationToken ct)
    {
        var saga = await repo.LoadAsync(ev.CorrelationId, ct);
        saga.State = "COMPENSATING"; await repo.SaveAsync(saga, ct);
        // compensate the charge first, then cancel the order
        await bus.SendAsync(new RefundCardRequested(saga.CorrelationId, saga.AmountCharged), ct);
    }
    public async Task On(CardRefunded ev, CancellationToken ct)
    {
        var saga = await repo.LoadAsync(ev.CorrelationId, ct);
        await bus.SendAsync(new CancelOrderRequested(saga.CorrelationId, saga.OrderId), ct);
    }
    public async Task On(OrderCancelled ev, CancellationToken ct)
    {
        var saga = await repo.LoadAsync(ev.CorrelationId, ct);
        saga.State = "REJECTED"; await repo.SaveAsync(saga, ct);   // consistent, failed
    }
}

Notice: every state transition is a persisted saga-log entry, every message carries the correlation-id, and each compensation is an explicit new command. There is no distributed transaction anywhere. Any service can crash and be restarted; the orchestrator will resume from the saga log.

Choreography variant — three services, no orchestrator

// Order Service — publishes an event when an order is created
public async Task Handle(CreateOrderCommand cmd) {
    using var tx = db.BeginTransaction();
    var order = db.Orders.Add(new Order { State = "PENDING", Total = cmd.Total });
    db.Outbox.Add(new OutboxEvent("OrderCreated", new { order.Id, order.Total })); // same tx
    await tx.CommitAsync();
}
// Payment Service — subscribes to OrderCreated
public async Task On(OrderCreated ev) {
    if (inbox.SeenBefore(ev.MessageId)) return;                    // idempotency
    var ok = await gateway.ChargeAsync(ev.OrderId, ev.Total);
    using var tx = db.BeginTransaction();
    db.Outbox.Add(new OutboxEvent(ok ? "CardCharged" : "CardChargeFailed", new { ev.OrderId }));
    await tx.CommitAsync();
}

Choreography feels lighter but the flow is only visible if you trace it across three log streams — the reason orchestration wins as complexity grows.

⚖️ Trade-offs & likely interview follow-ups

When to use — and when NOT to

  • Use when a business operation spans two or more services with independent databases, and you cannot use 2PC.
  • Don't use when everything is inside one service (just use a DB transaction), or when compensations are impossible/dangerous (real-world side-effects — physical shipment, external payment) — those need pivots and manual escalation.
  • Don't use when the business genuinely needs strong isolation (financial ledgers with regulatory constraints) — consider a real distributed transaction or a single write-authoritative service.

What breaks in production

  • Missing idempotency: at-least-once delivery causes double-charges. Every handler must store a seen-message-id.
  • Unwritten compensations: teams ship the happy path and skip the failure paths. Guarantee: production will find them.
  • Cross-service transactions creep back: someone adds a sync HTTP call from Order → Payment "just to check". This is 2PC in disguise.
  • Orchestrator becomes God-service: business rules for every domain leak into the orchestrator. Keep it as a state machine, not a business layer.
  • Long-running sagas: some business operations take days (fraud review). Use a workflow engine (Temporal, Step Functions) — do not build durable timers yourself.

Likely interview questions

Q: How does a saga guarantee "at-least-once" delivery doesn't cause double compensation?

Every handler is idempotent — stores a message-id in an Inbox table and short-circuits on replay. Compensations are themselves idempotent for the same reason.

Q: What is a pivot transaction and why does it matter?

The pivot is the go/no-go step. Before it, failures compensate. After it, failures retry. Identifying it defines the shape of the saga — everything before must be compensable; everything after must be idempotent and always-eventually-succeed.

Q: Choreography or orchestration — which do you pick?

Choreography for 2–3-service flows dominated by domain events. Orchestration for 4+ services, branching logic, complex compensations, or when observability of the whole flow matters. Real systems mix both.

Q: Sagas lose Isolation. How do you handle it?

Semantic locks (an explicit PENDING state), commutative updates (deltas, not absolutes), optimistic concurrency (reread + version), and pessimistic ordering (do updates after the pivot). Chris Richardson lists six countermeasures — mention two or three by name.

Q: How do you ensure the event is published exactly when the DB write commits?

Transactional Outbox: write the event to an outbox table in the same DB transaction as the business row, then a poller or Debezium ships the outbox to the broker. There is no XA and no phantom events.

Q: What happens if the orchestrator crashes mid-saga?

Nothing — the saga log is durable. On restart, the orchestrator reads the last state, re-sends the pending command (idempotent), and continues.

Q: How do you test a saga?

Unit-test each step; contract-test between services; integration-test happy + every compensation path; chaos-test by injecting failures at every step. Skipping the compensation tests is the #1 production hazard.

🗣️ Interview cheat sheet

30-second pitch: "A saga is the recommended alternative to distributed 2PC in a microservices architecture. It breaks a business transaction into a sequence of local transactions, each committed inside its own service, coordinated by events (choreography) or by an orchestrator (a state machine). If a step fails, the saga runs compensating transactions — new local transactions that semantically undo the earlier ones — in reverse order back to the point of failure. Sagas give you A, C, and D of ACID but drop I (isolation), so you handle concurrency with semantic locks, commutative updates, optimistic concurrency, or pivot ordering. Reliable messaging is provided by the Transactional Outbox pattern; idempotency by an Inbox table. The two go-to books are Chris Richardson's Microservices Patterns and Sam Newman's Building Microservices. On AWS, use Step Functions for orchestrators and SNS+SQS or EventBridge for choreography."

Five talking points that win the round

  • "2PC is not an option in microservices — sagas replace it, at the cost of automatic rollback and isolation."
  • "The pivot is the design decision that shapes the whole saga." Everything before is compensable, everything after is idempotent-retryable.
  • "Isolation is lost — recover it with semantic locks and optimistic concurrency." Naming Richardson's countermeasures = senior signal.
  • "Reliable messaging via the Transactional Outbox." Idempotency via the Inbox. Non-negotiable.
  • "Orchestrator as a state machine, not a business layer." Use Step Functions / Temporal, not ad-hoc code.

Numbers & principles to quote

  • Two coordination flavours: Choreography (peer events) and Orchestration (central state machine).
  • Three step classes: Compensable, Pivot, Retryable.
  • Three isolation anomalies: lost updates, dirty reads, fuzzy reads.
  • Six countermeasures: semantic lock, commutative updates, pessimistic view, reread, version file, by-value.
  • Guarantees provided: ACD (no I). Consistency is eventual.
