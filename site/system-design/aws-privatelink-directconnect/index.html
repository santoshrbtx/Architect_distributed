AWS Networking — PrivateLink, Direct Connect & VPC Endpoints

How to keep traffic off the public internet — the four private paths every AWS architect must know, when to pick each, and how they work under the hood.

🎯 Real-world scenario

What we're solving & where it's used

Any regulated or high-scale AWS customer — a bank, a hospital, an insurer, an e-commerce giant, or a SaaS vendor — hits the same wall the moment they leave a toy demo: they don't want their data going over the public internet. When your EC2 instance in a private subnet has to talk to S3, DynamoDB, or a partner's API, the naïve path is NAT → Internet Gateway → the internet → back into AWS. That's slow, costly (NAT + data-transfer fees), audit-unfriendly (traffic leaves your VPC), and forces you to punch outbound holes in firewalls. It also makes bandwidth unpredictable — you share the public internet with everyone else.

AWS solves this with four private-networking primitives you'll be expected to name and justify: Gateway VPC Endpoints (free, S3/DynamoDB only), Interface VPC Endpoints (PrivateLink) (ENI-based, for every other AWS service and third-party SaaS), Direct Connect (dedicated fiber from your data centre into AWS), and Site-to-Site VPN (encrypted tunnel over the internet, as fallback or first step). Netflix, Snowflake, Datadog, and Databricks all sell their services through PrivateLink endpoints, so customers consume them without ever leaving their own VPC. Capital One, ING, and Goldman rely on Direct Connect for the deterministic 10/100 Gbps circuits that make hybrid cloud actually work.

The interview question is almost always: "Design the network for a company that wants zero public-internet exposure between their VPC and AWS services / on-prem data centre / a partner SaaS." You win by picking the right tool for each traffic flow, explaining why, and knowing the trade-offs.

🧑‍🎓 In laymen terms

Explained like you've never touched a cloud console

Imagine your company runs offices in a big secure building — that's your data centre or private cloud area. Amazon runs another giant building full of shared services (photo storage, address books, delivery trucks) — that's AWS. Most of the time your office needs to call Amazon's building to do things: "please store this file", "send this email", "look up that customer".

The obvious way to call is through the public street — the internet. It works, but it's exposed: anyone can see the traffic go by (even if it's inside a sealed envelope), it costs money to hire messengers to walk through the street, and if the street is jammed everyone is stuck. For a bank or hospital that's not good enough.

So Amazon offers you four ways to talk without using the public street:

1. A magic mail slot (Gateway Endpoint) — for two very common services (file storage and a big address book), Amazon punches a private mail slot right inside your office wall. You drop the letter in the slot; it teleports directly to Amazon's mailroom. It's free, but it only works for those two services.

2. A private phone line to any Amazon service (PrivateLink) — Amazon runs a private phone cable into your office, wired to any of the hundreds of services they run (email, translation, payments…). You dial a normal-looking number and the call never enters the street. Vendors like Salesforce and Snowflake use the same wiring, so you can call them privately too.

3. A dedicated fibre highway between your building and Amazon's (Direct Connect) — for companies moving huge amounts of data (a hospital's imaging system, an airline's reservation system), Amazon builds you a literal glass fibre cable that goes from your building into their nearest data centre. Massive bandwidth, predictable, private, but you have to pay to build the road.

4. A secret encrypted tunnel through the street (Site-to-Site VPN) — cheapest fallback: your traffic still goes over the public street but it's in a locked armoured van (encrypted), so nobody can peek. Slower and less reliable than the fibre highway, but you can set it up in an afternoon.

Picture Netflix, Stripe, or Datadog: they built their whole service inside AWS, and they let their customers plug into it via method 2 (PrivateLink), so no customer's data ever touches the public internet on the way in. Big banks like JPMorgan use method 3 (Direct Connect) to move terabytes of trading data every day. That's why these primitives are the backbone of "the cloud" for anyone who actually cares about security.

📋 Requirements & scale

What each mechanism gives you

Functional requirements we're solving: reach AWS services privately, reach on-premises data centres privately, reach partner SaaS privately, control which callers can reach which services, and give apps a normal-looking DNS name so no code changes are required. Non-functional: encryption at rest and in transit, predictable throughput and latency, high availability across AZs, and audit trails.

Back-of-envelope numbers to volunteer: A single Direct Connect 10 Gbps port at ~70% utilization pushes ~5.5 TB/hour. An interface endpoint's ENI can push 10 Gbps and there is one ENI per AZ, so a 3-AZ deployment is a 30 Gbps aggregate. Gateway endpoints have no bandwidth limit imposed by the endpoint itself — you're bounded by the S3/DynamoDB service and your instance NIC. VPN tunnels max out around 1.25 Gbps each, and you can bond up to 4 with ECMP via Transit Gateway.

🏗️ High-level architecture

How the pieces fit together

The mental model is one VPC in the middle, with four different doors leading out, each labeled with what it's optimized for. The default door (Internet Gateway + NAT) sends traffic to the public internet — you want to avoid that for anything sensitive. Everything else pulls specific traffic off the public path onto AWS's private backbone or your own dedicated circuit.

Gateway Endpoint (free, S3/DDB) Interface Endpoint (PrivateLink) Direct Connect Site-to-Site VPN Public internet (avoid)

The route table is the traffic controller. When your instance sends a packet, the VPC picks the most-specific route: an S3 request matches the AWS-managed prefix list for S3 → routed to the Gateway Endpoint; a call to kms.us-east-1.amazonaws.com matches Private DNS → resolved to an Interface Endpoint's ENI in the same AZ; on-prem CIDR (172.16.0.0/12) matches the DX gateway → routed onto your fibre. Only unmatched traffic (e.g. arbitrary 0.0.0.0/0) falls back to the Internet Gateway / NAT.

🔍 Component deep-dive

1. Gateway VPC Endpoint (S3 & DynamoDB only)

Gateway endpoints are the oldest and simplest primitive — a route-table entry, not an ENI. When you attach a gateway endpoint to your route table, AWS injects a special route: the destination is an AWS-managed prefix list (pl-63a5400a for S3 in us-east-1, for example — a live-updating list of the service's IP ranges), and the target is the endpoint ID. Any packet whose destination matches the prefix list wins the longest-prefix-match contest against your 0.0.0.0/0 → igw route and gets forwarded onto the AWS backbone instead of the internet. There's no ENI, no DNS trick, no additional charge — that's why it's the very first thing you add to any production VPC. Limitations: works only for S3 and DynamoDB, only within the same Region (cross-Region S3 still goes via the internet), and it does not work from on-prem or a peered VPC — so DX users must use the S3 interface endpoint instead.

2. Interface VPC Endpoint (PrivateLink)

An interface endpoint is a much richer construct: AWS creates one ENI (Elastic Network Interface) in each AZ you select, wires it into a global service called AWS Hyperplane (a stateless, distributed L4 shuffle service), and Hyperplane forwards the traffic to a Network Load Balancer sitting in front of the target service (KMS, SQS, SSM, ECR, an SaaS vendor's VPC, or your own VPC's NLB if you're publishing a service). You typically enable Private DNS, which lets AWS's DNS resolver override the public hostname (kms.us-east-1.amazonaws.com) to return the ENI's private IP — so no code change is required. Access control is layered: a security group on the ENI restricts which sources can reach it, and an endpoint policy (a JSON IAM-style document) restricts which API actions and resources can flow through. Cost is roughly $0.01/hour per ENI per AZ plus about $0.01/GB processed.

3. AWS PrivateLink for third-party SaaS

The same interface-endpoint machinery is what lets vendors like Snowflake, Datadog, Databricks, MongoDB Atlas, Salesforce, Confluent sell "PrivateLink" as a security feature. The vendor puts a Network Load Balancer in front of their service inside their own VPC, then creates a VPC Endpoint Service that exposes it. Their customers create an interface endpoint pointing to that service's ID and get a private ENI in their own VPC — traffic never traverses the internet in either direction. There's no VPC peering, no CIDR conflict, and the vendor's IPs stay private to them. This is the most under-appreciated networking primitive in AWS.

4. AWS Direct Connect

Direct Connect is the physical primitive: you (or a Direct Connect Partner) run a single-mode fibre cross-connect between your router in a colocation facility and an AWS router in the same facility. AWS calls these facilities Direct Connect locations — Equinix DC/LHR/AMS, CoreSite, Digital Realty, and dozens of others worldwide. Speeds are 1 Gbps, 10 Gbps, 100 Gbps, or 400 Gbps, and multiple ports can be bonded into a Link Aggregation Group (LAG) for capacity and redundancy. Optionally you turn on MACsec for line-rate L2 encryption on 10/100 Gbps ports.

On top of the physical circuit you carve Virtual Interfaces (VIFs), each on a different 802.1Q VLAN tag: a Private VIF peers BGP with your VPC's Virtual Private Gateway (or a Direct Connect Gateway) so you can reach private IPs across any Region; a Public VIF gives you a BGP session that advertises AWS's public prefixes, so you can hit S3 or DynamoDB across the fibre without going through the internet; a Transit VIF connects to a Transit Gateway for hub-and-spoke topologies across many VPCs. All VIFs use BGP with MD5 auth; failover is via BGP path attributes (AS-path prepending, local-pref, MED) and BFD for sub-second dead-peer detection.

5. Site-to-Site VPN & Transit Gateway

Site-to-Site VPN is the pragmatic fallback: an IPsec tunnel over the public internet between your customer gateway (an on-prem router) and an AWS Virtual Private Gateway. Each tunnel maxes out around 1.25 Gbps; you get two tunnels per VPN connection for redundancy. Modern deployments hang everything off a Transit Gateway, which is essentially a regional cloud-scale router that terminates VPN, DX, and dozens of VPC attachments and lets them talk to each other via a routing domain of your design. TGW plus ECMP over multiple VPN connections can push tens of Gbps, and is the standard "day-one hybrid" while a Direct Connect port is being provisioned (typically 4–8 weeks of physical work).

🧮 The DSA behind it

Routing algorithms, prefix trees & BGP

Under the polished console, the whole system is a routing algorithm — a decision tree that picks the outgoing interface for every packet. The core primitives:

ConceptData structure / algorithmWhy
Route table lookupLongest-Prefix Match (LPM) over a trie of CIDR blocks (Patricia / radix tree)Constant number of steps per address bit → O(32) or O(128); most specific route wins.
AWS-managed prefix listLive-updated list of /24-ish CIDRs for a service (S3, DynamoDB)Lets AWS grow the service's address space without you editing routes.
BGP route selection (Direct Connect)Best-path algorithm: highest local-pref → shortest AS-path → lowest MED → oldest → tie-breakersDeterministic, decentralized route choice across ISPs and AWS AS 7224.
Interface endpoint DNSHash-based load balancing across ENIs; DNS returns the ENI in the caller's AZKeeps traffic AZ-local for lowest latency and no cross-AZ charge.
AWS HyperplaneStateless N-to-M shuffle-sharded L4 forwarder — no per-flow stateMassive horizontal scale and instant failover across AZs.
BFD (Bidirectional Forwarding Detection)Small hello-timer heartbeat protocol running under BGPDetects a dead peer in ~300 ms instead of waiting for BGP's 90-s hold-timer.

The one algorithm to name in the interview: longest-prefix match. When you say "the route table always picks the most specific route," you're describing LPM over a radix tree, and it's what makes gateway endpoints work — the S3 prefix list is more specific than your 0.0.0.0/0 internet route, so it wins.

💻 C# reference — using an Interface Endpoint from .NET

When Private DNS is enabled on the interface endpoint (the default), no code change is needed — the AWS SDK resolves kms.us-east-1.amazonaws.com to the endpoint's private ENI automatically. That's the whole point. But if you want to be explicit (or you have Private DNS disabled for some reason), you can override the endpoint at client-construction time:

// Program.cs — reach AWS KMS through a Private-DNS-disabled interface endpoint
using Amazon;
using Amazon.KeyManagementService;
using Amazon.KeyManagementService.Model;
using Amazon.Runtime;

var endpointDns = "vpce-0abc123-9y8x7w6v.kms.us-east-1.vpce.amazonaws.com";

var config = new AmazonKeyManagementServiceConfig
{
    // Point the SDK at the interface-endpoint DNS name.
    ServiceURL = $"https://{endpointDns}",
    // The SDK still needs to sign for the real region.
    AuthenticationRegion = RegionEndpoint.USEast1.SystemName
};

var kms = new AmazonKeyManagementServiceClient(config);

async Task<byte[]> DecryptAsync(byte[] ciphertext)
{
    var resp = await kms.DecryptAsync(new DecryptRequest
    {
        CiphertextBlob = new MemoryStream(ciphertext)
    });
    // Traffic went ENI → Hyperplane → KMS NLB. Never left AWS.
    return resp.Plaintext.ToArray();
}

And a Terraform/CloudFormation-style snippet for the endpoint itself — the piece that actually creates the private path:

# Terraform — interface endpoint for KMS with policy + SG lockdown
resource "aws_vpc_endpoint" "kms" {
  vpc_id              = aws_vpc.main.id
  service_name        = "com.amazonaws.us-east-1.kms"
  vpc_endpoint_type   = "Interface"
  subnet_ids          = aws_subnet.private[*].id       # one ENI per AZ
  security_group_ids  = [aws_security_group.endpoints.id]
  private_dns_enabled = true                          # magic — clients need no code change
  policy              = jsonencode({
    Version   = "2012-10-17"
    Statement = [{
      Effect    = "Allow"
      Principal = "*"
      Action    = ["kms:Decrypt", "kms:Encrypt", "kms:GenerateDataKey*"]
      Resource  = "*"
      Condition = { "StringEquals" = { "aws:PrincipalOrgID" = "o-abc123" } }
    }]
  })
}

The endpoint policy is the security control most people forget — it means even a compromised IAM key inside your VPC can only call the APIs you've allowlisted through this endpoint.

⚖️ Trade-offs, bottlenecks & likely follow-ups

Gateway vs Interface Endpoint (S3)

Gateway is free but Region-local and doesn't work from on-prem. Interface (PrivateLink) costs money but resolves via private DNS from anywhere (VPN/DX). A common pattern: both on the same VPC — gateway for in-Region EC2 traffic, interface for on-prem callers.

PrivateLink vs VPC Peering

Peering exposes entire CIDR ranges bidirectionally and forbids overlapping CIDRs. PrivateLink exposes exactly one service, one direction, and CIDRs can overlap. Peering is right for "two VPCs I own that need broad connectivity"; PrivateLink is right for "I sell this API and I want isolation."

Direct Connect vs Site-to-Site VPN

DX is faster, more consistent, and can be cheaper at scale — but takes weeks to install and needs a colocation presence. VPN is provisioned in an hour and uses the public internet with IPsec. Real designs use VPN as backup on top of DX for the "fiber cut" scenario.

MACsec vs VPN-over-DX

MACsec encrypts at L2 at line rate but only some hardware supports it and only on ≥10 Gbps ports. Running VPN over the private VIF adds ~5% CPU overhead but works everywhere. Regulated industries often mandate one of the two.

Bottlenecks and gotchas the interviewer will probe

  • Interface-endpoint sprawl. Every service, every VPC, every AZ = an ENI at $0.01/hour. A 300-VPC estate can spend $50k/month on endpoint ENIs alone. Fix: centralize endpoints in a "shared services" VPC and share via Transit Gateway or Route 53 private hosted zones.
  • Cross-AZ traffic to endpoints. If your caller in AZ-A hits the ENI in AZ-B you pay cross-AZ transfer and add latency. Fix: enable "AZ affinity" on the SDK and put ENIs in every AZ you deploy to.
  • DNS resolution from on-prem. Interface endpoints only get their nice private DNS name inside the VPC. From on-prem, you must either use the endpoint's ugly regional DNS name or use a Route 53 Resolver inbound endpoint. Miss this and you'll wonder why the endpoint "doesn't work."
  • Endpoint policies vs IAM policies. Both apply — the request must be allowed by both. Interviewers love asking "who wins if they conflict?" (answer: the intersection is what's allowed).
  • Direct Connect single-fibre risk. One DX port = one PoP = one construction crew away from an outage. Best practice: two DX connections at two different DX locations (max resiliency), or DX + VPN as a warm standby.
  • Gateway endpoint doesn't traverse peering or DX. Traffic from a peered VPC to your gateway endpoint is silently dropped. This is a top-10 AWS support ticket.
  • DX data-transfer pricing. Egress via DX is cheaper than egress via the internet, but you still pay per-GB. If most of your traffic is into AWS, DX is essentially free.
  • Latency floor. DX doesn't beat physics — a Mumbai → Frankfurt DX still incurs the ~110 ms speed-of-light round trip. It just makes that RTT predictable.

🗣️ Interview cheat sheet

30-second pitch: AWS gives you four private paths so your traffic never touches the public internet. Gateway endpoints are free route-table entries for S3 and DynamoDB. Interface endpoints (PrivateLink) put an ENI in your VPC connected via AWS Hyperplane to any other service — AWS-owned or a partner SaaS. Direct Connect is a physical fibre from your data centre to an AWS router, with BGP-managed private/public/transit VIFs. Site-to-Site VPN is the encrypted IPsec fallback over the public internet. The routing decision on every packet is longest-prefix match against the VPC route table.
  • Volunteer the numbers: 10 Gbps per endpoint ENI per AZ, DX ports at 1/10/100/400 Gbps, ~1.25 Gbps per VPN tunnel, gateway endpoints are free.
  • Say the phrase "longest-prefix match against the route table" — it demonstrates you understand the routing layer.
  • Name AWS Hyperplane as what actually powers PrivateLink. Very few candidates do.
  • Explain the S3 gotcha: gateway endpoint for in-Region VPC traffic + interface endpoint for on-prem callers over DX.
  • Always cite endpoint policy as the least-privilege control most people forget.
  • Design for HA: two DX circuits in two DX locations, endpoints in every AZ, and VPN as a standby on top of DX.
