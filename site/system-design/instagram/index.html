🎯 Real-world scenario

What we're building & where it's used

Instagram lets a user snap a photo or record a short video, add a caption, and publish it to their followers, who then see it in a scrollable, personalized home feed. Underneath a deceptively simple UI sit three hard problems that show up in almost every large consumer app: storing and delivering huge amounts of immutable media cheaply and fast, modeling a massive social graph (who follows whom), and assembling a per-user feed from the posts of everyone you follow — quickly, for hundreds of millions of people at once.

The same architectural spine powers Facebook, Twitter/X, TikTok, Pinterest, Snapchat, and LinkedIn. That is exactly why interviewers love it: it forces you to reason about a read-heavy workload (people scroll far more than they post), the classic fan-out-on-write vs. fan-out-on-read trade-off, object storage plus a CDN for blobs, and how you shard a relational database when a single box can no longer hold the data. The real Instagram famously scaled to 14M+ users with a tiny team on Django, PostgreSQL, Redis and Amazon S3 before Facebook acquired it — a reminder that the fundamentals, done well, take you a very long way.

🧑‍🎓 In laymen terms

Your own private magazine, freshly printed every second

Think about what Instagram actually is: two billion people take pictures, and two billion people want to see pictures — but only from the handful of friends and celebrities they care about. Nobody wants to see everyone's photos. That's the whole trick.

Imagine a giant printing press in the sky. Every time your friend Priya posts a beach photo, the press immediately prints a copy into your personal magazine — and into the personal magazines of everyone else who follows her. Same for your cousin's dog, your college batch group, the food blogger you follow. When you open the app, you're not searching through a huge pile of "everyone's photos." You're just flipping through your own custom magazine that was already assembled for you. That's why it feels instant.

But there's a catch: some people, like Cristiano Ronaldo, have 640 million followers. If the printing press tried to stamp his photo into 640 million magazines the moment he posts, it would jam. So Instagram plays a clever mixed game — for ordinary people (a few hundred followers), it stamps the photo into everyone's magazine instantly. But for superstars, it does the opposite: their photos sit in one central spot, and when a fan opens their app, Instagram quickly runs over and mixes in the latest superstar posts on the way.

The photos themselves? They're not on Instagram's main computers. They live in a giant, cheap warehouse called S3 — basically Amazon's endless attic. And to make them appear in your feed in a blink no matter where you are on the planet, Instagram keeps small copies of the popular photos in warehouses close to you — one in Mumbai, one in Frankfurt, one in São Paulo. Your phone grabs the picture from the nearest warehouse, not the original one.

The really hard part is that all of this — write once, be seen a million times, feel instant — has to hold together while people upload a hundred million photos every single day.

📋 Requirements & scale

Requirements and back-of-the-envelope math

Functional requirements

  • Upload media — post a photo or video with a caption.
  • Follow / unfollow other users (a directed social graph).
  • Home feed — see a reverse-chronological (or ranked) feed of posts from people you follow, with low latency.
  • View a user's profile — their posts in reverse-chronological order.
  • (Extensions) likes, comments, search, stories, notifications — mention them, then scope them out to stay focused.

Non-functional requirements

  • Highly available — feed should never be "down"; availability > strict consistency.
  • Low latency — feed loads in < 200–500 ms; media renders instantly via CDN.
  • Durable — an uploaded photo is never lost (11 nines of durability on object storage).
  • Eventually consistent — it's fine if your new post takes a few seconds to appear in every follower's feed.
  • Read-heavy — design for a ~100:1 read:write ratio.

🏗️ High-level architecture

How the pieces fit together

Clients talk to an API gateway / load balancer that routes to stateless microservices. The write path and the read path are deliberately separated. On upload, the client gets a pre-signed URL and pushes the raw bytes directly to object storage (bypassing our servers), then the Post service writes a small metadata row and drops an event on a queue. A fan-out worker consumes that event and pushes the new post ID into the Redis feed-cache list of each follower ("fan-out on write"). On read, the Feed service simply reads the pre-computed list of post IDs from Redis, hydrates them with metadata, and returns CDN URLs for the media — so the scroll is a couple of fast cache lookups, not a giant join.

write / async fan-out read / feed path media (blob + CDN) data stores

The two paths, step by step

  1. Upload: client asks Post service for a pre-signed URL, uploads raw bytes straight to S3, then confirms — Post service writes a metadata row (author, caption, media key, timestamp, a globally-unique post ID) and emits a "new post" event.
  2. Process: a worker generates thumbnails / transcodes video into multiple resolutions, storing them back in S3 behind the CDN.
  3. Fan-out: the fan-out worker looks up the author's followers in the graph store and prepends the post ID to each follower's Redis feed list (capped, e.g. latest 500–1000).
  4. Read feed: Feed service reads the pre-computed post-ID list from Redis, batch-hydrates metadata from cache/DB, and returns items with CDN media URLs — the client streams the bytes from the nearest edge.

🔍 Component deep-dive

Media storage — never put blobs in your database

Photos and videos are large, immutable, and served by the millions per second — a perfect fit for object storage (S3), not a relational DB. The DB stores only a small row with the S3 object key. Uploads go directly from client to S3 via a pre-signed URL so our app servers never touch the bytes, and downloads go through a CDN that caches media at edge locations near the user. This is what makes media "render instantly" — the bytes travel the shortest possible network path, and origin (S3) load stays low because of high cache-hit ratios.

Feed generation — fan-out on write vs. fan-out on read

This is the heart of the interview. Fan-out on write (push): when you post, we immediately write the post ID into every follower's precomputed feed list in Redis. Reads are then O(1) — dirt cheap and fast — at the cost of expensive writes and storage duplication. Fan-out on read (pull): we store nothing precomputed; at read time we gather recent posts from everyone you follow and merge them. Writes are cheap, but reads are expensive (a merge across hundreds of authors on every scroll).

The production answer is hybrid. Use push for the common case (most users have a modest follower count), but for celebrities with tens of millions of followers, fanning out a single post to 50M lists is catastrophic (the "hot key" / thundering-herd problem). So for those high-fan-out accounts we switch to pull: their posts are not pushed; instead, at read time the Feed service merges the viewer's precomputed list with a fresh pull of the few celebrities they follow. Best of both worlds.

The social graph & sharding

Follows are a directed graph stored as adjacency lists (followers(userId) → [followerIds] and following(userId) → [...]). Everything is sharded by user ID so one user's data lives on one shard. That means a globally unique, roughly time-sortable post ID matters a lot: Instagram's real solution was a 64-bit ID minted in Postgres that packs a millisecond timestamp, the shard ID, and a per-shard sequence number — so IDs sort by time and encode where the row lives, with no central coordinator.

Caching & hot keys

Multiple cache layers: the CDN for media, Redis for feed lists, and Memcached/Redis for hot metadata (post rows, user profiles, follower counts). Hot keys (a viral post, a celebrity profile) are mitigated with request coalescing, short TTLs, and replicas. The DB sits behind all of this and mostly serves cache misses and writes.

🧮 The DSA behind it

Data structures & algorithms

The design looks like plumbing, but each choice is a deliberate data-structure decision:

  • Redis list / sorted set per user — the precomputed feed. A capped list gives O(1) prepend and O(1) range-read of the latest N. A sorted set keyed by timestamp (or a ranking score) lets you keep it ordered and trim the tail cheaply.
  • k-way merge with a min/max-heap — fan-out-on-read and the hybrid merge combine posts from many authors in timestamp order. Push the newest post from each of the k sources into a heap and pop the max repeatedly: O(N log k) to produce N feed items.
  • 64-bit time-sortable IDs (Snowflake-style) — [41 bits ms timestamp | 13 bits shard | 10 bits sequence]. Distributed, collision-free, and monotonic-by-time so a feed can be ordered by ID alone.
  • Adjacency lists for the follow graph — the natural structure for "get all followers of X" in O(degree).
  • Consistent hashing to map user ID → shard/cache node, so adding capacity moves only a small slice of keys.
  • Bloom filter — a compact "have you already seen this post?" check to de-duplicate the feed without a DB round-trip.

💻 C# implementation

Two pieces of core logic an interviewer will ask you to sketch: a Snowflake-style ID generator (globally-unique, time-sortable post IDs) and the fan-out + feed-read logic. This is correct, idiomatic C#.

// ---- 1. Snowflake-style 64-bit ID: [41b timestamp | 13b shard | 10b seq] ----
public sealed class SnowflakeIdGenerator
{
    private const long Epoch = 1288834974657L;  // custom epoch (ms)
    private const int ShardBits = 13, SeqBits = 10;
    private const long MaxSeq = (1L << SeqBits) - 1;   // 1023 IDs / ms / shard

    private readonly long _shardId;
    private readonly object _lock = new();
    private long _lastMs = -1, _seq = 0;

    public SnowflakeIdGenerator(long shardId) => _shardId = shardId;

    public long NextId()
    {
        lock (_lock)
        {
            var ms = DateTimeOffset.UtcNow.ToUnixTimeMilliseconds();
            if (ms == _lastMs)
            {
                _seq = (_seq + 1) & MaxSeq;
                if (_seq == 0) while ((ms = DateTimeOffset.UtcNow.ToUnixTimeMilliseconds()) <= _lastMs) { } // spin to next ms
            }
            else _seq = 0;
            _lastMs = ms;
            return ((ms - Epoch) << (ShardBits + SeqBits))
                 | (_shardId << SeqBits)
                 | _seq;
        }
    }
}

// ---- 2. Hybrid fan-out: push for normal users, skip celebrities (pulled at read) ----
public class FeedService
{
    private readonly IRedis _redis;          // per-user capped list of post IDs
    private readonly IGraph _graph;          // followers / following
    private readonly IPostStore _posts;       // metadata by id
    private const int CelebThreshold = 100_000, FeedCap = 800;

    // Called by the async fan-out worker when a post is created.
    public async Task FanOutAsync(long authorId, long postId)
    {
        var followerCount = await _graph.FollowerCountAsync(authorId);
        if (followerCount > CelebThreshold) return; // celebrity → pulled at read time

        await foreach (var follower in _graph.GetFollowersAsync(authorId))
        {
            await _redis.LPushCappedAsync($"feed:{follower}", postId, FeedCap);
        }
    }

    // Read path: merge precomputed feed with fresh posts from followed celebrities.
    public async Task<List<Post>> GetFeedAsync(long userId, int limit = 30)
    {
        var pushedIds = await _redis.LRangeAsync($"feed:{userId}", 0, limit * 2);
        var celebs    = await _graph.GetFollowedCelebritiesAsync(userId);
        var pulledIds = await _posts.RecentPostIdsAsync(celebs, limit);

        // IDs are time-sortable → merge & take newest. (heap for true k-way merge)
        var merged = pushedIds.Concat(pulledIds)
                             .Distinct()
                             .OrderByDescending(id => id)
                             .Take(limit);

        return (await _posts.HydrateAsync(merged)).ToList(); // attach CDN URLs
    }
}

In other stacks you'd reach for the same shapes: Redis LPUSH/LTRIM for the capped list, a real min-heap for the k-way merge (e.g. PriorityQueue<T> in .NET), and Twitter's open-source Snowflake or a DB sequence for IDs.

⚖️ Trade-offs, bottlenecks & likely follow-ups

The celebrity fan-out problem. Pure push dies on accounts with tens of millions of followers — one post triggers tens of millions of writes and a latency spike. The hybrid (push for the masses, pull for celebrities) is the standard answer; be ready to name the follower threshold as a tunable.

Consistency vs. availability. We choose availability and eventual consistency (a post appearing a few seconds late is fine), but a user must always see their own post immediately — so on read we can splice the user's own recent posts in even before fan-out finishes ("read-your-writes" for the author).

Storage & bandwidth are the dominant cost. Video multiplies everything; you transcode into multiple bitrates and lean hard on CDN cache-hit ratio and tiered/cold storage (S3 Glacier) for old media.

Hot keys & thundering herds on viral content — solved with request coalescing, multiple cache replicas, and short TTLs.

"How do you keep the feed fast at 500M users?"

Precompute it. Reads become O(1) Redis lookups; the expensive work happens asynchronously off the write path.

"What breaks first as you scale, and how do you shard?"

The single DB. Shard by user ID with consistent hashing, and use time-sortable Snowflake IDs that encode the shard so you never need a global lookup.

"How do you order the feed — chronological or ranked?"

Start chronological (sort by ID). To rank, store a score in a Redis sorted set and compute it from engagement signals; the structure is the same, only the sort key changes.

"How does upload not overload your servers?"

Pre-signed URLs — the client uploads bytes straight to S3; our servers only handle the small metadata write and enqueue processing.
