🎯 Real-world scenario

The problem: your app grew, your database didn't

Every internet-scale product you've ever used — Amazon, Instagram, Uber, LinkedIn, Netflix, Shopify — began life on a single Postgres or MySQL box. That single box is one of the fastest, most productive pieces of infrastructure ever built: transactional, consistent, expressive, well-understood. And that same single box is the number-one cause of "why is the site down" during any traffic spike. The database is always the bottleneck, and the whole point of "system design" is knowing which lever to pull, in which order, when your user count grows from 10k → 100k → 1M → 100M.

This page is the interview-grade playbook: the eleven architectural moves that engineering teams reach for, in the order they reach for them, with real numbers and specific trade-offs. It's the answer to the question interviewers love: "your database is the bottleneck — walk me through everything you would try". A junior says "add a cache". A mid-level engineer says "read replica and sharding". An architect walks all eleven rungs, names the failure mode of each, and states clearly which one they would try next given the specifics of the workload.

🧑‍🎓 In plain English

The story, told without any jargon

Imagine you run a small library. One librarian sits behind the desk, answering questions and stamping books. Five people a day walk in — it's fine. One librarian handles it easily.

Business booms. Fifty people a day. The librarian's line gets long. What do you do? First move: hire an intern — someone who only answers "where is book X?" and doesn't need to touch the stamp. That's a cache — most people are asking the same thing over and over, and the intern remembers the popular answers. It takes 80% of the traffic off the head librarian.

Still growing. 500 visitors. Now you notice: most people just read — they don't check anything out. So you photocopy the popular books and put them on a self-service shelf outside. That's a read replica — a second copy of the library that only handles reads. Writes still go through the head librarian.

5,000 visitors. The library building itself is packed. You split into five smaller libraries in different neighbourhoods, and each one holds only certain letters of the alphabet: A–E in one, F–J in another, and so on. Now a visitor asking about "Zoology" goes straight to the Z branch — no bumping into the Astronomy crowd. That's sharding.

500,000 visitors. Your quiet requests ("give me a report of everything checked out this year") slow down the busy librarian. Solution: make a nightly summary book that answers those questions from a snapshot. That's a materialized view or a separate reporting/analytics warehouse.

Eventually, the librarian is doing so much stamping that even the writes are the bottleneck. So you switch to a totally different model: everyone shouts out their loans and returns into a "log book", and a team of clerks catches up in the background. That's event sourcing + CQRS. The library is now a stream of events, and any question about state is answered by playing the log forward.

That's the whole scaling ladder in five paragraphs. Every real-world database scale-up follows this exact shape: cache the hot stuff, split reads from writes, split the data itself, split reads from writes logically (CQRS), and finally treat the truth as a stream. You already understand it — the rest of this page just gives you the vocabulary and the numbers.

The golden rule: never jump rungs. Sharding a system that never had caching is almost always premature; CQRS on a codebase that hasn't tried materialized views is over-engineering. Pull the cheapest lever that solves the actual bottleneck first, measure, and only then move up.

🪜 The 11-rung scaling ladder

Read top-to-bottom in the order you'd apply them. Each rung buys a specific capability and breaks in a specific way that motivates the next rung.

1Vertical scaling (bigger box)

Move from db.t3.medium to db.r6i.24xlarge. More CPU, more RAM, more IOPS. Zero code change. Storage resizes without downtime; instance resize takes seconds on Multi-AZ (standby upgraded, then failover). This is always the first move — it's cheap and works.

Buys: 5–20× headroom, hours of work.

Breaks when: single-box limits hit (~64 vCPU, ~500k IOPS). Cost curves quadratic.

2Indexing & query tuning

Add B-tree/hash indexes on WHERE and JOIN columns; drop unused indexes (they slow writes); rewrite N+1 queries into batch IN (…) or joins; add EXPLAIN ANALYZE to CI. This move regularly delivers 100× speed-ups on production hot paths.

Buys: 10–1000× on the tuned queries.

Breaks when: every query is already an index seek, and there's still too much load.

3Connection pooling / DB proxy

Postgres opens a full process per connection (~10 MB each); MySQL a thread. 10k Lambda invocations = 10k connections = OOM. Put PgBouncer, ProxySQL, or AWS RDS Proxy in front — they multiplex "hundreds of thousands of connections" over a small pool of real DB connections. Zero code change.

Buys: 10–100× more concurrent clients without touching schema.

Breaks when: even the pooled queries are too slow. Session state (temp tables, prepared statements) can be tricky.

4Caching (Redis / Memcached / DAX)

Put an in-memory cache in front of the DB. Patterns: cache-aside (app reads cache, falls back to DB, writes cache on miss), write-through (writes go through cache), write-behind (writes queued to DB async), read-through (cache library owns the miss path). 90%+ hit ratios are typical for read-heavy workloads.

Buys: 10× read throughput at 10× lower latency for hot keys.

Breaks when: cache invalidation goes wrong. "There are only two hard things in computer science…"

5Read replicas (leader–follower)

Async replication from primary → N followers. App routes reads to followers, writes to primary. Followers can be 100 ms–seconds behind (replication lag). Great for reports/dashboards; dangerous for read-your-writes without care.

Buys: near-linear read scale (add follower = add read capacity).

Breaks when: writes saturate the primary; lag violates freshness SLAs.

6Partitioning (single-DB, split-table)

Split a huge table into partitions by range (e.g. by month), list, or hash. The query planner prunes irrelevant partitions. Still one DB, still one primary. Cheap to operate; huge speed-up on time-bounded queries.

Buys: 10–100× on partition-pruned queries; faster maintenance (drop old partitions).

Breaks when: even the largest partition is too hot; every partition writes hit the same box.

7Sharding (multi-DB, split-data)

Split the entire dataset across N independent databases keyed by a shard key (user_id, tenant_id, geo). Every write goes to exactly one shard. Cross-shard joins are painful — pick a key that keeps 95%+ of queries single-shard. Consistent hashing keeps re-shards manageable.

Buys: linear write scale; no single-box ceiling.

Breaks when: shard key was picked wrong; hot shard; cross-shard transactions.

8Materialized views & OLAP replica

Precompute expensive aggregates into a materialized view (Postgres native, or a summary table refreshed by cron). For truly analytical workloads, replicate to a column-store OLAP system (Redshift, Snowflake, BigQuery) so heavy reports never touch OLTP.

Buys: reports run in ms instead of minutes; OLTP path is protected.

Breaks when: freshness of the materialized view is not enough; refresh cost exceeds savings.

9CQRS — split read model from write model

Command Query Responsibility Segregation: the write side and the read side are separate models, often separate storage engines. Writes go to a normalized OLTP store; reads served from denormalized projections (Redis, Elasticsearch, DynamoDB, a read-optimized SQL view) built from a change-stream. Massively different scale/latency profiles handled independently.

Buys: reads and writes scale independently; read side can be denormalized freely.

Breaks when: eventual-consistency window is too big for users; two models drift.

10Event sourcing

Instead of storing current state, store the append-only log of every event. Current state is a projection. This scales writes trivially (append-only), gives free audit history, enables time-travel, and is the natural pair to CQRS. Kafka / Kinesis as the log, projectors build materialized reads.

Buys: write-only OLTP; audit for free; multiple read models from one log.

Breaks when: schema evolution of events is hard; eventual consistency on projections; complexity.

11Polyglot persistence & NoSQL migration

The final rung: the wrong storage engine. Key-value workloads should be on DynamoDB or Redis, not Postgres. Timeseries on Timescale, ClickHouse, or InfluxDB. Full-text on Elasticsearch/OpenSearch. Graphs on Neptune. Documents on MongoDB. Real systems use 3–5 stores together, each fit-for-purpose. This unlocks horizontal scale that a general-purpose RDBMS never will.

Buys: right tool for the job — often 10–100× on the mismatched workloads.

Breaks when: operational surface area explodes; cross-store consistency is hard.

🔍 Deep-dive — each rung, in detail

Vertical scaling — cheap but bounded

Bigger CPUs, more RAM, faster disk. On AWS RDS, storage and instance type are decoupled — storage resizes without downtime, and Multi-AZ instances scale by upgrading the standby first, then failing over (~30–60 s blip). Real ceilings: about 64 vCPU and 400–500 k IOPS on a single node. Watch out for BYOL licensing on Oracle / SQL Server when you resize. Vertical scaling is the right answer surprisingly often — modern instance families are so beefy that most apps never need to leave rung 1.

Indexing & query tuning — the highest-leverage rung

The most common production win is not "add a cache" — it's find the missing index. Every DBA cliché: check pg_stat_statements or slow_query_log, sort by total time, run EXPLAIN ANALYZE, add the covering index, watch P99 drop 100×. Also: kill N+1 patterns (one query per row instead of one query with IN), avoid SELECT * on wide tables, use LIMIT and cursor-based pagination instead of OFFSET N. Every index costs write throughput — drop unused ones.

Connection pooling — invisible but critical

Postgres forks a process per connection; each process holds ~10 MB. A microservice fleet or a Lambda function can easily open thousands of connections and OOM the DB before any query runs. Solution: a pooler. PgBouncer is the community classic; RDS Proxy is the AWS-managed answer that supports "hundreds of thousands of connections". Modes: session (client owns a connection until disconnect), transaction (connection reassigned per transaction — best throughput but no session state), statement (aggressive; watch prepared statements).

Caching — patterns that actually work

Cache-aside is the default: read → miss → DB → set. Simple, robust. Write-through writes to cache and DB in one call — good for consistency, bad for latency. Write-behind queues DB writes async — great throughput, risky if the queue loses data. Read-through hides the miss behind a library. Always set a TTL; always jitter it (avoid stampedes when many keys expire at once); always plan for cache warming after a flush. Consider a two-level cache (in-process LRU + Redis) for extreme hot keys.

Read replicas — what to watch out for

Async replication buys read scale but introduces replication lag. Reading a value you just wrote can hit a follower that hasn't caught up — a "read-your-writes" violation. Two fixes: (1) route read-after-write to the primary for a short window, (2) use a session token / LSN pin to wait until the replica catches up. AWS Aurora replicates via a shared distributed storage layer — lag is typically < 20 ms — but the semantic problem is the same.

Partitioning — pruning is everything

Partition by range (usually time), list, or hash. The whole point is partition pruning — the planner never touches irrelevant partitions. Query filters must include the partition key or you scan them all (worse than the un-partitioned table). Time-based partitioning is a maintenance win too: drop last year's partition in ms instead of running DELETE for hours.

Sharding — the shard key is the whole decision

The hard question isn't "how do I shard" — it's "what's the shard key". Pick something that keeps 95% of queries single-shard. Multi-tenant SaaS → tenant_id. Consumer social → user_id. Global marketplace → maybe country + user_id. Bad shard keys create hotspots (one shard doing all the work) or fanout queries (every query hits every shard). Use consistent hashing so re-sharding only moves a slice of data. Cross-shard transactions require 2PC or careful business logic — usually you design them out.

Materialized views & OLAP replica

OLTP databases (row-oriented) are optimized for point reads and small updates. OLAP workloads (column-oriented) scan millions of rows for one aggregate. Mixing the two crushes both. Solution: replicate commits to an OLAP store (Redshift, Snowflake, BigQuery, ClickHouse) via Debezium/CDC/Kinesis Data Streams. Dashboards query the OLAP mirror; the OLTP primary never sees them. Materialized views (Postgres native, or a summary table refreshed by cron) work when refresh cost is acceptable and freshness bounds are loose.

CQRS — the two-model design

The write model and read model are different objects, possibly different databases. A write hits a normalized OLTP store; a stream of changes (CDC or event log) fans out to projections — a Redis structure for a leaderboard, an Elasticsearch index for search, a DynamoDB table for a mobile API, a materialized SQL view for a dashboard. Each read model is optimized for its own query pattern. The write side stays clean and consistent; the read side scales orthogonally.

Event sourcing — writes as an append-only log

The store of record is not "current state" but "every event that ever changed state". Current state is a projection built by folding the event stream. Writes become trivial (append-only, huge throughput on Kafka/Kinesis). You get free audit history, time-travel, and multiple read models. Trade-off: schema evolution of old events is a real engineering discipline; projections are eventually consistent; the mental model is unfamiliar to newcomers.

Polyglot persistence — use the right store

General-purpose SQL is a jack-of-all-trades. Some workloads have masters:

  • Key-value / massive scale → DynamoDB, Redis, Cassandra
  • Timeseries → TimescaleDB, InfluxDB, ClickHouse
  • Full-text search → Elasticsearch, OpenSearch, Meilisearch
  • Graph → Neo4j, Amazon Neptune, TigerGraph
  • Document / flexible schema → MongoDB, DocumentDB
  • Wide-column → Cassandra, ScyllaDB, HBase
  • OLAP → Redshift, Snowflake, BigQuery, ClickHouse, Druid

Real systems typically use three to five stores together, wired via change streams and background workers. This is the ceiling of the ladder — beyond it, every scaling problem is now an application-architecture problem, not a database problem.

💻 Code — the patterns you'll actually write

Cache-aside in C#

public class OrderService(IDistributedCache cache, AppDb db)
{
    public async Task<Order?> GetAsync(int id, CancellationToken ct)
    {
        var key = $"order:{id}";
        var hit = await cache.GetStringAsync(key, ct);
        if (hit is not null) return JsonSerializer.Deserialize<Order>(hit);
        var order = await db.Orders.FindAsync([id], ct);
        if (order is not null)
            await cache.SetStringAsync(key, JsonSerializer.Serialize(order),
                new DistributedCacheEntryOptions { AbsoluteExpirationRelativeToNow = TimeSpan.FromMinutes(5) }, ct);
        return order;
    }
}

Consistent-hash shard picker

public class ShardRouter {
    private readonly SortedDictionary<uint, string> ring = new();
    public ShardRouter(IEnumerable<string> shards, int vnodes = 128) {
        foreach (var s in shards)
            for (int v = 0; v < vnodes; v++)
                ring[Hash($"{s}#{v}")] = s;
    }
    public string ShardFor(string key) {
        var h = Hash(key);
        foreach (var kv in ring) if (kv.Key >= h) return kv.Value;
        return ring.First().Value;   // wrap around the ring
    }
    private static uint Hash(string s) => BitConverter.ToUInt32(SHA1.HashData(Encoding.UTF8.GetBytes(s)), 0);
}

⚖️ Trade-offs, bottlenecks & likely follow-ups

Consistency vs availability at every rung

Each rung introduces new consistency choices. Vertical scaling and indexing preserve strong consistency — same box, same transaction. Cache and read replicas step into eventual consistency (stale read possible). Sharding preserves consistency within a shard but not across. CQRS and event sourcing make eventual consistency explicit and central — the business must accept that "the report shows what was true a second ago". Interviewers love this: at every rung, ask "is that OK for this feature?"

Cost curve

Vertical scaling is linear in dollars. Read replicas are linear per replica. Caching is cheap. Sharding is cheap per shard but expensive in engineering — the operational surface (deployments, backups, monitoring) multiplies. CQRS and event sourcing carry a permanent complexity tax — worth it at scale, punishing at small scale.

Rebalancing is the killer bug

Adding a shard, promoting a replica, migrating storage — every one of these is a distributed-systems problem. Use consistent hashing so re-shards move ~1/N of the data. Use online schema-change tools (pt-online-schema-change, gh-ost) so DDL doesn't lock a table for hours. Test failover regularly — an untested standby is a fake standby.

The observability multiplier

Every rung you add is another metric to monitor. Cache hit ratio, replica lag, connection pool saturation, per-shard QPS, CDC lag, projection age. Without dashboards, you're flying blind and won't spot the next bottleneck until it pages you at 3 am.

Likely follow-up questions

Q: We already have replicas. Why still shard?

Replicas only scale reads. If your write rate exceeds one primary's ceiling (~15k–30k writes/s on a beefy box, tens of thousands more on Aurora), you must shard to scale writes horizontally.

Q: How do you pick a shard key?

Pick something (a) high-cardinality (avoid hot partitions), (b) present on 95%+ of queries (avoid cross-shard fanout), (c) doesn't change often (avoid re-sharding a row). Multi-tenant SaaS → tenant_id; consumer → user_id.

Q: How does Aurora differ from RDS Postgres?

Aurora rewrites the storage layer as a distributed, log-structured, 6-way-replicated fabric. Replicas share storage (no async streaming, < 20 ms lag), storage auto-scales, and failover is sub-30-seconds. Same SQL surface, different guts.

Q: Can we skip rungs?

Rarely. Most teams that sharded before caching regretted it. Most that adopted CQRS before trying materialized views wrote systems they couldn't operate. Pull the cheapest lever first.

Q: Read-your-writes on replicas?

Pin recent writers to the primary for a short window (5–30 s), OR use logical-timestamp/LSN tracking so a read waits until the replica catches up to the client's last observed write.

Q: When should we switch to NoSQL entirely?

When the access pattern is pure key/value, timeseries, document, or graph — and the operational cost of the polyglot outweighs the SQL joins you'd give up. DynamoDB for user profiles/sessions at massive scale is a common landing.

Q: What's the biggest myth?

"Just add a cache". Caching only shifts the bottleneck; it doesn't solve write pressure, replication lag, or wrong-index problems. Diagnose before prescribing.

🗣️ Interview cheat sheet

30-second pitch: "Scaling the database layer is a ladder, not a switch. I start by diagnosing the bottleneck — CPU, IOPS, connections, replica lag, or query plan. Then I climb: vertical scale, then indexing, then connection pooling, then caching, then read replicas, then partitioning, then sharding, then materialized views / OLAP, then CQRS, then event sourcing, then polyglot persistence. Each rung buys a specific capability and breaks in a specific way that motivates the next. Sharding without caching is premature; CQRS without materialized views is over-engineering. The interview answer is not 'add DynamoDB' — it's 'here's how I'd read the metrics and pick the cheapest lever that actually solves the problem'."

Five talking points that win the round

  • "Diagnose first — a missing index beats a shard cluster." Show the interviewer you check EXPLAIN before proposing architecture.
  • "Every rung introduces eventual consistency." Reads from cache / replica / projection are stale. Business must accept it feature-by-feature.
  • "Shard key is the whole sharding decision." Pick one that keeps 95%+ of queries single-shard.
  • "CQRS + event sourcing decouples read and write scaling." This is what powers Amazon, Uber, Netflix under the hood.
  • "Polyglot is the endgame." Real systems have 3–5 stores, each fit-for-purpose, connected by a change stream.

Numbers to quote confidently

  • Single beefy box: ~15–30 k writes/s, ~100 k reads/s.
  • Postgres connection: ~10 MB each; PgBouncer/RDS Proxy multiplexes 100 k+ clients → 500 real conns.
  • Cache hit ratio for well-designed hot paths: 90–99%.
  • Aurora replica lag: < 20 ms typical; classic RDS async replicas: 100 ms – seconds.
  • Consistent hashing: re-shard moves ~1/N of the data, not everything.
