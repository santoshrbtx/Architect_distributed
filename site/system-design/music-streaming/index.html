🎵 Design a Music Streaming Platform

Spotify / Apple Music / YouTube Music at scale: how you serve 100M+ songs to 700M+ users at low latency — audio pipeline, CDN, metadata, search, and recommendations.

🎯 The scenario & where it's used

Streaming audio to hundreds of millions, instantly

A music streaming platform lets users search a huge catalog, hit play, and hear audio start within a fraction of a second — then keep listening smoothly on a train, on wifi, or on a flaky mobile connection. The canonical example is Spotify (as of early 2026: ~761 million monthly active users, ~293M paying subscribers, a catalog of over 100 million songs and 7M+ podcasts), alongside Apple Music, YouTube Music, Amazon Music, and Tidal. The defining characteristic versus video (Netflix/YouTube) is that audio files are small — a few megabytes — so the hard problems shift away from raw bandwidth toward catalog metadata at scale, discovery/search, personalized recommendations, and instant start-up latency.

Interviewers love this problem because it's a rich mix: a read-heavy content-delivery problem (serve the same audio to millions ⇒ CDN + caching), a big-data metadata problem (100M songs, billions of playlist entries), a search problem (autocomplete + fuzzy matching), and an ML problem (Discover Weekly-style recommendations). It lets you show breadth — but the winning move is to lead with the audio delivery path and the read-heavy caching story, because that's the core of "streaming."

🧑‍🎓 In laymen terms

A jukebox in every corner store on Earth

Imagine you and a billion strangers all reach for the same "play" button at once. In under a second, each of you has to hear music. Not "loading… loading…" — actual sound. How on earth does one company pull that off?

Here's the trick: Spotify doesn't keep all its songs in one giant warehouse in California and pipe them to your phone. That would be like everyone in the world calling the same pizza shop — the phone line would jam. Instead, Spotify runs thousands of tiny jukeboxes tucked into cities all over the planet — often inside your own phone company's building. When you tap play, your phone asks the nearest jukebox first. It almost always has the song, because that jukebox is quietly stocked with whatever's trending near you — the top hits in your country, your saved playlists, your morning workout mix.

The songs themselves are also chopped into small three-second chunks, like a book split into single pages. Your phone grabs one page, starts reading (playing) it, and quietly fetches the next page in the background. If your train dips into a tunnel, you're never stuck mid-page — the next few pages are already in your pocket. And if the signal gets bad, Spotify quietly slips you a lower-quality version of the next page so the music never stops, then upgrades again when your wifi comes back.

The really hard part isn't the audio at all — a song is only about 4 megabytes, which is nothing. The hard part is finding what you want among 100 million songs. Type "sha" and you expect "Shape of You" to appear before your finger lifts. That's a giant, ever-updating index — like a librarian who's memorized every book on Earth. And then there's the eerie magic of Discover Weekly: 30 songs you've never heard that somehow feel like yours. That's the computer noticing which songs other people-like-you keep replaying, then quietly slipping them onto your playlist. A jukebox that knows your taste better than your best friend does.

🏗️ High-level architecture

How the pieces fit together

The client talks to an API gateway behind a load balancer for everything except the audio bytes. Control-plane calls (search, playlists, "give me the stream URL for track X") hit stateless microservices backed by databases and caches. When the user presses play, the metadata/streaming service returns a signed, expiring CDN URL; the client then pulls the audio directly from the CDN edge, chunk by chunk — origin is bypassed on a cache hit. Uploads/ingestion run an async transcoding pipeline that produces multiple bitrates and pushes them to blob storage + CDN.

control plane (metadata) data plane (audio bytes) ingestion pipeline

The crucial split is control plane vs data plane. Metadata (small, structured, must be fresh) goes through your services and databases. Audio (large-ish, immutable, cacheable) goes straight through the CDN and never touches your app servers on a cache hit. Keeping them separate is what lets the system scale — say this explicitly in the interview.

🔍 Component deep-dive

Audio ingestion & transcoding pipeline

When a label uploads a master track, an async pipeline (triggered off a queue) transcodes it into multiple codecs and bitrates — e.g. Ogg Vorbis at 96/160/320 kbps and AAC at 128/256 kbps, plus FLAC for lossless — and segments each into small chunks. Spotify streams up to 160 kbps Vorbis on free and 256 kbps AAC / up to 24-bit FLAC on Premium. Outputs are written to blob storage (S3) as the origin and pushed/warmed into the CDN. This is done once per track and is embarrassingly parallel, so a queue + worker fleet (or serverless) handles it. The result is immutable objects with content-hash keys — perfect for caching.

Streaming: chunks + adaptive bitrate

Playback uses chunked, adaptive streaming — the same idea as HLS/DASH for video, applied to audio. The client fetches a manifest, then pulls small time-slices (a few seconds each). If the network degrades, it switches down to a lower bitrate for the next chunk (ABR), avoiding rebuffering; on seek, it jumps to the chunk covering that timestamp instead of re-downloading. To hit sub-250 ms start latency, the client requests a short low-bitrate opening chunk first, prefetches the next track in a playlist, and caches recently-played audio locally. Byte-range requests over HTTP against the CDN are the workhorse.

Metadata store & caching

Track/artist/album metadata is relational-ish and read-heavy: store it in a sharded SQL database (PostgreSQL) or a wide-column store, fronted by Redis for the hot catalog. Because the catalog is ~200 GB and mostly static, caching hit rates are extremely high. User activity — plays, likes, listening history — is append-heavy and huge, so it goes into a horizontally-scalable store like Cassandra (an AP, write-optimized choice) rather than the relational metadata DB.

Search & autocomplete

Search runs on Elasticsearch (inverted index) for full-text/fuzzy matching across song/artist/album names, with a trie/prefix index for instant type-ahead autocomplete. Results are ranked by popularity and personalization signals.

Recommendations

Discovery (Discover Weekly, home feed, radio) is powered by collaborative filtering (matrix factorization over the user×track play matrix), content-based audio features, and NLP over playlists/reviews. It runs offline as batch ML jobs (Spark) writing precomputed recommendations to a fast store the recommendation service reads at request time — you don't compute a model on the hot path.

DRM & licensing

Audio URLs are signed and short-lived so links can't be shared or scraped; premium/offline downloads are encrypted and license-checked. Geo-restrictions enforce per-market licensing at the edge.

💻 C# implementation — the "get stream URL" hot path

The single most-called endpoint is "give me a playable URL for this track at the right bitrate." It's a cache-first lookup that returns a signed, expiring CDN URL — never the raw origin — and picks the bitrate from the client's plan and network hint. This is the core of the streaming control plane.

public sealed class StreamingService
{
    private readonly IDistributedCache _cache;   // Redis
    private readonly IMetadataRepo _meta;         // Aurora/Postgres
    private readonly ICdnSigner _cdn;             // CloudFront signed URLs

    public async Task<StreamManifest> GetStreamAsync(
        string trackId, UserPlan plan, NetworkHint net)
    {
        // 1. Choose the best bitrate for this plan + measured bandwidth (ABR start).
        Bitrate br = PickBitrate(plan, net);

        // 2. Cache-first metadata lookup (hot catalog lives in Redis).
        string cacheKey = $"track:{trackId}:{br}";
        var cached = await _cache.GetStringAsync(cacheKey);
        TrackAsset asset = cached is not null
            ? TrackAsset.Deserialize(cached)
            : await LoadAndCache(trackId, br, cacheKey);

        // 3. Return a SIGNED, short-lived CDN url per chunk — DRM + no hotlinking.
        var url = _cdn.SignUrl(asset.CdnPath, TimeSpan.FromMinutes(30));

        // 4. Fire-and-forget the play event to Kinesis for history + ML features.
        _ = EmitPlayEventAsync(trackId, plan);

        return new StreamManifest(
            Url: url,
            Bitrate: br,
            Codec: asset.Codec,
            ChunkSeconds: 5,               // client pulls ~5s ranges, switches bitrate per chunk
            ExpiresInSec: 1800);
    }

    private async Task<TrackAsset> LoadAndCache(string id, Bitrate br, string key)
    {
        var asset = await _meta.GetAssetAsync(id, br);   // DB on miss
        await _cache.SetStringAsync(key, asset.Serialize(),
            new() { AbsoluteExpirationRelativeToNow = TimeSpan.FromHours(6) });
        return asset;                                    // immutable audio ⇒ safe to cache long
    }
}

Note what's not here: the service never proxies audio bytes. It hands back a signed CloudFront URL and the client streams directly from the edge. Alternatives: HLS/DASH manifests generated by the packager, gRPC instead of REST for internal calls, and Kafka in place of Kinesis for the event bus.

⚖️ Trade-offs, bottlenecks & likely follow-ups

"Why a CDN instead of serving audio from your servers?"

Audio is immutable and the same popular tracks are played millions of times, giving near-100% cache hit rates. CDN edges serve bytes close to users at low latency and offload origin/bandwidth. Your app servers only handle small metadata calls.

"How do you get instant playback (low start latency)?"

Chunked streaming with a short low-bitrate opening chunk, client-side prefetch of the next track, local caching of recent audio, and edge delivery. Adaptive bitrate then ramps quality up once buffered.

"SQL or NoSQL for storage?"

Both. Catalog metadata is relational and read-heavy → Aurora/Postgres with read replicas + Redis. Play history/activity is write-heavy and enormous → Cassandra/DynamoDB. Split by access pattern.

"How do recommendations scale?"

Compute offline in batch (Spark matrix factorization → user/track embeddings), store precomputed results, and serve them with an O(log n) ANN lookup at request time. Never train on the hot path.

"How is this different from designing Netflix/YouTube?"

Same CDN + transcoding backbone, but audio files are ~1000× smaller than video, so bandwidth is a minor concern. The hard parts move to catalog metadata scale, search, and recommendations rather than raw video encoding/bitrate ladders.

"How do you stop link sharing / piracy?"

Short-lived signed CDN URLs, encrypted downloads with DRM licensing, and geo-fencing at the edge for per-market rights.

🗣️ Interview cheat sheet

30-second pitch: "I'd split it into a control plane and a data plane. Audio is small, immutable, and hugely popular, so I transcode each track once into multiple bitrates, store it in S3, and serve it from a CDN via signed URLs — origin is bypassed on cache hits. The client streams in small chunks with adaptive bitrate for instant, smooth playback. The control plane is stateless microservices: metadata in Aurora+Redis (read-heavy), play history in Cassandra (write-heavy), search in Elasticsearch with a trie for autocomplete, and recommendations precomputed offline via collaborative filtering. That handles ~761M users because the CDN absorbs the bytes and each service scales independently."
  • Numbers: ~761M MAU, 100M+ songs, ~5 MB/song, >99.9% read-heavy, ~5M peak concurrent streams.
  • Control plane vs data plane — the core framing. CDN serves audio; services serve metadata.
  • Pipeline: upload → queue → transcode to multi-bitrate (Vorbis/AAC/FLAC) → S3 + CDN warm.
  • Streaming: chunked + adaptive bitrate + prefetch + signed URLs for sub-250 ms start.
  • Storage split: Aurora+Redis (catalog) · Cassandra/DynamoDB (plays) · Elasticsearch+trie (search).
  • Recommendations = offline collaborative filtering (matrix factorization) + ANN lookup.
