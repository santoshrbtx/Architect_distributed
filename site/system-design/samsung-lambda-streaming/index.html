Samsung Real-Time Pricing with Lambda Response Streaming

How Samsung.com replaced hourly precomputation with a stateless, edge-cached, streaming fan-out — cutting P90 latency 90× and shrinking a 100-instance fleet to 5–10 Lambdas.

🎯 Real-world scenario

The business problem Samsung faced

Samsung.com sells thousands of products — phones, TVs, appliances — each with variants (color, storage, size), stackable offers (trade-in, student, employee), and regional pricing rules. On product listing pages such as All Deals and Product Finder, a single viewport can render 30+ price combinations at once. During high-velocity moments — Black Friday flash sales, midnight drops — the price the customer sees on the listing page needs to be exactly the price they see in the cart, or trust collapses.

The legacy system cached prices from an authoritative Pricing Engine via an hourly cron job. That produced a ~1-hour desync window: during a flash sale the listing might still show the old price while checkout showed the new one. Cart shock, abandoned baskets, and support tickets followed. The engineering brief was: make listing pricing real-time and match checkout, without exploding compute cost during peak.

Samsung's answer, published on the AWS Architecture Blog, is a beautiful case study in AWS Lambda Response Streaming combined with CloudFront edge caching, VPC peering, HTTP/2 multiplexing, and a stateless "pass-through" pattern. Result: P90 fell from 4,500 ms → 50 ms (a 90× improvement), cache hit ratio hit 95%, and the compute fleet shrank from 100+ auto-scaled instances to 5–10 Lambda functions. This page walks the design so an architect can whiteboard it end-to-end.

🧑‍🎓 In plain English

The story, told without any jargon

Imagine you own a giant electronics store with 30 different price tags visible on one wall — every phone, every colour, with a matching current discount. Prices change during a Black Friday sale as the day goes on. If a customer sees $499 on the wall but the cash register rings up $449 (or worse, $549), something feels wrong and they leave.

Samsung's old approach was to have an intern (a computer job) walk the whole store every hour, write down every price on 30-inch cards, and stack them in a cupboard. When a customer arrived, the shop assistant grabbed the pre-written cards from the cupboard. Fast — but the cards could be an hour stale. In a flash sale, that's a lifetime.

The new approach is different: instead of pre-writing cards, Samsung set up a system where the moment a customer walks up, 30 tiny messengers sprint to the Pricing Office in parallel. As each messenger comes back with one price, the shop assistant immediately writes it on the wall — without waiting for the slowest one. That's what "response streaming" means: the answer starts appearing before it's fully ready.

On top of that, Samsung put a copy of the last price shown on a bulletin board just outside the shop (the CloudFront edge cache). The next customer within the next 5 minutes reads the bulletin board and never bothers the messengers at all. In practice, 95 out of 100 customers get their prices from the bulletin board; only 5 trigger the messenger run. The Pricing Office barely notices.

That's it. Fast because parallel; fresh because no long cache; cheap because most reads never even touch the server; consistent because everyone looks at the same source of truth. The same pattern powers everything from stock tickers to inventory dashboards — anywhere you need to show many things that each need a quick lookup.

🕰️ The legacy "Data Aggregation (DA)" design and why it broke

The old architecture was a classic Backend-For-Frontend (BFF) pattern with a precomputed cache. A Cron Worker ran hourly, walked the whole catalog, generated every variant × offer × add-on permutation, and dropped them in a local cache. The BFF then served listing pages from that cache.

Why it broke. Two structural failures. First, the cache had to store every permutation a customer might request, most of which were never viewed. Cache size grew combinatorially with variants × offers × regions. Second, the hourly refresh window meant a flash sale that launched at 12:00 didn't reach the listing until 13:00 — while the checkout cart already had the new price. Customers saw $499 in the list, $449 in the cart — good for them, bad for revenue accounting; and the reverse case ($449 in list → $499 in cart) triggered abandoned checkouts.

Fix attempts (a shorter cron interval, incremental refresh) all made cache size worse or compute cost worse. The team decided: rip the cache out entirely, go stateless, and use edge caching for the "hot" reads only.

🏗️ New architecture

The Bulk Arbitration Engine (stateless streaming)

The new design is a "pass-through" pattern. There is no long-lived server-side cache of the catalog. Every request either hits CloudFront's short-TTL edge cache or triggers a Lambda that fans out 30 parallel calls to the Pricing Engine and streams NDJSON results as each price arrives. Freshness comes from the source; latency comes from the edge; parallelism comes from streaming.

CloudFront (95% hit) Lambda streaming Pricing Engine (VPC) NDJSON stream back

Five architectural moves that unlocked the wins:

  1. Cacheable GET — the client encodes 30 SKUs into a dense ~800 B query string (g=group1(p=SKU-A:1:p=SKU-B:2)) so CloudFront can cache by URL. POST would kill caching.
  2. CloudFront edge cache with 5-min TTL and query_string_behavior = "all". Each unique SKU combination gets its own cache entry. 95% hit means the Pricing Engine sees only 1 in 20 real requests.
  3. Lambda in a VPC peered to the Pricing Engine — removes DNS (50 ms) + TCP handshake (100 ms) + TLS (150 ms) overhead that plagued the old public-internet path.
  4. HTTP/2 multiplexing — one persistent TCP + TLS session between Lambda and Pricing Engine carries all 30 lookups as concurrent streams. Cuts payload 76% (170 KB → 40 KB) with GZIP.
  5. Response streaming with NDJSON — Lambda writes each SKU's price to the response as soon as it lands, wrapped in awslambda.streamifyResponse(). TTFB drops to the time of the fastest price lookup, not the slowest.

🔍 Component deep-dive

Cacheable GET — dense query encoding

CloudFront can only cache GET/HEAD. A JSON POST body of 30 SKUs (3–4 KB) would be uncacheable. Samsung's client-side query builder packs each SKU into a token like p=SKU-A:variant1, groups them, and appends context: g=group1(p=SKU-A:1:p=SKU-B:2:c=customerId=123:region=US-EAST-1). Total ~800 B — well under URL length limits, fully cacheable, and includes customer segment as part of the cache key (so employee pricing doesn't leak to guests).

CloudFront cache & origin request policies

The Terraform picks a 5-min default TTL (300 s), 30-min max, and cache-by-query-string. Cookies are excluded so cache keys stay stable. A companion Origin Request Policy forwards allowlisted headers (variant selectors) and the query string to the origin — but only those. GZIP is enabled at the edge. On a cache MISS, CloudFront collapses concurrent identical requests (single-flight to origin), which is a hidden but crucial property that stops thundering herds during flash sales.

Lambda response streaming — how it works under the hood

Traditional Lambda is buffered: your handler returns an object, Lambda serialises it, and the whole body ships out at once. TTFB = duration of the slowest thing. Response streaming, introduced in 2023, adds a responseStream writable stream passed to the handler. Your handler is wrapped in awslambda.streamifyResponse(...). As soon as you write bytes to responseStream, the platform ships them to the caller via HTTP chunked transfer encoding. Metadata (status, headers) is emitted first with awslambda.HttpResponseStream.from(responseStream, {...}). Body follows as chunks.

Samsung's handler pipes three streams: a custom NDJSONTransform that emits one JSON object per line, a Node.js zlib.createGzip() at Z_BEST_SPEED (level 1, latency over ratio), and the responseStream sink:

pipeline(ndjsonTransform, gzip, responseStream);

Each SKU's price, as its Promise resolves, is pushed into ndjsonTransform — flowing through gzip and out to the client immediately.

Fan-out pattern

The controller pattern is Promise.all(skus.map(sku => fetchPricingForSKU(sku).catch(err => ({sku, error: err.message})))). The .catch per SKU ensures a single failure doesn't reject the whole batch — instead it becomes a per-line error object in the NDJSON stream, and the browser can render the other 29 prices normally. Batch is capped at 30 SKUs so Lambda duration stays under 5 seconds even on the slowest cold path.

VPC peering & the connection budget

On the legacy path, Lambda talked to the Pricing Engine over the public internet via a corporate VPN. Every request cold-paid 50 ms DNS + 100 ms TCP + 150 ms TLS = 300 ms of connection overhead. Moving Lambda into a VPC peered with the Pricing Engine's VPC (via a VPC endpoint) put the two on the same private network — the TCP + TLS session becomes long-lived, reused across warm invocations, and DNS resolves through Route 53 private zones with sub-millisecond latency. This alone chopped ~500 ms off P90.

Provisioned Concurrency

Cold starts for Node.js Lambda in a VPC can be 500–1000 ms because the Elastic Network Interface must be attached before the runtime starts. Provisioned Concurrency keeps N warm executions ready — no cold starts for the first N concurrent calls. Samsung pre-warms enough capacity for expected traffic, and CloudFront's 95% cache hit means the "N" needed is very small (5–10 concurrent Lambdas).

HTTP/2 multiplexing to the origin

The Lambda-to-Pricing-Engine hop uses HTTP/2. All 30 concurrent SKU lookups share one TCP + TLS connection, opened once, reused across warm invocations. No per-request handshake. This is the single biggest reason parallel fan-out became viable — over HTTP/1.1 with buffered response, you'd need 30 sockets and 30 handshakes and the response would still wait for the slowest one before shipping.

Client-side incremental rendering

The Angular/React client uses fetch() then response.body.getReader() to read Uint8Array chunks. A TextDecoder converts bytes to text; the client splits on \n, parses each complete line as JSON, and invokes onChunk(price). The DOM updates as each price arrives — TTFB, not full-response, is what the user perceives.

💻 Reference implementation — the streaming handler

Node.js 18+ Lambda handler wired for response streaming, NDJSON framing, GZIP, and per-SKU error isolation. This is the interview-whiteboard version, close to what Samsung deployed:

// index.mjs — Lambda handler (Node.js 18+)
import { pipeline } from "node:stream/promises";
import { Transform }  from "node:stream";
import { createGzip } from "node:zlib";

// A transform that turns each {sku, price} object into one NDJSON line.
class NDJSONTransform extends Transform {
  constructor() { super({ writableObjectMode: true }); }
  _transform(chunk, _enc, cb) {
    this.push(JSON.stringify(chunk) + "\n");
    cb();
  }
}

// Each SKU lookup — HTTP/2 fetch to Pricing Engine, VPC-private endpoint.
async function fetchPricingForSKU(sku, ctx) {
  const res = await fetch(`https://pricing.internal/v1/price/${sku}?ctx=${ctx}`, {
    headers: { "x-request-id": process.env.AWS_REQUEST_ID }
  });
  if (!res.ok) throw new Error(`price lookup ${sku} → ${res.status}`);
  return { sku, ...(await res.json()) };
}

// Wrap the handler so Lambda knows it's a streaming response.
export const handler = awslambda.streamifyResponse(async (event, responseStream, _context) => {
  // 1. Announce metadata (status + headers) before any body bytes.
  responseStream = awslambda.HttpResponseStream.from(responseStream, {
    statusCode: 200,
    headers: {
      "content-type":     "application/x-ndjson",
      "content-encoding": "gzip",
      "cache-control":    "public, max-age=300",   // CloudFront TTL
    }
  });

  // 2. Parse the compact GET query (?g=group1(p=SKU-A:1:p=SKU-B:2...)).
  const { skus, ctx } = parseQuery(event.rawQueryString);
  if (skus.length > 30) throw new Error("batch cap = 30");

  // 3. Build the streaming pipeline: NDJSON → gzip → response.
  const nd   = new NDJSONTransform();
  const gzip = createGzip({ level: 1 });   // Z_BEST_SPEED — latency > ratio
  const done = pipeline(nd, gzip, responseStream);

  // 4. Fan out 30 lookups; each price streams the moment it resolves.
  await Promise.all(skus.map(async (sku) => {
    try {
      nd.write(await fetchPricingForSKU(sku, ctx));
    } catch (err) {
      nd.write({ sku, error: err.message });      // isolate failure per SKU
    }
  }));
  nd.end();
  await done;
});

The client streaming reader — this is what makes the incremental rendering visible to the user:

// price-client.ts — read NDJSON as it streams
export async function streamPrices(url: string, onPrice: (p: Price) => void) {
  const res = await fetch(url);
  const reader = res.body!.getReader();
  const decoder = new TextDecoder();
  let buf = "";
  while (true) {
    const { value, done } = await reader.read();
    if (done) break;
    buf += decoder.decode(value, { stream: true });
    let nl;
    while ((nl = buf.indexOf("\n")) >= 0) {
      const line = buf.slice(0, nl).trim();
      buf = buf.slice(nl + 1);
      if (line) onPrice(JSON.parse(line));           // update DOM immediately
    }
  }
}

⚖️ Trade-offs, bottlenecks & likely follow-ups

Freshness vs latency — why 5-minute TTL and not longer?

Five minutes is short enough that flash sales stay coherent (checkout also honours the same window) yet long enough that CloudFront absorbs 95% of read traffic. Longer TTL improves cache hit but risks the stale-price problem again; shorter TTL raises Pricing Engine load. This is the classic cache TTL trade-off, and picking 5 min was product-driven, not technical.

Batch cap at 30 SKUs

Lambda's request timeout on Function URLs with streaming is 15 minutes, but you never want to sit near it. Capping at 30 keeps worst-case duration under 5 seconds even if a single SKU lookup goes wrong. The client splits larger listing pages into parallel batches with Promise.all, each landing on a different cache entry.

Partial failure semantics

Because each SKU's error becomes a per-line object in the NDJSON stream, the client renders 29 prices normally and shows a fallback for the 30th ("price unavailable, please refresh"). This is much better UX than 500-ing the entire batch. The interviewer might ask "what if the Pricing Engine is entirely down?" — the answer is a Lambda-side circuit breaker returning a cached-last-known-good or a graceful 503 that CloudFront won't cache (via Cache-Control: no-store).

Streaming and status codes

A subtle constraint: once you've written the first byte of the body, the HTTP status is already sent. If the Pricing Engine goes down mid-stream, you cannot switch to a 5xx — you must emit an error line and close cleanly. The client-side parser has to distinguish "SKU-level error object" from "protocol error".

CloudFront cache stampede on cold cache

CloudFront collapses concurrent identical requests to a single origin call per POP (single-flight per URL). This prevents thundering-herd during a flash sale when everyone lands on the same listing at once. If you span multiple POPs, each has its own miss — Origin Shield can further reduce this by adding a shared regional layer.

When streaming is the wrong choice

Streaming makes sense when (a) individual chunks are meaningful to the client (progressive render), and (b) end-to-end latency matters. If the client needs all data to compute a single answer (e.g. total cart), buffered is fine and simpler. If you'd JSON-parse the entire body before doing anything, don't bother streaming.

Likely follow-up interview questions

Q: Why NDJSON and not Server-Sent Events (SSE) or WebSockets?

NDJSON on plain HTTP is CDN-cacheable; SSE requires a persistent connection to origin and isn't cache-friendly. WebSockets are bidirectional and stateful — overkill and uncacheable. NDJSON gives you the streaming benefit while remaining a normal GET.

Q: Why not just cache prices in DynamoDB or ElastiCache?

That was essentially the old design's failure mode — stateful cache drift. The whole point is to keep the source of truth as the only truth and use edge caching only for read-through repeat traffic within a short window.

Q: Can you use response streaming through API Gateway?

API Gateway HTTP APIs (v2) support Lambda response streaming since 2023. REST APIs (v1) do not. Function URLs are the simplest path.

Q: What's the maximum response size?

Lambda response streaming caps at 20 MB per response (soft) and 6 MB for buffered mode. For 30 gzipped NDJSON prices, we're at ~40 KB — nowhere near limits.

Q: How do you invalidate a cache entry mid-TTL when a price actually changes?

You typically don't — 5-min TTL is the SLA. If a price change is critical (recall, compliance), CloudFront supports invalidation by URL pattern; but this is a break-glass, not the norm.

Q: How does Lambda cold-start behave with response streaming?

Cold starts still pay init overhead but the first byte can leave earlier because you don't have to build a full response object. Provisioned Concurrency removes this entirely.
