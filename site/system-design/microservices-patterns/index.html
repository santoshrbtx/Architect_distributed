🎯 Real-world scenario

What we're building & where it's used

Splitting a monolith into dozens of microservices sounds elegant on a whiteboard — until you realise you've traded one big deployment problem for a thousand tiny distributed-systems problems. Where does the mobile app find "the current shipping service" when there are 40 replicas coming and going? How do you keep a slow-dependency from taking down the whole request thread pool? How do you swap in a rewritten billing service without a Big-Bang cutover? The industry answers these with a small canon of well-named patterns — the ones every principal-engineer interview and every AWS Solutions Architect Professional exam probe.

The names in this page come from three canonical sources: Chris Richardson's microservices.io pattern language, Microsoft's Azure Architecture Center design-patterns catalogue, and Martin Fowler's original writings. They cover the whole life-cycle: how clients reach your fleet (API Gateway, BFF), how services find each other (Service Discovery), how they speak resiliently (Circuit Breaker, Bulkhead), how they get cross-cutting features for free (Sidecar, Service Mesh), and how you migrate off a monolith without a rewrite from hell (Strangler Fig). Real deployments — Netflix, Uber, Airbnb, Stripe, Meta, Amazon Retail — all use every one of these; if you can name and defend each one, you signal senior-plus-level thinking in any distributed-systems interview.

🧑‍🎓 In laymen terms

The whole thing, explained without any tech words

Imagine your favourite mall. In the old days it was a single giant shop that sold everything — clothes, groceries, medicines, phones. If the electricity broke in one corner, the whole shop went dark. That's a monolith. Modern malls are different: they're made of many small independent shops. You get more variety, one shop can renovate without closing the others, and no single problem shuts everything down. That's microservices. But now the mall has new problems, and each pattern on this page is the mall's fix for one of them.

How does a visitor find the phone shop when it keeps changing floors? There's a directory near the entrance — that's service discovery. How do you avoid making the visitor walk from shop to shop? There's a concierge at the entrance who takes their whole order and dispatches to the right shops — that's the API Gateway. What if the mobile shopper wants a smaller lighter menu than the counter walker? You give each type of shopper their own personal concierge — that's Backend for Frontend.

What if the electrician's shop is failing today? A helper standing next to every shop handles all the boring but universal stuff — power, plumbing, security cameras — the same way for every shop, in every language. That's the Sidecar. What if too many shoppers rush into a broken shop and the crowd starts blocking other shops? A fuse box automatically trips and diverts them; when the shop looks healthy again, the fuse lets a trickle through, then reopens. That's the Circuit Breaker.

How do you make sure a busy problem in the food-court doesn't drain all the mall's staff away from the electronics section? You put separate teams of staff on each floor, so a stampede in one place can't empty out another. That's the Bulkhead, named after the sealed compartments on a ship.

What about the old giant shop that still handles half the sales — can we replace it without shutting the mall for a year? Yes: build new small shops around it and slowly move each aisle, so shoppers barely notice. Eventually the old shop has nothing left to sell and you tear it down. That's the Strangler Fig, named after a vine that grows around a tree until only the vine is left.

And finally, when the sidecars, the fuse boxes, the retries, the security cameras and the concierge all become too much to configure per-shop, you build a city-wide plumbing and electric grid that every shop just plugs into. That's the Service Mesh. Same eight ideas that Netflix, Uber, WhatsApp and Amazon all rely on — every day, for every request.

📋 Requirements & scale

What a microservices platform must provide

Functional requirements

  • Locate live service instances in a constantly changing fleet (service discovery).
  • Route and compose external requests into internal ones (API Gateway, BFF).
  • Isolate failures so a single broken dependency can't take down the caller (Circuit Breaker, Bulkhead).
  • Provide cross-cutting features — TLS, retries, observability — uniformly across polyglot services (Sidecar, Service Mesh).
  • Evolve the architecture — swap out subsystems incrementally without downtime (Strangler Fig).

Non-functional requirements

  • High availability — no single pattern point-of-failure (registries are HA, gateways are horizontally scaled).
  • Low added latency — the gateway + sidecar overhead should be single-digit ms.
  • Language-agnostic — patterns should work whether the service is Go, Java, C#, Python or Rust.
  • Independently deployable — patching one pattern doesn't require redeploying every service.

🏗️ High-level architecture

Where each pattern lives on the map

Every request in a modern microservices platform passes through most of these patterns in sequence. Here's the canonical layout:

BFF (per client) API Gateway Service Registry Sidecar (data plane) Service Mesh (control plane) Legacy being strangled

A single "get product page" request from a phone travels through: Mobile BFF → API Gateway (auth, rate limit) → discovers Product-svc via registry → hits Svc-A sidecar (mTLS + circuit breaker) → Svc-A calls Svc-B via its sidecar (bulkheaded thread pool) → response composed back. Meanwhile, the same gateway may route /legacy/* to the fading monolith while /new/* already goes to the strangler-replacement services.

🔍 The eight patterns, one by one

Each pattern below is presented the way you should explain it in an interview: the problem it solves, the mechanism, when to use it, and the failure modes to name.

1 · Discovery

Service Discovery / Service Registry

Problem. In a dynamic environment, service instances come and go — autoscaling adds pods, deploys roll them, hosts crash. Clients cannot rely on hard-coded IPs. As microservices.io puts it, "the number of service instances and their locations changes dynamically." Something has to answer the question "where is the shipping-svc right now?" in real time.

Mechanism. A service registry — a highly-available database of (service name → healthy instance list). Instances register on startup and deregister on shutdown; the registry health-checks them and prunes dead ones. Two flavours of the lookup: client-side discovery (client queries the registry, picks an instance, calls it directly — Netflix Ribbon + Eureka) or server-side discovery (a load balancer or router does the lookup — AWS ALB, Kubernetes Service, Consul). Kubernetes bakes it in: every Service gets a stable DNS name resolved to a Cluster IP that routes to healthy pods automatically. Tools: Consul, etcd, ZooKeeper, Netflix Eureka, Kubernetes DNS, AWS Cloud Map.

Watch out. The registry itself must be HA (typically an odd-number Raft/Paxos cluster). Clients should cache lookups (default TTL 30 s in Eureka) to survive registry outages, but stale cache means stale routing. Bootstrapping the registry's address is a chicken-and-egg problem solved with fixed DNS names or Elastic IPs.

2 · Edge

API Gateway

Problem. Exposing dozens of internal services directly to clients is painful: clients must know every service's URL, do multiple round-trips (bad on mobile networks), and understand cross-cutting concerns like auth. As Chris Richardson notes, "a mobile client can only make a few" round trips before the UX suffers.

Mechanism. A single entry point for all external traffic. It performs routing (proxy to the right backend), composition (fan-out to N services and stitch results), protocol translation (public REST/GraphQL ↔ internal gRPC), and centralises auth, rate limiting, throttling, logging, tracing headers, and circuit breaking. Tools: Netflix Zuul, Kong, AWS API Gateway, Spring Cloud Gateway, Envoy at the edge. Gateways are latency-critical and are typically implemented with reactive/NIO stacks (Netty, Node.js).

Watch out. The gateway is on the hot path for every request — a bug or a slow response there takes down the whole platform. Deploy N replicas behind a load balancer, keep it stateless, and never put business logic in it.

3 · Edge variant

Backend for Frontend (BFF)

Problem. A single gateway ends up as a lowest-common-denominator compromise — the mobile app wants a slim, aggregated response; the web app wants richer nested data; the partner API wants a stable long-term contract. Serving all three from one gateway leads to bloated payloads and coupling between UI teams.

Mechanism. Instead of one gateway for everyone, deploy one gateway per client type — a "Backend for Frontend." The mobile BFF returns exactly the shape the mobile app needs; the web BFF returns richer objects; the partner BFF exposes a versioned public API. Each BFF is owned by the team that ships the corresponding frontend, so their release cadences aren't blocked on each other. Originated at SoundCloud (Phil Calçado) and popularised by Sam Newman.

Watch out. N BFFs = N deployables to run. Some duplication of composition logic between BFFs is fine and expected; the alternative — sharing library code that every BFF must upgrade in lockstep — reintroduces the coupling BFFs exist to break.

4 · Co-location

Sidecar

Problem. Every microservice needs the same peripheral capabilities — TLS, retries, timeouts, service discovery client, metrics scraping, distributed tracing, secrets fetching, config reloading. Rewriting these in every language (Go, Java, C#, Python, Rust) is duplication hell and version-drift hell.

Mechanism. Deploy a helper process alongside the application — in Kubernetes, a second container in the same Pod that shares localhost, filesystem volumes, and lifecycle. The main service calls the sidecar over localhost for those cross-cutting features, and outbound traffic passes through the sidecar too. Examples: Envoy (service-mesh data plane), Dapr (state, pubsub, secrets), Vault Agent (secret injection), OpenTelemetry Collector (telemetry pipeline), Fluent Bit (log shipping), istio-proxy (mTLS + policy).

Watch out. Adds ~50–200 MB RAM and 1 ms latency per hop per sidecar; ratio of sidecar-to-app CPU can be surprising for tiny services. Kubernetes 1.29+ supports "native sidecars" (initContainers with restartPolicy: Always) that solve startup ordering and shutdown ordering cleanly.

5 · Reliability

Circuit Breaker

Problem. A downstream service starts failing or slowing. Every incoming request hits the timeout, threads pile up, and the caller runs out of workers — the failure cascades. Retrying makes it worse. As Microsoft docs put it, "this problem can exhaust resources, which might fail other unrelated parts of the system."

Mechanism. A state machine with three states — Closed (calls pass through, failures counted), Open (calls short-circuit immediately with an exception or fallback, no downstream traffic), Half-Open (after a cool-down, let a trickle through; if they succeed close it, if any fail reopen). Tripped by a failure threshold (Hystrix defaults: ≥ 50 % error rate over ≥ 20 requests in a 10 s window). Cool-down 5–60 s. Libraries: Netflix Hystrix (retired), Resilience4j (JVM), Polly (.NET), and built into every service mesh (Istio, Linkerd).

Watch out. Combine with retries carefully — retries inside an Open circuit are wasted. The Half-Open probe must be limited (1–5 requests, not the flood) or you re-DOS the recovering service. Modern implementations replace fixed thresholds with adaptive algorithms based on recent traffic.

6 · Isolation

Bulkhead

Problem. Even with circuit breakers, one type of traffic can exhaust a shared pool (threads, connections, memory) that other traffic needs. Named after the sealed watertight compartments of a ship's hull — if one floods, the ship stays afloat.

Mechanism. Partition resources so a fault in one part cannot drain another. Two shapes: (a) partition the caller's resources — one thread pool or connection pool per downstream dependency (a slow Svc-B doesn't starve Svc-C calls). (b) partition the callee — separate service instances or pools per class of consumer (premium vs free tier, tenant A vs tenant B). Also called cell-based architecture at large scale (AWS uses this for S3 and DynamoDB). Implementations: Hystrix thread pools (default 10 threads/dep), Resilience4j bulkheads, Polly Bulkhead policy, Kubernetes ResourceQuotas, per-tenant separate pods.

Watch out. More partitions = less efficient resource use (each pool has idle capacity). Sizing is empirical — start at (peak concurrency × p99 latency) and adjust. Bulkhead + Circuit Breaker + Retry with jittered backoff is the canonical resilience triad.

7 · Migration

Strangler Fig

Problem. You have a huge monolith and want microservices. A full rewrite ("Big-Bang cutover") almost always fails — Martin Fowler notes wholesale rewrites "tend to go down in flames." How do you replace it without stopping the business?

Mechanism. Named after strangler fig vines that grow around a host tree and eventually replace it. Put a proxy/router in front of the monolith (usually the API Gateway itself). New capabilities are built as new microservices outside the monolith; the router sends only their routes to them and everything else to the monolith. Over months/years, existing capabilities are extracted one seam at a time — each is a small, low-risk, incrementally-shippable migration. When the last route moves off, the monolith is deleted. Fowler stresses this must also be an organisational shift; without team realignment (Conway's Law) "the new systems will end up in a similar mess."

Watch out. Requires clear seams — data ownership boundaries, well-defined APIs. If the monolith has tangled schemas, plan an intermediate "database strangler" first. Migration is measured in years for big estates; keep the migration KPI visible so it doesn't stall.

8 · Platform

Service Mesh

Problem. Once every service has a sidecar doing TLS, retries, circuit breakers, and tracing, configuring them consistently across hundreds of services is a nightmare. You want one place to define "all traffic from Svc-A to Svc-B must be mTLS with mutual auth, retry twice with jitter, timeout 500 ms, and be traced 100 %."

Mechanism. A dedicated infrastructure layer for service-to-service communication, split into a data plane (the sidecar proxies — usually Envoy — that intercept every packet) and a control plane (a component like Istio's istiod that pushes policy, cert bundles, and routes to every sidecar). Delivers zero-trust mTLS, workload identity, traffic management (canary, blue/green, weighted splits, mirroring), retries + circuit breakers at the mesh, and observability (Prometheus metrics + distributed traces on every hop). Products: Istio, Linkerd, Consul Connect, AWS App Mesh, Cilium Service Mesh (eBPF). Istio also supports "ambient mode" that skips the per-pod sidecar for lower overhead.

Watch out. Non-trivial operational cost — a mesh is a distributed system in itself. Not worth it for < 20 services. Overlaps with the API Gateway on north-south traffic; typically the gateway handles ingress (external → cluster) and the mesh handles east-west (service → service).

💻 C# implementation — Polly for Circuit Breaker + Bulkhead + Retry

In the .NET world the reference library is Polly (now integrated into Microsoft.Extensions.Resilience). One HttpClient-typed pipeline can express Circuit Breaker + Bulkhead + Retry + Timeout — the resilience triad — in a handful of lines. This is exactly what the sidecar would do at the mesh layer; you write it inline when you don't have a mesh.

// dotnet add package Microsoft.Extensions.Http.Resilience
using Microsoft.Extensions.DependencyInjection;
using Microsoft.Extensions.Http.Resilience;
using Polly;

public static class ResilienceExtensions
{
    public static IServiceCollection AddResilientClients(this IServiceCollection services)
    {
        // One resilient HttpClient per downstream dependency = BULKHEAD.
        // Each has its own connection pool and its own circuit breaker,
        // so a slow "inventory-svc" cannot exhaust the "payment-svc" pool.

        services.AddHttpClient("payment-svc", c => c.BaseAddress = new("https://payment-svc/"))
            .AddStandardResilienceHandler(o =>
            {
                // Retries: exponential + jitter, 3 attempts max
                o.Retry.MaxRetryAttempts   = 3;
                o.Retry.Delay              = TimeSpan.FromMilliseconds(200);
                o.Retry.BackoffType        = DelayBackoffType.Exponential;
                o.Retry.UseJitter          = true;

                // Circuit breaker: open if > 50% of the last 100 calls fail in a 30s window,
                // stay open for 15s, then trickle through in half-open.
                o.CircuitBreaker.FailureRatio            = 0.5;
                o.CircuitBreaker.MinimumThroughput       = 100;
                o.CircuitBreaker.SamplingDuration        = TimeSpan.FromSeconds(30);
                o.CircuitBreaker.BreakDuration           = TimeSpan.FromSeconds(15);

                // Per-attempt + total timeouts guard against slow dependencies.
                o.AttemptTimeout.Timeout        = TimeSpan.FromSeconds(2);
                o.TotalRequestTimeout.Timeout   = TimeSpan.FromSeconds(10);
            });

        // Second dependency = second bulkhead. Isolation is automatic.
        services.AddHttpClient("inventory-svc", c => c.BaseAddress = new("https://inventory-svc/"))
            .AddStandardResilienceHandler();

        return services;
    }
}

// Consuming service: inject a named client, call it, and let Polly do the rest.
public sealed class CheckoutHandler
{
    private readonly IHttpClientFactory _factory;
    public CheckoutHandler(IHttpClientFactory factory) => _factory = factory;

    public async Task<PaymentReceipt> ChargeAsync(ChargeRequest req, CancellationToken ct)
    {
        var client = _factory.CreateClient("payment-svc");
        try
        {
            var resp = await client.PostAsJsonAsync("/charges", req, ct);
            resp.EnsureSuccessStatusCode();
            return (await resp.Content.ReadFromJsonAsync<PaymentReceipt>(ct))!;
        }
        catch (BrokenCircuitException)
        {
            // Circuit is OPEN → fall back to a queued deferred charge.
            return PaymentReceipt.QueuedForRetry(req.Id);
        }
    }
}

For service discovery in .NET, the same idiom uses AddServiceDiscovery() from Microsoft.Extensions.ServiceDiscovery plus a resolver (DNS SRV, Kubernetes, Consul, or AWS Cloud Map). For the API Gateway/BFF pattern, use YARP (Microsoft's Yet Another Reverse Proxy) — a config-driven Kestrel-based gateway that Microsoft itself runs at scale. For the mesh, Istio + Envoy is universal regardless of language.

⚖️ Trade-offs, bottlenecks & likely follow-ups

Trade-offs

Sidecar-per-pod vs mesh vs in-process library. Sidecars are language-agnostic and hot-swappable but add RAM + latency. In-process libraries (Polly, Resilience4j) are zero-latency but must be maintained per-language. A full mesh centralises policy but is a big platform investment. Small teams start with libraries, mid-size add sidecars for a few services, and mesh comes when > 20 services need consistent policies.

API Gateway vs BFF. One gateway = one deploy, one team, one lowest-common-denominator API. Multiple BFFs = frontend teams own their own contracts, higher velocity, more infra. Standard advice: start with one gateway; split into BFFs only when you feel the coupling pain.

Circuit Breaker in code vs mesh. In-code circuit breakers give the app rich fallback logic (return cached data, degraded response); mesh circuit breakers happen invisibly to the app and can only fail-fast. Best practice: mesh does the coarse tripping, app has fine-grained fallbacks for the paths that matter.

Strangler timeline. Fowler warns the migration is 1–3 years for a real monolith. Sponsorship must be executive, or the "temporary" gateway becomes permanent tech debt.

Bottlenecks & failure modes

  • Registry split-brain — old cache says node X is alive, new cache says X is dead; clients call dead nodes and retry storm. Fix: TTL discipline + fail-fast on connection error.
  • Gateway single-point-of-failure — a bug in the gateway takes the whole cluster down. Fix: canary a small % of traffic to new gateway versions.
  • Sidecar startup ordering — app starts before sidecar has certs, first N requests fail. Fix: Kubernetes native sidecars (1.29+) with startup dependencies.
  • Circuit-breaker false trips — brief network blip → open circuit → cascading fast-fails to callers. Fix: require minimum throughput before tripping (e.g. 100 requests).
  • Bulkhead sizing — pool too small = queue fills, request rejected under normal load; too big = defeats isolation. Instrument and tune from real traffic.
  • Mesh operator complexity — misconfigured VirtualService can break routing cluster-wide. Fix: GitOps + progressive rollout of mesh config, not kubectl apply.
  • Strangler fig zombie routes — the router ends up with dozens of exception routes that nobody remembers. Fix: sunset dates on each temporary rule.

Likely interviewer follow-ups (with crisp answers)

Why not just DNS for service discovery?

DNS has long TTLs (seconds to minutes) and no health-check semantics — a healthy instance change won't propagate fast enough for autoscale/rolling deploys. A registry with sub-second health signals + client cache is the modern answer, though Kubernetes DNS is now good enough for many workloads because it's driven by the API server directly.

API Gateway vs service mesh — do I need both?

Yes, they solve different problems. Gateway = north-south (outside → cluster) with auth, rate limit, transformation. Mesh = east-west (service → service) with mTLS, retries, traffic splits. Some products (Istio Gateway, Kong Mesh) fuse both, but the responsibilities are distinct.

When does the circuit breaker close again?

After the configured cool-down (e.g. 15 s), it moves to Half-Open and lets a limited number of probe calls through. If they succeed, it closes; if any fail, it re-opens and restarts the timer. This prevents flooding a recovering service.

Circuit breaker vs Bulkhead — aren't they the same?

No. Circuit breaker stops calling a failing downstream. Bulkhead protects the caller's own resources so one failing downstream can't drain thread pools/connections used by other downstreams. You use both together.

Why is the Strangler Fig called that?

Named by Martin Fowler after the strangler fig vine, which germinates in a tree, grows around it, and eventually leaves only the fig once the host dies. The metaphor for gradual, incremental replacement of a legacy system.

What's the difference between BFF and API composition?

BFF is who owns and shapes the API — one per client class. API composition is the technique of a gateway or BFF calling multiple backends and merging their responses. A BFF typically uses API composition, but composition can happen in any gateway.

Is a service mesh worth the operational overhead for a 10-service system?

Usually not. Below ~20 services and one language, in-process libraries (Polly for .NET, Resilience4j for Java) get you the same resilience without operating a mesh. Above that, or with polyglot services, the mesh's cross-cutting benefits amortise the platform cost.

How do you version APIs during a strangler-fig migration?

The router (API Gateway) is your versioning surface. Add a /v2/ namespace that routes to new services; keep /v1/ pointing at the monolith. Never break v1 during migration — deprecate on a public schedule instead.

🗣️ Interview cheat sheet

30-second pitch: "There are eight canonical microservices patterns. Service Discovery is a registry that answers 'where is X right now?'. API Gateway is the single entry point that centralises auth, routing, and composition. BFF gives each client class its own tailored gateway. Sidecar is a helper process alongside every service for cross-cutting concerns like TLS and tracing. Circuit Breaker fast-fails a failing downstream to prevent cascading failure. Bulkhead isolates resources — one thread pool per dependency — so a slow service can't drain the whole pool. Strangler Fig is Fowler's incremental legacy-replacement pattern: put a router in front and shift traffic route-by-route until the monolith dies. Service Mesh is the platformisation of the sidecar — a control plane that configures a fleet of Envoy sidecars for mTLS, retries, circuit breaking, and observability. Together they turn 'a bunch of services' into 'a manageable platform.'"

Numbers to quote

  • Circuit-breaker default: 50% error rate over ≥ 20 requests in a 10 s window, open 5–60 s.
  • Hystrix bulkhead default: 10 threads per dependency.
  • Netflix Zuul p50 latency: ~2 ms.
  • Sidecar overhead: ~50–200 MB RAM, ~1 ms latency.
  • Service mesh worthwhile above ~20 services.
  • Strangler migration: months to years, executive-sponsored.
  • API Gateway is essential for mobile (round-trip cost).

Five talking points that win the round

  • Circuit Breaker and Bulkhead are different — one stops calling, the other protects your own pool. Say it out loud.
  • Sidecar → Service Mesh is the natural evolution — you go from a per-app helper to a fleet-wide control plane.
  • BFF fights the "shared gateway compromise" — one per client type, owned by the frontend team.
  • Strangler Fig requires organisational change, not just code — Fowler on Conway's Law.
  • Service Discovery is O(1) with cache — clients cache to survive registry blips.
