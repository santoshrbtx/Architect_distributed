Notification System

A push/email/SMS delivery pipeline built for reliability at billions of messages a day — with queues, retries, priorities, and idempotency baked in.

🎯 Real-world scenario

What we're building & where it's used

A Notification System is the shared platform inside a company that any internal team calls to reach a user through email, SMS, push notification, in-app banner, or WhatsApp. When Amazon sends you "your order has shipped," when Uber pings your phone that the driver is 2 minutes away, when Slack emails you a magic-login link, when your bank texts a one-time password — they all funnel through a notification service that looks like a single "send" API to callers but is really a distributed pipeline of queues, workers, retries, rate limiters, and third-party vendor integrations behind it.

Every large product needs one because reaching users reliably is surprisingly hard. Third-party gateways (Twilio, SendGrid, APNs, FCM) go down. Users disable channels. OTPs must arrive in seconds while a marketing blast can wait an hour. A duplicate push at 3am is a customer-support ticket. And volumes are enormous — LinkedIn sends billions of notifications monthly, Facebook well over 30 billion a day at peak, so the system must absorb massive spikes without dropping a single high-priority message. This is a classic staff-level interview scenario because it hits every core distributed-systems concept: async processing, at-least-once delivery, deduplication, backpressure, priority queues, rate limiting, and observability.

You'll see the design pattern reused everywhere: AWS SNS, Google Cloud Pub/Sub, Twilio, OneSignal, Braze, Airship, Amazon Pinpoint, and every in-house notification platform at Netflix, Uber, LinkedIn, Meta, Airbnb, and Flipkart.

🧑‍🎓 In laymen terms

Explained without the jargon

Imagine your bank has one giant post-office inside its building. Whenever any department — the credit-card team, the loans team, the fraud team — needs to tell a customer something, they don't run to the customer themselves. They drop a slip of paper into the post-office chute that says "tell Priya her card was charged $45." The post-office does all the messy work of actually reaching Priya — figuring out whether she prefers a text message, an email, or the little bell icon in the bank's app; making sure the message doesn't go out twice; slowing down promotional flyers if the post-office is already busy handling urgent fraud alerts.

That's a notification system. Any part of the company can say "tell user X, thing Y" and walk away confident the message will arrive.

Here's why it's harder than it sounds. First, the mailman isn't your employee — you rely on the phone company for texts and Apple/Google for phone notifications. They sometimes have bad days. Second, some messages are on fire and some are lukewarm — a login code you're waiting for right now cannot wait behind five million marketing emails ahead of it in line. Third, the same slip sometimes gets dropped in the chute twice because a computer somewhere got nervous and retried — but Priya must not get charged-notice twice. Fourth, you can't spam anyone — even if six teams want to ping the same customer in five minutes, the system has to be polite.

You've used systems like this every single day. When WhatsApp buzzes your phone, when Uber texts your OTP, when Amazon emails your invoice, when your bank flashes a low-balance alert — it all came through a post-office like this. The interesting design work is: how do you build a post-office that handles ten billion slips a day, never loses an urgent one, never sends the same slip twice, and gracefully slows down when the phone company is having a bad hour?

A tiny example. At 8:03 AM Priya taps "Login" on her bank app. At 8:03:00.4 the login service drops a slip: "SMS OTP 4821 to Priya, urgent." The post-office puts it in the express lane, texts Twilio, and Priya's phone buzzes by 8:03:02. Meanwhile a marketing job dumped 3 million promotional emails into the slow lane at 8:00 AM — those are still calmly draining through in the background, one batch at a time, and Priya's OTP jumped right past them because the express lane never gets stuck.

📋 Requirements & scale

Requirements and back-of-the-envelope math

Functional requirements

  • Accept notification requests from any internal service via a simple POST /notifications API.
  • Support multiple channels: email, SMS, mobile push (APNs/FCM), in-app, WhatsApp.
  • Respect per-user channel preferences and unsubscribe/opt-out state.
  • Support priority tiers — e.g. critical (OTP, security), transactional (order shipped), promotional (marketing blast).
  • Provide a template engine with placeholders and localization (i18n).
  • Support scheduled and bulk notifications.
  • Track delivery status and expose it via an API (queued → sent → delivered → opened).

Non-functional requirements

  • Reliability: at-least-once delivery for every notification; no silent drops.
  • Idempotency: same request ID sent twice → user is notified exactly once.
  • Low latency for critical messages: p99 < 5 s end-to-end for OTP.
  • High throughput: absorb bulk campaign spikes without slowing down critical traffic.
  • Availability: 99.99% for the ingest API; degraded delivery is preferable to rejecting requests.
  • Extensibility: adding a new channel or vendor should not require redesign.

🏗️ High-level architecture

How the pieces fit together

The system is a classic producer → queue → worker → third-party gateway pipeline, split into three logical planes: an ingest plane that validates and normalizes requests, a routing plane that sorts by priority and enforces rate limits and user preferences, and a delivery plane of channel-specific workers that call the actual gateway (SES, Twilio, FCM, APNs) and handle retries.

The flow of one request: a caller service (e.g. the Orders service) calls POST /notifications with a JSON body containing request_id, user_id, template_id, data, and priority. The ingest API validates the schema, deduplicates against a Redis cache keyed by request_id, and drops the message onto one of three priority-partitioned Kafka topics (notif.critical, notif.transactional, notif.promo). Downstream, a Notification Router service consumes each topic (critical topic has the largest consumer group so it drains fastest), fetches the user's channel preferences from a preferences store, checks per-user and per-tenant rate limits in Redis, and expands the request into one message per target channel. Each channel message goes onto its own channel queue (SQS or Kafka partition). Channel workers read from their queue, render the template, call the third-party gateway with a timeout, and on failure re-enqueue with an exponential-backoff delay. After N retries, the message goes to a Dead Letter Queue for later inspection or manual replay.

Every message emits a tracking event (queued/sent/delivered/failed) to a Kafka topic consumed by an analytics pipeline that lands rows in Cassandra + a Kinesis stream feeding real-time dashboards.

🔍 Component deep-dive

1. Ingest API — the deduplication front door

The Notification API is a thin, stateless service behind an API Gateway. It performs three cheap checks and then returns immediately with a 202 Accepted. First, it authenticates the caller (mTLS or short-lived JWT). Second, it validates the schema and rejects malformed requests. Third, and most importantly, it enforces idempotency by consulting Redis with the caller-supplied request_id: SET nx:req:{id} 1 EX 86400 NX. If the key already exists, we treat the request as a duplicate and drop it. This is the primary defence against double sends caused by client retries. Fast-path latency stays under 20 ms so callers can call it synchronously with confidence.

2. Priority-partitioned queues

A single FIFO queue would let a 5-million-row marketing job block an urgent OTP. Instead we run separate Kafka topics per priority tier (or SQS FIFO queues with a priority attribute if you prefer SQS). Consumers on the critical topic get a large consumer group (say, 200 pods), transactional gets 100, promo gets 20. Because Kafka guarantees no head-of-line blocking across topics, the critical lane drains at its own pace irrespective of promo backlog. A common alternative — a weighted round-robin consumer — reads from all three but skews 70/25/5 in favor of higher priority.

3. Notification Router

Consumes from all priority topics, resolves the user, and fans out to per-channel queues. It hits three stores per message: (a) User Preferences DB — which channels the user allows, quiet hours, opt-out flags; (b) Redis rate-limit — token bucket per user (say, 20 promo msgs/day) and per tenant; (c) an optional de-throttle cache to suppress duplicate content sent within N minutes. If a user opted out of email but allows push, only the push message is emitted. The router is where policy lives.

4. Rate limiter

We use a token-bucket algorithm in Redis with Lua-atomic decrement — the standard implementation for high-cardinality rate limits. Two dimensions of buckets are enforced simultaneously: per-user (protects the recipient from spam) and per-tenant / per-vendor (protects our vendor quotas — Twilio, SES). When a bucket is empty, the message is deferred back to the topic with a delay, not dropped, so it eventually gets through.

5. Channel workers & retry mechanism

Each channel has its own pool of workers matched to the vendor's rate limits. The worker fetches a message, renders the template, and calls the vendor with a strict timeout (email 5 s, SMS 3 s, push 2 s). On any 5xx or timeout it invokes the retry loop: exponential backoff with jitter, capped at ~5 attempts. The delay formula is min(cap, base × 2^attempt) + random(0, jitter). Delayed messages are re-enqueued to a delay queue (SQS delay queue, or Kafka with a scheduled-consumer pattern). After the retry budget is exhausted, the message goes to a Dead Letter Queue and an alert fires; operators can inspect and manually replay from the DLQ.

6. Idempotency in the delivery worker

Even with front-door dedup, the worker must be idempotent because Kafka guarantees at-least-once. Before calling the vendor the worker checks (and writes) a marker in Redis keyed on the notification's message_id: SET sent:{id} 1 EX 3600 NX. If a duplicate message arrives after the first has already been dispatched, the worker skips the vendor call. For vendors that also accept an idempotency key (Twilio does), we forward it too — belt and braces.

7. Notification tracker & analytics

Every state transition (queued, sent, delivered, failed, opened, clicked) emits a small event to a Kafka topic. A stream processor lands them in Cassandra partitioned by user_id, day — write-optimized, cheap seeks by user. A parallel branch feeds a real-time dashboard (deliveries/sec, DLQ growth, vendor latencies).

8. Template & personalization service

Templates live in an object store with a metadata table (S3 + DynamoDB). The router resolves template_id + locale and hands the worker a rendered payload, or defers rendering to the worker to save router CPU. Personalization tokens ({{first_name}}) get filled in from the request's data field.

💻 C# implementation

Two of the trickiest bits deserve reference code: the ingest-time idempotency check and the channel worker's retry loop with exponential backoff + jitter. The snippet below is realistic ASP.NET Core / StackExchange.Redis / Polly — the same libraries you would ship in production.

// ------------------------------------------------------------
// 1. Ingest API — accepts a notification, dedups, publishes to Kafka
// ------------------------------------------------------------
public class NotificationController : ControllerBase
{
    private readonly IConnectionMultiplexer _redis;
    private readonly IKafkaProducer _kafka;

    [HttpPost("/notifications")]
    public async Task<IActionResult> Send([FromBody] NotificationRequest req)
    {
        // Fail fast on missing idempotency key — callers must supply one.
        if (string.IsNullOrWhiteSpace(req.RequestId))
            return BadRequest("request_id required");

        // SET NX EX — atomic dedup; TTL bounds Redis memory.
        var db = _redis.GetDatabase();
        var firstSeen = await db.StringSetAsync(
            key:   $"nx:req:{req.RequestId}",
            value: "1",
            expiry: TimeSpan.FromHours(24),
            when:  When.NotExists);

        if (!firstSeen) return Accepted(new { status = "duplicate" });

        // Priority routes to a topic; unknown priority defaults to transactional.
        var topic = req.Priority switch
        {
            Priority.Critical      => "notif.critical",
            Priority.Promotional   => "notif.promo",
            _                       => "notif.transactional"
        };

        // key by user_id → same user's msgs land on the same partition (ordering).
        await _kafka.ProduceAsync(topic, key: req.UserId, value: req);
        return Accepted(new { status = "queued", req.RequestId });
    }
}

// ------------------------------------------------------------
// 2. SMS Channel Worker — retries with exponential backoff + jitter,
//    idempotency guard, DLQ on exhaustion.
// ------------------------------------------------------------
public class SmsWorker
{
    private readonly IConnectionMultiplexer _redis;
    private readonly ITwilioClient _twilio;
    private readonly ISqsClient _sqs;
    private static readonly Random Rng = new();

    private const int  MaxAttempts = 5;
    private const int  BaseMs      = 500;
    private const int  CapMs       = 30_000;

    public async Task HandleAsync(SmsMessage msg, CancellationToken ct)
    {
        // Second-line idempotency: skip if already sent (Kafka may redeliver).
        var alreadySent = !await _redis.GetDatabase().StringSetAsync(
            $"sent:{msg.MessageId}", "1", TimeSpan.FromHours(1), When.NotExists);
        if (alreadySent) return;

        try
        {
            // Twilio also accepts an idempotency key — belt and braces.
            await _twilio.SendSmsAsync(msg.PhoneNumber, msg.Body,
                idempotencyKey: msg.MessageId, cancellationToken: ct);

            // Emit tracking event (sent) — details omitted.
        }
        catch (TwilioTransientException ex) when (msg.Attempt < MaxAttempts)
        {
            var delayMs = Math.Min(CapMs, BaseMs * (int)Math.Pow(2, msg.Attempt));
            var jittered = Rng.Next(0, delayMs); // full-jitter (AWS blog): random[0, delayMs]

            msg.Attempt++;
            msg.LastError = ex.Message;

            // Re-enqueue with delay so the message becomes visible again after backoff.
            await _sqs.SendAsync("sms-queue", msg, delaySeconds: jittered / 1000);

            // IMPORTANT: clear the "sent" marker so the retry can proceed.
            await _redis.GetDatabase().KeyDeleteAsync($"sent:{msg.MessageId}");
        }
        catch (Exception ex)
        {
            // Non-retriable OR budget exhausted → DLQ + alert.
            await _sqs.SendAsync("sms-queue-dlq",
                new { msg, reason = ex.Message, ts = DateTime.UtcNow });
        }
    }
}

Two things worth calling out: (a) the ingest handler is synchronous but fast — the caller gets a 202 Accepted and moves on, while the actual delivery is async; and (b) the retry loop uses full jitter (Random(0, delay)) rather than plain exponential backoff — a small change that meaningfully reduces the thundering-herd after a vendor outage. Alternative stacks: Java with Spring + Resilience4j retries, Go with backoff/v4 + Kafka client, or Node.js with BullMQ (Redis) for smaller workloads.

⚖️ Trade-offs, bottlenecks & likely follow-ups

The main tension in a notification system is between reliability and cost. At-least-once delivery is easy; exactly-once is not — it requires end-to-end idempotency and monotonic message IDs, and you pay for it with more Redis, more state, more code. Most teams pick at-least-once + strong idempotency and call it a day, because a user rarely notices a duplicate SMS as much as they notice a missing OTP. The failure mode you are most likely to interview on is vendor outage: SES silently drops your throughput to 1k/s, your queue backs up, DLQ grows, and users see delayed emails. The escape hatch is multi-vendor fallback — route to Twilio if SNS SMS is degraded, use SendGrid if SES rate-limits — driven by circuit breakers that observe error rates per vendor.

Two other classic gotchas: hot users (a celebrity account gets 10,000 events at once — you need per-user rate limiting and partition-aware sharding to avoid one Kafka partition owning all the traffic) and token invalidation (an iOS app is uninstalled → APNs returns 410 Gone; the worker must remove that device token from the preferences store, otherwise it wastes vendor calls forever).

Likely interview follow-ups

How do you achieve exactly-once delivery?

You approximate it with at-least-once + idempotency: ingest-time dedup on request_id, worker-side dedup on message_id before the vendor call, and pass an idempotency key to the vendor when they support it (Twilio, Stripe, some SES flows do). True exactly-once across independent networks is impossible; users won't notice the difference if idempotency is airtight.

What if the third-party vendor is down for an hour?

The circuit breaker opens after N consecutive failures and stops calling the vendor; messages accumulate in the channel queue (harmless — queues are durable) or fan out to a secondary vendor. Once the breaker sees healthy probes it half-opens and drains the backlog. For truly urgent messages you can hot-swap to a fallback provider (SNS ↔ Twilio) on the fly.

How would you scale to 10× traffic tomorrow?

Ingest API and channel workers auto-scale horizontally — Kafka gets more partitions on the critical topic, SQS is elastic by default, Redis moves to cluster mode. The real bottleneck is vendor quotas, not us: negotiate higher SES/Twilio limits, buy more phone-number pools for SMS, add device-token sharding for push. Also expect the tracking store to be the next thing to feel it — pre-shard Cassandra with an extra partitioning dimension.

How do you handle scheduled or time-zone-aware sends?

A separate scheduler service stores scheduled jobs in a sorted set / min-heap keyed on send_at. A tight loop pops due entries every second and injects them into the priority topics. For time-zone-aware sends, store the user's TZ in preferences and resolve send_at per user at enqueue time.

How do you prevent notification storms after a bug?

Two guardrails. First, a global kill-switch per tenant/template — flipping a flag in DynamoDB causes the router to drop matching messages instantly. Second, a per-user cooldown maintained in Redis (max N msgs / hour); anything above it gets rejected and logged. In practice: also have an on-call runbook for "purge Kafka topic X" and rehearse it.

Why not just use SNS or SendGrid directly and skip building this?

Small startups do exactly that. You start needing this system when: (a) you have >1 channel per user (choice logic), (b) you need per-user preferences and unsubscribes across channels, (c) you need centralized rate-limiting, dedup, and analytics, (d) you have compliance rules (quiet hours, GDPR opt-out) that must be enforced uniformly. Below that scale, SNS/SendGrid is fine.

🗣️ Interview cheat sheet

30-second pitch: "I'd build a fan-out pipeline with an idempotent ingest API in front of three priority-partitioned Kafka topics — critical, transactional, promo. A router consumes them, applies user preferences and Redis token-bucket rate limits, then fans out to per-channel SQS queues. Channel workers call the vendor with a 3-attempt exponential-backoff-with-jitter retry, dead-letter after that, and everything emits tracking events to Cassandra. Idempotency is enforced twice — ingest and worker — using Redis SET NX. That gives at-least-once delivery, priority isolation, and no duplicate sends."

  • 60/25/5/10 split across push / email / SMS / in-app.
  • < 3 s p99 for OTP; < 60 s p99 for transactional; < 1 h for promo.

Five talking points that win the round

  • "Priority via separate topics, not a heap — avoids head-of-line blocking without an in-memory bottleneck."
  • "Idempotency is doubled: ingest Redis dedup on request_id, worker Redis marker on message_id."
  • "Retries use exponential backoff with full jitter, not plain backoff — cures thundering-herd."
  • "Bad-vendor day is handled by circuit breaker + multi-vendor fallback, not by dropping messages."
  • "Every message emits a tracking event — the tracker is a Kafka → Cassandra log, not a DB update — writes stay append-only."
