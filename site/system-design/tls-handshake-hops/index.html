🔒 TLS Handshake Across Hops (with TLS Termination)

What happens when your HTTPS request passes through DNS → CDN edge → WAF → Load Balancer → App → DB: where TLS terminates, which certificate is presented at each hop, and how mTLS, SNI, and session resumption plug in.

🎯 Real-world scenario

What we're building & where it's used

You type https://checkout.example.com in a browser. A little green padlock appears. Under that padlock, a chain of trust was just negotiated across four to six different servers, and each hop had to decide: do I terminate TLS here, forward the raw ciphertext, or re-encrypt to the next hop? Understanding this chain — where the certificate lives, where private keys are unwrapped, where the plaintext is briefly visible — is the difference between a system that survives a security audit and one that hemorrhages secrets.

Every production HTTPS stack does this. Netflix terminates TLS at Open Connect + AWS edge, re-encrypts to services with SPIFFE mTLS. Stripe pins to Cloudflare then Full-Strict to origin, plus mTLS between microservices. Cloudflare's public-facing "Full (strict)" mode is literally two independent TLS sessions stitched together. AWS ALB + CloudFront ships with server-cert termination at the edge and optional back-end HTTPS reencryption plus mTLS to origin. Istio / Linkerd service meshes put a sidecar in front of every pod so that even east-west traffic inside the cluster is mTLS-encrypted using SPIFFE identities issued by an internal CA. Any senior interview asking about "end-to-end encryption in a real stack" wants you to walk this chain.

🧑‍🎓 In laymen terms

What's actually happening under the padlock

Imagine you want to send a sealed letter to your bank. You care about two things: (1) nobody peeks inside and (2) you're actually writing to your bank, not a scammer pretending to be them.

Here's the trick real banks use. Your bank has a big ID card signed by a trusted authority — think of it like a passport office everyone in the world already trusts (Apple, Google, Microsoft all pre-installed a list of them on your phone). When you first knock on the bank's door, they show you the ID card. Your phone looks at the signature, walks up the chain — "this card was signed by GlobalTrust, and my phone trusts GlobalTrust, so I trust the bank" — and then quietly agrees on a secret handshake with the bank that no eavesdropper on the sidewalk can hear. Everything you send after that is scrambled with that secret. That's HTTPS. That agreement is called the TLS handshake.

Now the twist. In real life, your letter doesn't go straight to the bank. It stops at a chain of post offices: first the neighborhood mailbox (the CDN — a fast local drop-off near you), then the sorting facility (the firewall that inspects for suspicious mail), then the regional depot (the load balancer that spreads mail across many bank branches), and only then the actual bank clerk (the application server). Each stop needs to make a choice: "do I open the envelope, read the address, and re-seal it before passing it on? Or do I forward the sealed envelope as-is?"

Most big websites choose the "open and re-seal" approach at each stop, because otherwise the sorting facility can't sort — it doesn't know which branch the letter is for without reading the address. That's called TLS termination. Every re-sealing stop needs its own ID card (its own certificate), and it needs to trust the ID card of the next stop. When two neighboring stops both show ID to each other, that's called mTLS — mutual — like two secret agents flashing badges before speaking.

Concrete example. You tap "pay" in the Amazon app. Your phone talks to a CloudFront server 30 km away (that's stop 1, and it shows you an amazon.com certificate). CloudFront decrypts your request, checks it, then opens a fresh sealed envelope to an Amazon load balancer inside AWS. The load balancer decrypts again, picks a healthy checkout server, and sends a third sealed envelope to that server. Three separate handshakes, three separate certificates, three chances for something to go wrong — and the padlock icon in your browser only sees the first one. The whole reason engineers design this so carefully is that the padlock on your screen is only proving the first hop. The rest is on the platform team.

📋 Requirements & scale

Requirements and back-of-the-envelope math

Functional

  • Every hop that inspects or routes traffic (CDN, WAF, LB) must be able to read the plaintext HTTP to make its decision — meaning TLS must terminate there.
  • End-to-end confidentiality of app data, even inside our VPC — traffic to origin, and pod-to-pod, must not be cleartext.
  • Support one wildcard cert or SAN-multiplexed certs (*.example.com, api.example.com) with SNI-based selection.
  • Support mTLS for B2B partner APIs and for service-to-service inside the mesh.
  • Session resumption to keep P99 first-byte latency low for repeat visitors.
  • Zero-downtime cert rotation on 90-day (Let's Encrypt) or 13-month (public CA) cadences.

Back-of-envelope traffic

A production tier at ~50 K HTTPS RPS with 20% new handshakes (session-cache hit ratio 80%) needs ~10 K TLS handshakes/sec. Each 1.3 handshake is one ECDHE key gen + one signature verify (leaf) + one signature (server side, plus OCSP staple). On a modern CPU that's roughly 1–2 ms of crypto per handshake, i.e. ~15–30 CPU-seconds of crypto per second → dedicated TLS termination on hardware-accelerated LB or ASIC-backed CDN edge is a big win.

🔍 Component deep-dive

1. The TLS 1.3 handshake (single hop, in detail)

TLS 1.3 (RFC 8446) is a 1-RTT handshake — half the round trips of TLS 1.2. The magic: the client speculatively sends its ECDHE key share in the very first message.

  1. ClientHello. Random nonce, list of supported cipher suites (TLS 1.3 only allows AEAD suites: AES_128_GCM_SHA256, AES_256_GCM_SHA384, CHACHA20_POLY1305_SHA256), SNI extension naming example.com, ALPN extension proposing h2/h3, supported_versions extension announcing 1.3, and one or more key_share ECDHE public keys (typically x25519).
  2. Server picks. Server sees the SNI, looks up the right cert bundle for example.com, generates its own ECDHE key pair, computes the shared secret, derives the handshake traffic keys.
  3. ServerHello. Server random, chosen cipher suite, its key_share. Everything after this in the handshake is already encrypted under the handshake key — a TLS 1.3 innovation.
  4. {EncryptedExtensions, Certificate, CertificateVerify, Finished}. Server sends its leaf + intermediate certs (roots are never sent — they're pre-installed on the client). CertificateVerify is a signature over the transcript proving the server owns the private key. Finished is an HMAC over everything, sealing the handshake.
  5. Client validates. Walks the chain: leaf → intermediate signature checks → intermediate is signed by a root in the local trust store. Checks SAN matches the hostname. Checks validity dates. Checks OCSP/CRL revocation (usually via a stapled OCSP response glued to the cert). If any check fails → hard failure.
  6. Client Finished + application data. One RTT total. The client can start sending HTTP requests immediately.

0-RTT (resumed). On a subsequent visit, the client uses a PSK (pre-shared key) issued by the server as a NewSessionTicket during the previous handshake. It can send application data with the ClientHello — zero round trips before first byte. The trade-off: 0-RTT data is replayable, so it's only safe for idempotent GETs. Anti-replay hooks live at the edge.

2. SNI — why it's the hinge of multi-tenant TLS

Server Name Indication is a TLS extension in the ClientHello where the client says "I'm talking to example.com" before the server sends its certificate. Without SNI, a shared-IP server (like a CDN POP with 10,000 tenants) can't know which cert to present. SNI is cleartext in TLS 1.2 and 1.3 — a well-known privacy leak that Encrypted Client Hello (ECH) is designed to fix; ECH is deployed at Cloudflare and Chrome but not yet universal. Every hop that terminates TLS uses SNI to route to the right cert bundle.

3. Session resumption (session ID vs session ticket vs PSK)

MechanismStateWhere used
Session ID (TLS 1.2)Server keeps a cache of session state keyed by IDHistoric; problematic in a load-balanced fleet because state doesn't share.
Session Ticket (TLS 1.2/1.3)Server issues an encrypted ticket; client holds it. Server key = "STEK".Stateless resumption. STEKs must be rotated cluster-wide (~24 h) to preserve forward secrecy.
PSK (TLS 1.3)Pre-shared key derived from a prior handshake, delivered via NewSessionTicketEnables 1-RTT resumption and 0-RTT early data.

Every TLS-terminating hop maintains its own session cache/tickets. Session resumption on the browser → edge hop does not extend to the edge → origin hop — each pair independently resumes with its own keys.

4. The termination decision at each hop — the five modes

Off

insecure

Plain HTTP end-to-end. No cert on any hop. Ubiquitous still on internal legacy — never appropriate for public traffic.

Flexible

partial

TLS visitor→edge, plain HTTP edge→origin. Browser sees a padlock, but the origin leg is cleartext. Considered anti-pattern; enables downgrade attacks.

Full

crypto only

TLS both hops, but edge accepts any origin cert including self-signed. Encrypts but doesn't authenticate the origin — MITM feasible on the edge→origin path.

Full (strict)

baseline

TLS both hops, edge validates origin cert against a public CA or Cloudflare Origin CA. The correct default for public HTTPS.

Strict (SSL-Only Origin Pull)

strongest

Edge always uses HTTPS to origin with full validation, even when the visitor came in over HTTP. Best origin protection.

End-to-end mTLS (mesh)

strongest

Every hop presents its own cert and validates the peer's. SPIFFE/SPIRE issues short-lived workload identities. Istio, Linkerd, App Mesh do this transparently via sidecars.

5. mTLS — mutual TLS

In normal TLS, only the server authenticates. In mTLS, the server ALSO sends a CertificateRequest after ServerHello, and the client responds with its own Certificate + CertificateVerify proving key ownership. Real-world uses:

  • B2B partner APIs — Stripe, Plaid, ACH gateways demand a client cert.
  • Service mesh — Istio auto-injects an Envoy sidecar that terminates+re-originates every east-west connection as mTLS with SPIFFE IDs like spiffe://prod.example/ns/checkout/sa/api.
  • Zero-Trust corporate access — Google BeyondCorp, Cloudflare Access, Tailscale.
  • AWS ALB mTLS — ALB can be configured in passthrough mode (forward client cert in headers like X-Amzn-Mtls-Clientcert) or verify mode (validate against a trust store, then forward).

6. Certificate lifecycle at each hop

Certs are ephemeral. Real stacks automate rotation aggressively:

  • Public edge (browser-facing): Let's Encrypt / DigiCert / Sectigo, 90-day rotation, ACME auto-renewal. AWS ACM does this for CloudFront/ALB automatically.
  • Origin (edge-facing): Cloudflare Origin CA (private trust root, 15-year cert), or AWS ACM for ALB.
  • Service mesh (pod-to-pod): SPIFFE/SPIRE issues 1-hour certs, refreshed by the sidecar in the background. Rotation invisible to app code.
  • Database (app-to-DB): AWS RDS/Aurora ship with a rotating CA bundle; clients pin to it. Snowflake, Postgres, MySQL support TLS in transit with peer verification.

The two "why this?" moments an interviewer will probe:

  • Why ECDHE and not RSA key exchange? Because RSA key exchange encrypts the pre-master secret with the server's long-term public key, so anyone who later steals that private key can decrypt all past traffic. ECDHE uses ephemeral keys — the private half is destroyed after the handshake — giving forward secrecy. TLS 1.3 removes RSA key exchange entirely for exactly this reason.
  • Why AEAD-only ciphers? Because CBC + MAC-then-encrypt suffered a decade of attacks (Lucky Thirteen, BEAST, POODLE). AEADs combine encryption + integrity in one primitive with a formal security proof.

💻 C# implementation

The two most common code you'll write in this space, in .NET 8+:

1. Kestrel: force TLS 1.3, require client cert (mTLS)

// Program.cs — an ASP.NET Core service that terminates TLS itself
// and requires clients to present a valid X.509 cert (mTLS).
var builder = WebApplication.CreateBuilder(args);

// Load the server cert (in production: from ACM via a rotation Lambda,
// or from a mounted secret; here we read from the local file system).
var serverCert = X509CertificateLoader.LoadPkcs12FromFile(
    "/etc/tls/server.pfx", builder.Configuration["CertPassword"]);

builder.WebHost.ConfigureKestrel(k =>
{
    k.ConfigureHttpsDefaults(https =>
    {
        https.ServerCertificate       = serverCert;
        https.SslProtocols            = SslProtocols.Tls13;   // pin to 1.3
        https.ClientCertificateMode   = ClientCertificateMode.RequireCertificate;
        https.CheckCertificateRevocation = true;

        // Validate the client cert against OUR private root, not the OS store.
        https.ClientCertificateValidation = (cert, chain, errors) =>
        {
            var ourRoot = X509CertificateLoader.LoadCertificateFromFile("/etc/tls/ca.pem");
            chain.ChainPolicy.CustomTrustStore.Clear();
            chain.ChainPolicy.CustomTrustStore.Add(ourRoot);
            chain.ChainPolicy.TrustMode = X509ChainTrustMode.CustomRootTrust;
            chain.ChainPolicy.RevocationMode = X509RevocationMode.Online;
            return chain.Build(cert);
        };
    });
});

var app = builder.Build();

// Authorization by SPIFFE-ish SAN URI on the client cert.
app.MapGet("/whoami", (HttpContext ctx) =>
{
    var cert = ctx.Connection.ClientCertificate;
    var spiffe = cert?.Extensions
        .OfType<X509SubjectAlternativeNameExtension>()
        .FirstOrDefault()?.EnumerateUriNames().FirstOrDefault();
    return new { subject = cert?.Subject, spiffe };
});

app.Run();

2. Outbound: strict TLS + cert pinning (SPKI) when calling a partner

// Cert pinning: don't just trust the CA — pin to the exact public-key
// hash the partner published. This defeats a rogue CA issuing a fake cert.
public static class PinnedHttpClientFactory
{
    private static readonly HashSet<string> PinnedSpkiSha256 = new
    {
        "bXG3d0K...==",    // primary key pin
        "aLmN8Pq...==",    // backup key pin (rotate to)
    };

    public static HttpClient Create(string baseUrl)
    {
        var handler = new SocketsHttpHandler
        {
            SslOptions = new SslClientAuthenticationOptions
            {
                EnabledSslProtocols = SslProtocols.Tls13,
                RemoteCertificateValidationCallback = (sender, cert, chain, errors) =>
                {
                    if (errors != SslPolicyErrors.None) return false;   // chain must build
                    var spki = (cert as X509Certificate2)!.PublicKey.EncodedKeyValue.RawData;
                    var pin  = Convert.ToBase64String(SHA256.HashData(spki));
                    return PinnedSpkiSha256.Contains(pin);
                }
            }
        };
        return new HttpClient(handler) { BaseAddress = new Uri(baseUrl) };
    }
}

Alternatives. In Go, crypto/tls with VerifyPeerCertificate. In Node, use tls.createSecureContext + checkServerIdentity. In a service-mesh world, don't roll your own — configure Envoy/Linkerd via CRDs and let the sidecar terminate TLS with SPIFFE IDs. Application code then never touches certs.

⚖️ Trade-offs, bottlenecks & likely follow-ups

Why terminate TLS at the edge at all — isn't end-to-end passthrough more secure?

Passthrough (NLB TCP mode, no decryption at the LB) is more private, but you lose everything the LB was there for: L7 host/path routing, WAF rules, sticky sessions, rate limiting, ALPN negotiation, HTTP/2 → HTTP/1.1 downgrade, header injection for auth. Real-world designs terminate at the edge and re-encrypt to the next hop with strict cert validation. You get both routing intelligence and confidentiality.

Where's the crypto bottleneck at scale?

RSA private-key ops (~1–2 ms each) at the terminating LB. Two mitigations: (a) prefer ECDSA-P256 leaf certs (~10× faster than RSA-2048 signatures for the server side), and (b) session resumption to skip full handshakes. Cloudflare famously moved from RSA to ECDSA-only edge certs and cut edge CPU by ~40%.

What happens if the origin cert expires but the edge cert is fine?

Under Full-Strict: browser padlock still green (session 1 is healthy), but the edge cannot validate session 2, so it returns HTTP 526 / 502 / 503 to the user. The blast radius is the origin fleet, not the edge fleet. This is why cert-expiry alerting must monitor every hop's cert independently, not just the public one.

How does SNI interact with certificate selection at a multi-tenant CDN?

The CDN worker inspects the SNI in the ClientHello, looks up the tenant's cert bundle in a shared cache (typically keyed by hostname → SNI dictionary), and serves the right leaf. Because SNI is cleartext, an eavesdropper can still see which domain you're visiting even though the URL path is encrypted — ECH (Encrypted Client Hello) fixes this by wrapping the inner ClientHello in an outer hello encrypted to the CDN's HPKE key.

Isn't mTLS operationally painful — cert distribution, rotation, revocation?

Yes, unless you delegate it. That's what SPIFFE/SPIRE and service meshes exist to solve. SPIRE issues short-lived (1 h) SVIDs to each workload identity, the sidecar rotates transparently, revocation happens by refusing to reissue. App code never sees a cert. AWS's App Mesh and Nitro Enclaves push it further — private keys never leave a hardware boundary.

What about 0-RTT replay?

TLS 1.3 0-RTT lets the client send app data with the ClientHello using a PSK from a prior session. An attacker who captures 0-RTT data can replay it. Only allow 0-RTT on idempotent requests (GET, HEAD) and configure the edge to reject 0-RTT for POST/PUT/DELETE. Cloudflare and CloudFront both have a per-path 0-RTT toggle.

Where does HTTP/3 (QUIC) change this?

QUIC embeds TLS 1.3 into the transport layer — the handshake is folded into UDP packets, and the handshake + first data is 1-RTT (0-RTT with a token). Termination logic is identical (SNI, cert chain, ALPN), but the LB must speak QUIC/UDP — CloudFront and ALB both support it since 2023. The number of hops and where each terminates is unchanged.

Failure modes to name in the interview

  • Cert chain hole: forgot the intermediate — clients whose OS store doesn't have that intermediate cached will fail (Firefox is famously stricter than Chrome here).
  • Clock skew: a client with a wildly wrong clock rejects even a valid cert. Common in embedded devices.
  • Wildcard depth: *.example.com covers api.example.com but not v1.api.example.com. Bug seen in production.
  • STEK divergence: if two edge nodes have different session-ticket keys, resumption fails and you pay full handshake cost.
  • OCSP stapling stale: if the server's stapled OCSP response is stale, strict clients may reject.
  • Downgrade to Flexible mode: an operator accidentally flips Cloudflare from Full-Strict to Flexible — the padlock stays green for users, but origin traffic is now cleartext for an attacker to sniff.

🗣️ Interview cheat sheet

The 30-second pitch: "HTTPS end-to-end in a real production stack isn't one TLS session — it's a chain of them. The browser negotiates TLS 1.3 with the CDN edge using a public leaf cert selected by SNI; the edge terminates, decrypts to inspect the request, then opens a fresh TLS session to the load balancer; the LB terminates again for L7 routing and re-encrypts to the app pod via a private-CA cert; and inside a service mesh, every pod-to-pod hop is mTLS with SPIFFE workload identities. Each hop is its own cert, its own private key, its own compliance surface — and only the first hop is what your browser padlock covers."

  • Say TLS 1.3 = 1-RTT handshake, 0-RTT with a PSK (only for idempotent traffic).
  • Say SNI selects the cert on multi-tenant edges — it's cleartext; ECH fixes that.
  • Name the five Cloudflare modes: Off / Flexible / Full / Full-Strict / Strict-Origin-Pull. Full-Strict is the default answer.
  • Name ECDHE for forward secrecy; drop the phrase "AEAD-only in TLS 1.3."
  • Name ACM + ACM Private CA + SPIFFE/SPIRE for the four cert-issuance layers.
  • Name the compliance surface: every process that decrypts is in PCI/HIPAA/SOC2 scope.
  • Numbers to quote: 1 RTT handshake, 1–2 ms RSA op, 90-day ACM rotation, 1-hour SPIFFE SVID.
