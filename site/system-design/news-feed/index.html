📰 Design a News Feed (Twitter / Facebook Timeline)

Show each user a fresh, ranked stream of posts from the people they follow — at hundreds of thousands of reads per second. The classic test of the fan-out trade-off and the celebrity problem.

🎯 Real-world scenario

What we're building & where it's used

A news feed (a.k.a. timeline) is the scrolling, reverse-chronological-or-ranked list of posts you see when you open Twitter/X, Facebook, Instagram, LinkedIn, or Reddit. The system takes the content published by everyone a user follows and assembles a personalised, ordered stream — fast enough that the feed is already waiting the instant the app opens. It's a top-tier FAANG interview question because it forces you to confront the central tension of social systems head-on: do you do the expensive work when a post is written, or when a feed is read?

It's deceptively simple to state ("show me posts from people I follow, newest first") but every real product hits the same walls: the volume is brutally read-heavy (people scroll far more than they post), a single celebrity post must reach tens of millions of followers, and the feed must feel instant. Interviewers love it because the obvious first design (query everyone you follow at read time) collapses at scale, and the "correct" design (push posts into precomputed timelines) collapses for celebrities — so you're forced into a hybrid and have to defend the boundary.

Say this up front: "This is a read-heavy feed-generation problem. The core decision is fan-out-on-write versus fan-out-on-read, and the right answer at scale is a hybrid that pushes for normal users and pulls for celebrities."

🧑‍🎓 In laymen terms

The personal magazine already waiting on your doormat

Imagine every morning a magazine lands on your doormat, and it's a personal magazine — put together just for you. It only contains pages from the friends, family, celebrities, and shops you actually care about. Your neighbour, who follows different people, gets a completely different magazine. When you open Instagram or Twitter or Facebook, this is exactly what you're doing — picking up your personal, freshly-assembled magazine.

Now here's the puzzle. You have hundreds of millions of readers, and each of them wants their magazine to be ready the instant they touch the app. If we tried to assemble each magazine on the spot — running out to every friend's house, checking what they posted, and stapling it together in that one second — the whole thing would freeze. So the clever people running the app do the work earlier. Every time your friend posts a photo, a little worker in the background quietly slides a copy into every magazine that follows her. Your feed isn't built when you open the app — it was pre-assembled bit by bit through the day.

But here's the twist. What if your friend is Cristiano Ronaldo, with 600 million followers? Sliding a copy of his photo into 600 million magazines takes hours. So the trick is: for a normal person like your school friend, we push their post into everyone's magazine right away. For a giant celebrity, we do the opposite — we leave the post sitting on their doorstep, and when you open your app, we quickly run and grab the celebrity's latest before showing you the page. Push for normal people, pull for the celebrities. Nobody waits.

And if you haven't opened Instagram for six months? They stop building your magazine altogether until you come back. That's how a few servers keep half a billion people happy.

📋 Requirements & scale

Requirements and back-of-the-envelope math

Functional requirements

  • Publish a post — a user creates a post; it must reach all their followers' feeds.
  • Read the feed — return a paginated, ordered list of posts from the people a user follows.
  • Follow / unfollow users (defines the social graph that drives the feed).
  • Optional: ranking (relevance, not just time), media, likes/comments counts.

Non-functional requirements

  • Low latency on read — the feed should render in well under a second (target ~200 ms server-side); this is the experience that matters most.
  • High availability — feeds should always load; eventual consistency is acceptable (a post arriving a few seconds late is fine).
  • Massive read scale — reads dominate writes by a large factor.
  • Eventual consistency & durability — never lose a published post, but it can propagate asynchronously.

🏗️ High-level architecture

How the pieces fit together

There are two paths, and the whole interview hinges on separating them. On the write path, a user publishes a post: it's written durably to the Post store, then a Fan-out Service (driven by a message queue) looks up the author's followers and pushes the post id into each follower's precomputed home-timeline cache. On the read path, opening the app is cheap: the Feed Service reads the user's ready-made timeline (a list of post ids) straight from Redis in roughly O(1), then "hydrates" those ids into full posts via the Post and User services. Because the heavy lifting happened asynchronously at write time, the read is fast.

The exception is the celebrity: pushing one post to 50 million timelines is hugely expensive and slow. So for high-follower accounts the system skips fan-out and instead pulls their recent posts at read time and merges them into the requesting user's feed. That hybrid — push for the masses, pull for celebrities — is the design's centrepiece.

Walk it verbally: "Publish → durable Post DB → event on a queue → Fan-out Service expands the follower list and writes the post id into each follower's Redis timeline. On read, the Feed Service grabs the precomputed id list from Redis, pulls in celebrity posts separately, merges and ranks, hydrates the ids into full posts, and returns a page. The queue decouples the slow fan-out from the fast publish acknowledgement."

🔍 Component deep-dive

1. Fan-out on write (push model) — the default

When a user posts, the Fan-out Service looks up their followers and inserts the new post id at the head of each follower's home-timeline list in Redis. Reads then become trivial: the timeline is already built. The cost is paid on write and it's an O(number-of-followers) operation — 1,000 followers means 1,000 cache writes. This is ideal because most users have modest follower counts and reads vastly outnumber writes, so doing work once on write saves it on millions of reads.

2. Fan-out on read (pull model) — for celebrities

For an account with tens of millions of followers, pushing one post to every timeline would generate tens of millions of writes and can take minutes to complete — unacceptable. So we don't fan out celebrity posts at all. Instead, at read time, the Feed Service fetches the recent posts of the (few) celebrities a user follows and merges them into the feed. This shifts a little work to the read path but avoids the write explosion.

3. The hybrid (what you actually propose)

Combine them: push for normal users, pull for celebrities, using a follower-count threshold (e.g. > ~1M followers, tunable). A user's final feed = their precomputed pushed timeline ∪ recent posts pulled from the celebrities they follow, then merged and ranked. This is the answer FAANG interviewers are listening for.

4. Timeline cache & the inactive-user optimisation

Home timelines live in a Redis cache as lists of post ids (cheap: ids, not full posts), capped at a few hundred entries per user. To save memory, only active users' timelines are kept warm; if someone has been inactive for, say, 30+ days, their timeline is dropped and lazily rebuilt from the database on next login by querying who they follow. This stops you from fanning out to tens of millions of dormant accounts.

5. Ranking

Pure reverse-chronological is the simple version. Real feeds rank by relevance — recency, affinity to the author, engagement (likes/comments velocity), and media type — typically scored by an ML model at read time over the candidate set. Mention it as an extension; keep the core design chrono + pluggable ranker.

🧮 The DSA behind it

Data structures & algorithms

  • Fan-out (scatter): expanding one post to N follower timelines — the defining operation; O(N) writes, done async on a queue.
  • k-way merge with a min/max-heap: merging the pushed timeline with several celebrity post-streams into one ordered feed is exactly a k-way merge of sorted lists — a heap keyed on timestamp/score pops the next item in O(log k).
  • Redis list / sorted set (ZSET): the home timeline is a capped list of post ids, or a sorted set scored by timestamp/rank for O(log n) insert and O(1) head reads.
  • Hash maps: the follower/following graph and the id→post hydration are hash lookups (O(1) average).
  • Cursor / keyset pagination: "load more" uses a cursor (last post id/score), not OFFSET, so paging stays O(page) regardless of depth.

Deployment story: CloudFront/Route 53 → API Gateway → Fargate services. A publish writes to DynamoDB and drops an event on Kinesis; Fan-out consumers read the follower adjacency list from DynamoDB and write post ids into ElastiCache Redis timelines. On read, the Feed Service reads the Redis timeline, pulls celebrity posts from DynamoDB/Redis, k-way-merges, hydrates post bodies (DynamoDB, media via S3/CloudFront), and returns a cursor-paginated page. Inactive timelines are evicted and rebuilt on demand.

💻 C# implementation

The heart of the system is the hybrid fan-out on publish and the merge on read. Below: publish pushes to followers' Redis timelines (skipping celebrities), and read merges the pushed timeline with pulled celebrity posts via a heap.

const int CelebrityThreshold = 1_000_000;   // followers above this = pull, not push

// ---- WRITE PATH: publish + hybrid fan-out (runs async off a queue) ----
public async Task PublishAsync(Post post)
{
    await _postStore.SaveAsync(post);              // 1) durable write first

    int followers = await _graph.FollowerCountAsync(post.AuthorId);
    if (followers >= CelebrityThreshold)
        return;                                  // 2) CELEBRITY → don't fan out; pulled at read time

    // 3) normal user → push post id into each follower's timeline (fan-out on write)
    await foreach (var batch in _graph.GetFollowersAsync(post.AuthorId))
        await _timelineCache.PushToTimelinesAsync(batch, post.Id, post.CreatedAtUnix);
}

// ---- READ PATH: pushed timeline ∪ pulled celebrity posts, merged & ranked ----
public async Task<List<Post>> GetFeedAsync(long userId, int limit = 50)
{
    // a) precomputed timeline (push) — O(1) head read of post ids from Redis
    List<PostRef> pushed = await _timelineCache.GetTimelineAsync(userId, limit);

    // b) celebrities this user follows → pull their recent posts (pull)
    var celebs = await _graph.GetFollowedCelebritiesAsync(userId);
    var pulled = await _postStore.GetRecentByAuthorsAsync(celebs, limit);

    // c) k-way merge by score (timestamp/relevance) using a max-heap
    var heap = new PriorityQueue<PostRef, long>();   // key = -score → max-heap
    foreach (var r in pushed.Concat(pulled))
        heap.Enqueue(r, -r.Score);

    var top = new List<PostRef>(limit);
    while (heap.Count > 0 && top.Count < limit)
        top.Add(heap.Dequeue());

    // d) hydrate ids → full posts in one batched multiget
    return await _postStore.HydrateAsync(top.Select(r => r.Id));
}

Other tools / languages: the same design ships in Java/Spring or Go; the timeline cache could be Redis ZSETs; the queue could be Kafka; the post store Cassandra (Twitter's real choice) instead of DynamoDB.

⚖️ Trade-offs, bottlenecks & likely follow-ups

The central trade-off: fan-out-on-write makes reads cheap and writes expensive (great because reads dominate), while fan-out-on-read makes writes cheap and reads expensive. The hybrid picks per-user by follower count. The main bottleneck is the Fan-out Service under celebrity posts and "thundering herd" events; the queue + the pull-for-celebrities rule are what tame it.

Consistency vs availability: feeds are eventually consistent (AP) — a post may take a few seconds to appear in all timelines, which is fine. Never block a publish on full fan-out; acknowledge the write after the durable store + queue enqueue.

Q: Push or pull — which and why?
A: Hybrid. Push (fan-out on write) for normal users so reads are O(1) cache hits; pull (fan-out on read) for celebrities to avoid tens of millions of writes per post. Threshold on follower count.
Q: How do you handle a celebrity with 100M followers?
A: Don't fan out their posts at all. Fetch their recent posts at read time and merge into each requesting feed; cache those hot posts aggressively since many users want them.
Q: A user follows 5,000 people — how is the read still fast?
A: Their timeline is precomputed in Redis, so the read is a single capped list fetch, not 5,000 queries. Only the handful of celebrities they follow are merged in at read time.
Q: Why keep only post ids in the timeline cache?
A: Memory. Ids are tiny; storing full posts ×N timelines would explode RAM. Hydrate ids to bodies in a batched multiget at read time.
Q: What about inactive users?
A: Don't fan out to them. Evict dormant (e.g. 30-day) timelines and rebuild lazily from the DB on next login.
Q: New follow — do you backfill?
A: Optionally merge the newly-followed user's recent posts into the timeline (a small pull), rather than rebuilding everything.

🗣️ Interview cheat sheet

30-second pitch: "A news feed is a read-heavy feed-generation problem. I precompute each user's home timeline by fanning out posts on write into a Redis list of post ids, so reads are O(1) cache hits. For celebrities, fanning out to tens of millions is too expensive, so I don't push — I pull their recent posts at read time and k-way-merge them into the feed. That hybrid, plus an async fan-out queue, eviction of inactive timelines, and cursor pagination, gives instant feeds at ~100k reads/sec while keeping writes sane. It's eventually consistent (AP) — a post can land a few seconds late."
