🎯 Real-world scenario

What we're building & where it's used

A web crawler — sometimes called a spider or a bot — is the piece of software that walks the web, downloads pages, extracts the links inside them, and then walks those links too, recursively. Every big search engine has one: Google runs Googlebot, Microsoft runs Bingbot, and the Internet Archive runs the open-source Heritrix. Beyond search, crawlers power price-comparison sites, SEO auditors, LLM pre-training pipelines (Common Crawl is the internet's biggest public crawl), and enterprise site-search over your own intranet.

Interviewers love this problem because it's a Rorschach test for distributed-systems intuition. It sounds like a homework assignment ("just download HTML and parse the links"), but at real scale it exposes every hard problem in the field: queue design, deduplication at the URL and content level, rate limiting and politeness, DNS bottlenecks, fault tolerance under partial failure, crawl-trap detection, ordering under a fixed bandwidth budget, and freshness re-crawl scheduling. A senior candidate should be able to talk through crawling 10 billion pages in under 5 days and defend every number.

The reference bar in real interviews — set by Meta/Uber/Airbnb-style loops — is: (1) fetch scale, (2) URL frontier queueing, (3) politeness and robots.txt, (4) content dedup, (5) crawler-trap defense, (6) freshness re-crawling. Miss any of those six and it's a hire-fail on this round. Get all six, plus the freshness/priority follow-up, and you've cleared staff-level bar.

🧑‍🎓 In laymen terms

Explaining it to someone who's never heard of Googlebot

Imagine you want to make a giant phone book of the entire internet. Not a hand-picked "best websites" list — every page anyone has ever put online. How would you even start?

You'd start with a handful of well-known websites (say, Wikipedia and BBC) and write down their addresses. Call these your starter list. Now you'd visit the first address, print out the page, and — importantly — write down every link on that page. Then you'd visit each of those links and do the same thing again. And again. This tireless address-collecting robot is what engineers call a crawler.

Now, here's where it stops being simple. There are billions of pages on the internet, and most of them keep changing. If one robot did this alone, it would take decades. So you hire thousands of robots — like a small army of postmen — and give each one a slice of the address list. But now new problems appear.

First: don't be rude. If a hundred of your robots all knock on Wikipedia's door at the same second, Wikipedia crashes. So the robots have to be polite — one visit per second per website, and they always read the "please don't come in here" sign every website posts (this is a real file called robots.txt).

Second: don't waste time. The same page can appear at ten different addresses (example.com vs www.example.com vs example.com/index.html). You need a giant checklist so you don't crawl the same page twice.

Third: watch for traps. Some pages generate infinite links (imagine a calendar with "next month" buttons going forward forever). Robots can get stuck there.

The tiny example. Your seed URL is bbc.com. Robot A downloads it, sees links to bbc.com/news and bbc.com/sport, and drops those into a shared to-do list. Robot B picks up bbc.com/news, downloads it, finds more links, adds those to the list. Multiply by ten thousand robots. In four days, you've made your phone book. Then you have to start over, because the internet has already changed.

Think of it as: an army of postmen delivering thank-you cards to every address they've ever heard of, then writing down the addresses on the mailboxes they pass, forever.

📋 Requirements & scale

Requirements and back-of-the-envelope math

Functional requirements (what it does)

  • Take a set of seed URLs and crawl reachable pages, following links to a bounded depth.
  • Store the raw HTML and extracted text for each page.
  • Deduplicate at both URL level (same address twice) and content level (near-duplicate pages).
  • Support re-crawling on a schedule so the index stays fresh.

Non-functional requirements (how well)

  • Politeness: respect robots.txt and never hit one domain faster than ~1 req/sec.
  • Scale: crawl 1010 pages in ≤ 5 days.
  • Fault tolerance: crash of any worker must not lose URLs or double-write content.
  • Extensibility: parser must be swappable — HTML today, JavaScript-rendered pages tomorrow.

🏗️ High-level architecture

How the pieces fit together

A production crawler is a pipeline of stateless workers connected by durable queues, plus a set of shared metadata stores. The stages don't share memory — they hand work off through Kafka/SQS so any one worker can crash and its message is redelivered. The pipeline: seed URLs are pushed into the URL Frontier; Fetcher workers pull URLs, honor robots.txt and per-domain rate limits, download HTML, and store it in blob storage; a Parser stage extracts text and outbound links; extracted URLs are normalized, deduplicated, and filtered, then pushed back into the frontier; a Scheduler occasionally re-injects known URLs for freshness. The Metadata DB tracks per-URL state (queued/fetched/failed), per-page content hash, and per-domain robots.txt policy plus last-fetched timestamps.

Three things to note about this diagram in an interview: (1) the pipeline is looped — parsed URLs re-enter the frontier, which is what makes it a crawler and not a scraper. (2) The Metadata DB and Blob store are the only stateful things; every worker is stateless and horizontally scalable. (3) The DNS resolver has its own dashed line because it's the single stealthiest bottleneck in the whole system — the Mercator paper measured it at up to 70% of per-thread time.

🔍 Component deep-dive

1) URL Frontier — the priority queue of the internet

The frontier is where every design decision comes to a head. It has three simultaneous jobs: (a) hold billions of pending URLs durably (so a worker crash doesn't lose them), (b) enforce per-domain politeness (never let two workers hit bbc.com at the same second), and (c) enforce a priority order so important pages are crawled first. The classic Mercator design uses two queues: a front queue for priority (high-PageRank pages first) and a back queue for politeness (one queue per host, so a fetcher's next URL is guaranteed to be on a different host from the last one). In practice today, teams use Kafka partitioned by domain or SQS with per-domain FIFO groups as the durable substrate, plus a Redis-backed rate limiter in front of the fetcher.

2) Fetcher — the network-bound heart

Each fetcher is an async HTTP client that pulls a URL from the frontier, does a per-domain rate check, resolves DNS (from cache), reads the cached robots.txt, downloads with a size cap (HTTP HEAD first for large-file probing), and stores the raw response in S3. The two things that matter here: concurrency model (Rust/Go/C# async on ~10 k in-flight connections per box; not thread-per-request) and DNS caching. Mercator observed DNS lookups eating up to 70% of per-thread time; every real crawler runs an in-process resolver with a per-domain TTL and rotates across multiple upstream DNS providers (Google 8.8.8.8, Cloudflare 1.1.1.1, own recursive resolver).

3) robots.txt and politeness — don't be the reason your IP gets banned

The robots exclusion protocol is a plaintext file at /robots.txt that says which paths a bot may fetch. Politeness has two layers: (a) parse and respect Disallow plus Crawl-delay, cached in the Metadata DB with a 24-hour TTL; (b) enforce a per-domain rate limit using a Redis SETNX lock or a token-bucket keyed by host — even in the absence of a Crawl-delay directive, 1 req/sec is the sensible default. Skip either and (in order of severity) you get IP-banned, blacklisted by CDNs, or sued.

4) Parser + URL normalization

The parser extracts text (for the search index), and every <a href>, <link>, and <iframe> URL. Then it normalizes each URL — lowercasing host, resolving ./ and ../, stripping fragments, sorting query params, adding a trailing slash for empty paths. Without normalization, example.com/a?x=1&y=2 and Example.com/a?y=2&x=1 would look like different URLs and both get crawled.

5) Dedup — URL-seen vs content-seen

Two orthogonal dedup layers: URL-seen (have I ever queued this address?) and content-seen (have I already downloaded this content, even at a different address?). URL-seen is a giant Bloom filter (or a Metadata DB primary-key check) — cheap, small, wrong 0.1% of the time. Content-seen at exact-match level is a SHA-256 of the response body. Content-seen at near-duplicate level uses SimHash or MinHash — you compute a 64-bit fingerprint and consider two pages duplicate if their Hamming distance is ≤ 3. Near-dup detection is essential: mirror sites, print-friendly copies, and syndicated news articles otherwise get indexed dozens of times.

6) Crawler traps — depth-bounded BFS

Some URLs are infinite — a calendar with "next month" buttons, a session-ID-generating URL, an infinite-scroll paginator. The defense is a hop depth counter stored per URL: depth 0 = seed, and every followed link increments. Cap at 15–20 hops and the trap dies. A second-line defense: reject URLs with pathological query-string cardinality on a per-domain basis (if example.com?sid= keeps generating new URLs, blacklist the parameter).

7) Fault tolerance — the message contract

The pattern is at-least-once with idempotent writes: queue messages carry URL IDs, not raw HTML. If a fetcher dies mid-download, the message's visibility timeout expires and another worker picks it up. The blob store write is keyed by URL ID, so a duplicate delivery just overwrites the same S3 object. After N retries a URL goes to a dead-letter queue for human inspection.

8) Freshness & the re-crawl scheduler

The first crawl builds the index; the re-crawl maintains it. Simple strategy: re-inject each URL into the frontier at an interval proportional to its change frequency (news sites daily, static docs monthly). Better: Cho & Garcia-Molina showed the uniform policy beats the proportional one for average freshness — treat all pages equally rather than trying to be clever, and deprioritize extremely volatile pages. Priority buckets ("news / product / blog / rest") are the practical middle ground.

🧮 The DSA behind it

Data structures & algorithms that make it work

1) Bloom filter for URL-seen

A bit-array of size m and k hash functions. Inserting a URL sets k bits; checking asks "are all k bits set?" — if any is 0 the URL is definitely new. For 10 B URLs at 1% false-positive rate, m ≈ 12 GB (fits in one big Redis or partitioned across a few). The 1% FP means we miss a few new URLs; we never re-crawl a duplicate falsely.

2) SimHash / MinHash for near-dup

SimHash produces a 64-bit fingerprint of a document's shingle set; two fingerprints with Hamming distance ≤ 3 are near-duplicates. Lookup is done with permutation tables — you keep a few sorted copies of the 64-bit fingerprints with different bit permutations, then a candidate match is a range query on each table. O(1)ish per document at a few tens of MB per million.

3) Priority queue for frontier front

A binary heap keyed by priority score (PageRank estimate + freshness deficit). At 23 k pops/sec across 10 B entries, this is partitioned per domain and per priority bucket — Kafka topics with bucketed partitions do this in practice.

4) Trie / suffix automaton for URL filters

To reject URLs by hostname pattern or path prefix (e.g. crawl only *.wikipedia.org), a Trie over reversed hostnames answers "is this in-scope?" in O(k) where k is the hostname length.

5) Consistent hashing for domain-partitioning

Which fetcher handles which domain? Consistent hashing on host(url) gives each fetcher a stable slice; adding/removing a fetcher moves only K/N domains, and per-host rate limits stay coherent because one fetcher owns each host.

A canonical deployment: ECS Fargate service fetcher-svc on c6in hosts consumes an SQS queue partitioned by domain, resolves DNS with a per-container cache, checks Redis for per-domain rate + robots.txt cache, downloads HTML, writes to S3 by URL-ID, and pushes a <URL, S3-key> message to the parser queue. A separate scheduler Lambda re-enqueues URLs based on last-crawl-time. If you can explain that in five sentences on the whiteboard, the AWS section is done.

💻 C# implementation — the fetcher core

The most interviewable part is the fetch loop: it hides the politeness gate, DNS caching, size cap, and normalize/dedup pipeline in idiomatic C#. Below is a self-contained sketch — production code adds retries, DLQ push, and per-domain Redis locks, but this is what you should be able to sketch on a whiteboard.

using System;
using System.Collections.Concurrent;
using System.Net.Http;
using System.Security.Cryptography;
using System.Text;
using System.Threading;
using System.Threading.Tasks;

// 1) A URL work item on the frontier queue — just the ID + normalized URL.
public sealed record CrawlJob(string UrlId, Uri Url, int Depth);

// 2) Ports the fetcher depends on — kept as interfaces so tests can fake them.
public interface IRobotsCache { Task<bool> IsAllowedAsync(Uri u); }
public interface IPolitenessGate { Task<bool> TryAcquireAsync(string host); }
public interface IUrlSeen { Task<bool> SeenBeforeAsync(string url); }
public interface IContentSeen { Task<bool> SeenHashAsync(byte[] sha256); }
public interface IBlobStore { Task PutAsync(string key, byte[] body); }
public interface IFrontier { Task EnqueueAsync(CrawlJob job); }

// 3) The fetch loop — single job, all the guards, idempotent.
public sealed class Fetcher
{
    private readonly HttpClient _http;
    private readonly IRobotsCache _robots;
    private readonly IPolitenessGate _politeness;
    private readonly IUrlSeen _urlSeen;
    private readonly IContentSeen _contentSeen;
    private readonly IBlobStore _blob;
    private readonly IFrontier _frontier;
    private const int MaxDepth = 20;
    private const long MaxBytes = 10_000_000;

    public Fetcher(HttpClient http, IRobotsCache r, IPolitenessGate p,
        IUrlSeen u, IContentSeen c, IBlobStore b, IFrontier f)
    { _http = http; _robots = r; _politeness = p; _urlSeen = u; _contentSeen = c; _blob = b; _frontier = f; }

    public async Task<bool> ProcessAsync(CrawlJob job, CancellationToken ct)
    {
        // (a) crawler-trap defense
        if (job.Depth > MaxDepth) return false;

        // (b) robots.txt gate
        if (!await _robots.IsAllowedAsync(job.Url)) return false;

        // (c) per-domain rate limit — bail and requeue if lock unavailable
        if (!await _politeness.TryAcquireAsync(job.Url.Host))
        {
            await _frontier.EnqueueAsync(job); // deferred retry
            return false;
        }

        // (d) fetch with a size cap — HEAD first to avoid gigantic files
        using var head = await _http.SendAsync(new HttpRequestMessage(HttpMethod.Head, job.Url), ct);
        if (head.Content?.Headers?.ContentLength > MaxBytes) return false;

        using var resp = await _http.GetAsync(job.Url, HttpCompletionOption.ResponseHeadersRead, ct);
        if (!resp.IsSuccessStatusCode) return false;
        var body = await resp.Content.ReadAsByteArrayAsync(ct);
        if (body.LongLength > MaxBytes) return false;

        // (e) exact-content dedup by SHA-256 — near-dup (SimHash) handled downstream
        var sha = SHA256.HashData(body);
        if (await _contentSeen.SeenHashAsync(sha)) return false;

        // (f) durable write — idempotent by UrlId, so redelivery is safe
        await _blob.PutAsync(job.UrlId, body);

        // (g) hand off to Parser stage (via a separate queue in real life)
        // Parser will extract links → normalize → check IUrlSeen → EnqueueAsync new jobs.
        return true;
    }
}

// 4) URL normalization — the tiny code that saves millions of redundant crawls.
public static class UrlNormalizer
{
    public static Uri Normalize(string raw, Uri baseUri)
    {
        var u = new Uri(baseUri, raw.Trim());
        var b = new UriBuilder(u)
        {
            Host = u.Host.ToLowerInvariant(),
            Scheme = u.Scheme.ToLowerInvariant(),
            Fragment = "" // strip #anchor
        };
        // Sort query params for canonical form.
        if (!string.IsNullOrEmpty(b.Query))
        {
            var pairs = System.Web.HttpUtility.ParseQueryString(b.Query);
            var sorted = new StringBuilder();
            foreach (string k in pairs.AllKeys!.OrderBy(x => x))
                sorted.Append(sorted.Length == 0 ? "" : "&").Append(k).Append("=").Append(pairs[k]);
            b.Query = sorted.ToString();
        }
        return b.Uri;
    }
}

Language alternatives. Real crawlers are typically written in Go (Colly), Rust (custom async on Tokio + reqwest), Java (Nutch, Heritrix, StormCrawler), or Python (Scrapy). C#'s HttpClient is a first-class async fit for this workload — the only thing you'd swap in production is HttpClient for something like YARP's HttpMessageInvoker to bypass the connection-pool ceilings.

⚖️ Trade-offs, bottlenecks & likely follow-ups

The bottlenecks in order of pain

(1) DNS. Uncached, DNS is up to 70% of your fetcher's clock. Fix: in-process cache with TTL + multiple upstream resolvers + Route 53 Resolver.

(2) Politeness. The per-domain 1-req/sec limit means a slow domain can't be sped up — throughput scales only if the crawl is spread across many domains. If your seed set is 90% Reddit, you'll be idle waiting for the rate limit.

(3) Bandwidth. At 23 k pages/sec × 2 MB = ~370 Gbps. This is why fetcher hosts are network-optimized instances, not compute-optimized.

(4) Storage & dedup lookup. 12 GB Bloom filter, 5 PB S3 — cheap and easy. The lookup latency (~1 ms Redis, ~5 ms DynamoDB) is what caps QPS per parser worker.

(5) Crawler traps. A single misconfigured site can produce millions of unique URLs per hour and eat your frontier. Depth cap + per-domain URL budget.

Common interviewer follow-ups (with crisp answers)

"How do you keep the crawler polite?"

Two layers: (1) fetch and cache robots.txt per host with a 24h TTL, respect Disallow and Crawl-delay. (2) A per-host token bucket in Redis (SETNX + TTL) enforces a hard 1 req/sec floor across the whole fleet. Add jitter to avoid synchronized retry storms.

"How do you deduplicate?"

Two orthogonal checks. URL-level: a global Bloom filter for "have I ever queued this URL?" — 12 GB for 10 B URLs at 1% FPR. Content-level: SHA-256 for exact match, SimHash (Hamming ≤ 3) for near-dup — catches mirror sites, print-versions, syndicated articles.

"How do you avoid crawler traps?"

A hop-depth counter on each URL (0 at seed, +1 on follow), capped at ~20. Plus a per-domain URL budget: if example.com is generating >100 k unique URLs an hour, blacklist the query parameter that's exploding.

"How do you scale to 10 B pages in 5 days?"

Roughly 23 k pages/sec. On c6in.32xlarge (200 Gbps NIC, ~3.7 k pages/sec sustained at 30% network utilization), ~8 hosts saturate the target. Parser and storage scale independently — Lambda for parsing, S3 for HTML.

"How do you handle JavaScript-rendered pages?"

A separate "heavy fetcher" fleet running headless Chromium (Playwright/Puppeteer). It's ~50× more expensive per page — you route only URLs flagged as JS-heavy (either via a per-site heuristic or from a first-pass fetch that detects <noscript> boilerplate).

"How do you re-crawl for freshness?"

A scheduler re-injects URLs at intervals per priority bucket — news daily, product docs weekly, static content monthly. Cho & Garcia-Molina showed a uniform revisit policy beats a proportional one for average freshness; deprioritize extremely volatile pages.

"What if a fetcher crashes mid-download?"

Messages carry only URL IDs; SQS visibility timeout returns the message to the queue if not ack'd. The blob write is keyed by URL ID, so a redelivery is idempotent. After N failed retries, the URL goes to a DLQ for human inspection.

"How do you prioritize which URLs to fetch first?"

A front-queue priority score — usually a mix of PageRank estimate, freshness deficit, and manual boosts (news, homepages). Mercator's classic design uses a front-queue selector (priority) that feeds a back-queue-per-host (politeness).

"How do you keep the crawl from being blocked?"

Rotate outbound IP pools, respect robots.txt and Crawl-delay, identify honestly via User-Agent, back off on 429/503, and monitor per-host block rates in real time. Malicious crawler spoofing is what gets you blacklisted.

🗣️ Interview cheat sheet

30-second pitch: "It's a pipelined, stateless fleet of workers behind a durable URL frontier. Fetchers pull URLs, honor robots.txt and per-host rate limits, download HTML, and store to S3. Parsers extract text and links, deduplicate by URL Bloom filter and by content SimHash, and push new URLs back to the frontier. Metadata DB tracks per-URL state. To hit 10 B pages in 5 days we need ~23 k pages/sec on ~8 network-optimized instances, and the sneakiest bottleneck is DNS — which we cache aggressively and load-balance across multiple providers."

  • Numbers to quote: 23 k pps target, 2 MB avg page, ~5 PB gzipped, ~12 GB URL Bloom filter, 1 req/sec/host politeness floor, hop-depth 20 for trap defense.
  • The six components: frontier, fetcher, DNS resolver, parser, dedup (URL + content), storage. Always show them as pipeline stages, never as one process.
  • The three sneaky bottlenecks: DNS (70% of thread time uncached), politeness ceiling, crawler traps.
  • The two dedup layers: URL Bloom filter (cheap, false-positive-tolerant) and content SimHash (catches near-duplicates a hash can't).
  • The mental model: stateless workers + durable queues + idempotent writes = crash-safe. Everything stateful (Bloom filter, dedup index, blob store) is externalized.
