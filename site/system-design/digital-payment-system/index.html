🎯 Real-world scenario

What we're building & why it's a top-5 interview question

A digital payment system moves money between two parties — a payer and a payee — over the internet, using a bank card, bank account, or wallet. It must never lose a rupee, never double-charge, produce an audit trail that regulators can accept years later, and answer in under two seconds even when a million people are checking out at the same moment. That's the mandate behind Stripe, PayPal, Square, Razorpay, Adyen, and the giant public infrastructures like UPI (India, 20 billion txns/month), PIX (Brazil), and FedNow (US).

It's asked in interviews because it sits at the crossroads of everything hard about distributed systems: exactly-once semantics in the face of retries, strong consistency against a database that must never drift, regulatory compliance (PCI DSS, PSD2/SCA, RBI, GDPR), real-time fraud detection in the request path, and reconciliation with 30+ external banks whose statements arrive as flat files at 2 AM. A candidate who can explain idempotency keys, double-entry ledgers, the four-party card model, and 3DS in one flow is signalling senior-level thinking.

🧑‍🎓 In laymen terms

Money moves — but no one physically carries it

Imagine you're at a coffee shop. You want to pay ₹200. You tap your phone on the machine and a second later the barista says "done." No cash has moved. No one has run to your bank vault to fetch two hundred-rupee notes. So what actually happened?

Behind that tap, four different companies had a very fast conversation. The coffee shop's app (a "merchant") sent the request to a helper called the payment company — think of it like a translator that speaks the language of every bank. The payment company asked the shop's bank ("hey, we've got a customer paying"). The shop's bank asked the card network — Visa, Mastercard, or in India, UPI — to find your bank. Your bank checked: does this person have ₹200? is this really them? does this look like fraud? If yes, it says "approved." That "approved" travels all the way back — your bank → network → shop's bank → payment company → shop's till. The whole conversation takes about 1.5 seconds.

But your money hasn't actually moved yet. All that happened was a promise: "I promise your account will be debited and the shop's account will be credited." The actual movement of money — called settlement — happens later that night, in a giant batch across all the day's transactions. This two-step design (promise now, move later) is what lets the system be fast and safe. If something goes wrong overnight, they can reverse the promise without any real money having sloshed around.

Why is this hard? Because if your phone loses signal mid-tap and retries, the shop can't charge you twice. Because if a hacker tries the same card 10,000 times a minute, the system must catch them without slowing down real customers. Because at the end of every day, every rupee must add up — the total leaving customers' accounts must equal the total arriving in merchants' accounts, or someone (the payment company) has to eat the difference. And because governments will audit the ledger years later, so nothing can ever be erased. Every mistake is fixed by adding a correction, never by editing what happened before.

Tiny example: Priya pays a Zomato bill of ₹350 with GPay. Her phone sends ₹350 → NPCI (the UPI network) → HDFC (Priya's bank), which asks her for a 4-digit PIN. She types it. HDFC says OK, debits her account, credits Zomato's ICICI account through NPCI. All this in ~1.2 seconds. The next morning, HDFC and ICICI's actual money moves between them via the RBI. Priya never saw any of that — she just saw "Payment successful."

Back-of-the-envelope

At 50k TPS with a 400-byte ledger entry, you're writing 20 MB/s = 1.7 TB/day = 620 TB/year of ledger data. That's why the ledger is on DynamoDB or Aurora, not a single Postgres box. You'll also generate ~4× that in supporting logs (fraud signals, webhook history, audit). At 500 kb of AI-model features per fraud decision, real-time features need a sub-10 ms feature store like DynamoDB Accelerator (DAX) or Redis.

🏗️ High-level architecture

The 4-party (or 6-party) machine, top-down

The classic 4-party card model — cardholder, merchant, acquirer, issuer — is the mental model. In real systems, a fifth actor sits between the merchant and the acquirer: the PSP (Payment Service Provider like Stripe, Razorpay, Adyen) that gives you the API. And a sixth actor is the card network (Visa, Mastercard) that switches between acquirer and issuer. UPI's variant collapses the network into NPCI as the central switch, and the acquirer/issuer are simply "beneficiary bank" and "remitter bank."

The synchronous blue path is what the customer waits on — must complete in under 2 seconds. The green events are async, fired after the ledger commits (outbox pattern) so an integration failure never blocks a payment. The amber dashed path is settlement — the actual movement of funds — which runs overnight in batch and reconciles against files the banks push back.

🔍 Component deep-dive

1. The four-party model (and its offspring)

Every card payment has four roles: the cardholder, the merchant, the merchant's bank called the acquirer, and the cardholder's bank called the issuer. Between the acquirer and the issuer sits the card network — Visa or Mastercard — which is a global switch that routes ISO 8583 messages by BIN (Bank Identification Number, the first 6 digits of the card). Modern PSPs like Stripe technically sit as an additional layer between the merchant and the acquirer, offering one API in exchange for handling the complexity of dealing with 100+ acquirers globally. In UPI's design the network is NPCI, the acquirer/issuer are just banks, and the routing happens by VPA (priya@hdfc) instead of card number. The essence is unchanged: the money-source institution and the money-destination institution never talk directly — they always go through a trusted switch.

2. Idempotency: the single most important primitive

Networks fail. Clients retry. If a POST to /charges for ₹500 gets retried three times because the response was lost, you must not charge ₹1500. Stripe made this a mandatory API contract: every mutating call carries an Idempotency-Key header (typically a UUID tied to the client's cart or session). The PSP stores (key → response) for at least 24 hours; a retry with the same key returns the cached response byte-for-byte, without touching the ledger. This is why Stripe advertises "no double charges" as an API-level guarantee. Internally, the idempotency table is a DynamoDB row with the key as the partition key and a strong-consistency write; even under a race between two retries, DynamoDB's conditional write makes exactly one win.

3. Double-entry ledger — the accounting core

The ledger is append-only. You never UPDATE balance = balance - 500. Instead you INSERT two entries — a debit from one account and a credit to another — whose sum is zero. A single ₹500 payment from Priya to Zomato looks like:

txn_idaccountdirectionamount
tx_9f4ahdfc:priya-checkingDR500.00
tx_9f4aicici:zomato-merchantCR500.00

A refund is not an UPDATE — it's another transaction with the mirror-image legs. A balance is a SUM(credits) - SUM(debits); for performance, you maintain materialized balance snapshots and replay entries after the snapshot version. This design gives you time-travel ("what was Zomato's balance at 3:47 PM yesterday?"), a fraud-proof audit trail, and mathematical reconciliation — every batch of entries must sum to zero, and if it doesn't, something is wrong before you even look at balances. It's how Uber's Ledgerstore, Airbnb's ledger, Square's Cash App, and Modern Treasury's Ledgers API all work under the hood.

4. Auth vs capture vs settlement — three different clocks

These are often conflated but are three distinct events. Authorization is a real-time yes/no from the issuer: "does this cardholder have ₹500 available and is this transaction not fraudulent?" It places a hold on the card but no money moves — the hold expires in 5-30 days if not captured. Capture is the merchant's declaration "I'm shipping the goods, please turn the hold into a real charge." For most e-commerce this happens instantly (auto-capture). For hotels, capture happens at check-out; for pre-orders, capture happens at ship time. Settlement is the actual money movement — nightly, in batch, between acquirer and issuer via the card network's central clearing. Merchants get paid at T+1 or T+2 depending on the country and risk profile. UPI is special: it settles in near-real-time via RBI's IMPS rails, which is a big reason it feels different from cards.

5. 3-D Secure and Strong Customer Authentication

Europe's PSD2 regulation and India's RBI both require SCA — the payer must prove two of "something they know / have / are." 3-D Secure 2 is the technical protocol: the merchant/PSP sends the authorization request enriched with device fingerprints, IP, and transaction context to the issuer, who scores the risk. Low-risk transactions get frictionless flow (approved silently). High-risk ones get challenge flow — a redirect (or in-app popup) that asks the cardholder to authenticate with a bank OTP, biometric, or app confirmation. Once complete, a signed authentication token is returned to the PSP, which appends it to the auth message. Successful 3DS shifts liability from the merchant to the issuer for chargebacks — a huge commercial reason to use it, not just a compliance one.

6. Tokenization and PCI DSS scope

Storing a raw card number (PAN) puts you in PCI DSS Level 1 scope — quarterly audits, network segmentation, penetration testing, expensive. The dodge is tokenization: the moment a card enters the PSP, its PAN is replaced with an opaque token (pm_1Nx…), which is what your systems store. The raw PAN lives only inside a hardened vault (often an HSM-backed service in its own VPC with no outbound internet). Every subsequent operation — charge, refund, save-for-later — is done by referencing the token. This is how Stripe lets merchants "save a card for future use" without those merchants ever touching real card data.

7. Fraud and risk scoring in the request path

The fraud model sits inline with authorization — it must return a decision in under 100 ms so it doesn't blow the 2-second budget. It reads dozens of features: geo distance from prior use, device fingerprint, velocity (how many charges from this IP in the last minute), BIN reputation, order-value distribution, and often 3rd-party signals like address-verification. A gradient-boosted tree or a small NN scores the transaction; above a threshold it triggers 3DS, above another it declines. The feature store is Redis or DynamoDB DAX — sub-millisecond point lookups. Retraining happens offline in S3 → Redshift with feedback labels arriving 30-90 days later (chargeback horizon).

8. Webhooks and the outbox pattern

After the ledger commits, downstream systems need to know: the merchant's dashboard, the fulfillment service, analytics, receipts. You can't have the ledger-write transaction include an HTTP call — networks fail, and you'll roll back a perfectly good payment. The outbox pattern solves this: in the same DB transaction as the ledger writes, insert an event row into an outbox table. A separate poller reads outbox rows and publishes them to Kafka/SNS. Webhook delivery to merchants is at-least-once with exponential backoff, signed with an HMAC so merchants can verify authenticity. Merchants are expected to be idempotent on their side.

9. Reconciliation — where internal reality meets external reality

Every morning, banks and networks push settlement files: BAI2 or MT940 from banks, ISO 20022 messages from newer rails, or CSV from card networks. A reconciliation job parses them and matches each line against the internal ledger by trace_id + amount + date. Matched pairs reduce the "in-transit" clearing account to zero. Unmatched items become exceptions — a missing bank credit, a fee not booked, a chargeback the network filed but you didn't get the webhook for. Ops resolves each with a new balanced ledger entry. This is why "the ledger is append-only and self-checking" isn't a religious principle — it's what makes daily reconciliation a diff, not a data-repair project.

Typical deployment. Everything PCI-in-scope lives in a dedicated VPC with no internet gateway, only VPC endpoints and PrivateLink to the vault. Payment service pods on EKS talk to the ledger via a Data Access Layer that enforces double-entry writes atomically (either PostgreSQL transaction or DynamoDB TransactWriteItems). Outbox events land in MSK; a stream processor fans them to webhook delivery, analytics, and the fraud feature-store updater. Nightly, Glue crawlers ingest settlement files into S3 and Athena queries produce reconciliation reports; exceptions get filed as ledger correction entries. S3 Object Lock keeps every event around for 10 years.

💻 C# implementation — idempotent charge + double-entry ledger

This is the core primitive of a payment API: a POST /charges handler that (1) dedupes by idempotency key, (2) posts a balanced pair of ledger entries in a single transaction, (3) enqueues an outbox event. Real Stripe/Razorpay do exactly this shape, just at higher fanout.

// Program.cs — ASP.NET Core 8. Pseudo-repo interface omitted for brevity.
using Microsoft.EntityFrameworkCore;

public record ChargeRequest(string IdempotencyKey, string PayerAccount,
                              string PayeeAccount,     decimal Amount,
                              string Currency);

public record ChargeResponse(string TxnId, string Status, decimal Amount);

app.MapPost("/v1/charges", async (
    ChargeRequest req,
    LedgerDb db,
    IOutbox outbox,
    HttpContext ctx) =>
{
    // 1. Idempotency check — return cached response on retry.
    var cached = await db.Idempotency.FindAsync(req.IdempotencyKey);
    if (cached != null)
        return Results.Ok(JsonSerializer.Deserialize<ChargeResponse>(cached.Response));

    // 2. Guard rails.
    if (req.Amount <= 0) return Results.BadRequest("amount must be > 0");
    if (req.PayerAccount == req.PayeeAccount)
        return Results.BadRequest("payer == payee");

    // 3. Fraud gate — non-blocking above the 100 ms budget.
    var risk = await _fraud.ScoreAsync(req, ctx.Connection.RemoteIpAddress);
    if (risk.Decision == RiskDecision.Decline)
        return Results.StatusCode(402);

    // 4. Atomic double-entry write + outbox in ONE transaction.
    await using var tx = await db.Database.BeginTransactionAsync(IsolationLevel.Serializable);
    var txnId = $"tx_{Ulid.NewUlid()}";

    db.Entries.AddRange(
        new LedgerEntry { TxnId = txnId, Account = req.PayerAccount,
                            Direction = Dir.Debit,  Amount = req.Amount,
                            Currency = req.Currency, CreatedAt = DateTime.UtcNow },
        new LedgerEntry { TxnId = txnId, Account = req.PayeeAccount,
                            Direction = Dir.Credit, Amount = req.Amount,
                            Currency = req.Currency, CreatedAt = DateTime.UtcNow }
    );

    // Outbox: same transaction as ledger, so an event is published iff the ledger commits.
    db.Outbox.Add(new OutboxEvent {
        EventId = Guid.NewGuid(),
        Type    = "charge.succeeded",
        Payload = JsonSerializer.Serialize(new { txnId, req.Amount, req.Currency })
    });

    var response = new ChargeResponse(txnId, "succeeded", req.Amount);

    // Persist idempotency-key → response so retries return the same result byte-for-byte.
    db.Idempotency.Add(new IdempotencyRecord {
        Key = req.IdempotencyKey,
        Response = JsonSerializer.Serialize(response),
        ExpiresAt = DateTime.UtcNow.AddHours(24)
    });

    await db.SaveChangesAsync();
    await tx.CommitAsync();

    return Results.Ok(response);
});

Notes on why this is correct: (a) The idempotency check is the first thing — before validation, before fraud scoring — so retries are as cheap as possible. (b) The ledger uses Serializable isolation, which is the only level that guarantees no lost updates on concurrent writes to the same account. (c) The outbox insert lives in the same DB transaction as the ledger, so events are published if-and-only-if the ledger commits (this is the Transactional Outbox pattern). (d) The response is serialized into the idempotency row, so a retry returns the exact same JSON, including the same txnId. Real production code adds: currency conversion, tenant isolation, fee splits (interchange, PSP fee, GST), and a "charge status machine" (requires_capture → captured → refunded → disputed).

⚖️ Trade-offs, bottlenecks & likely follow-ups

Where it actually breaks

Hot merchant accounts — a big-box retailer during Black Friday concentrates writes on one ledger partition. Fix: shard by merchant_id + hour and sum sub-balances asynchronously, or use per-shard append with a periodic roll-up. Slow issuer — one bank's auth endpoint goes to 5-second latency and takes down your whole checkout. Fix: circuit breakers with per-issuer budgets, retry with a backup route, degrade gracefully to "manual review." Reconciliation drift — bank fee changes silently and your ledger drifts by paise per transaction until someone notices at month-end. Fix: daily reconciliation is non-negotiable; a tolerance of ₹0.00 with rollup diff alarms.

Consistency & the CAP question

Payments are one of the few domains where you cannot trade consistency for availability. If your ledger disagrees with itself across replicas, you have created or destroyed money. Design accordingly: primary ledger on a strongly-consistent store (Aurora with sync replicas, or DynamoDB with strongly-consistent reads); the fraud store, feature store, and analytics can be eventually consistent because a stale fraud feature is only slightly worse, but a stale balance is catastrophic.

Interviewer follow-ups (with crisp answers)

Q. How do you guarantee exactly-once payment processing?

A. You don't at the network level; TCP retransmits and app-level retries are unavoidable. Instead you guarantee it at the application layer with an idempotency key on every mutating call and an idempotency table that has a unique constraint on the key. First write wins; retries return the cached response. The ledger itself is idempotent by virtue of the same key being written inside the same transaction.

Q. Why not just update a balance column?

A. Because a balance is a derived quantity. Storing it as ground truth means a bug can silently corrupt it and you can never reconstruct the truth. Append-only entries give you a self-auditing record, mathematical invariants (sum of DR = sum of CR), and the ability to reconstruct any historical balance for disputes or regulator queries. Balance snapshots are a performance optimisation, not the source of truth.

Q. What's the difference between authorization and capture?

A. Authorization is a real-time "yes, this cardholder has funds and this isn't obviously fraud" — it places a hold, no money moves. Capture is "please turn this hold into a real charge," which triggers the merchant getting paid in the next settlement batch. Some flows auto-capture (e-commerce), some delay it (hotels, marketplaces).

Q. Why do refunds go back the same way, not the other way?

A. Because the card network only knows how to route money in one direction — merchant → cardholder. A refund is technically a "negative capture" traveling the same rails: acquirer → network → issuer, credited to the same card. If the card has expired, most networks now support account-level fallback, but if not, the issuer contacts the cardholder to arrange a manual credit.

Q. How does UPI settle in real-time when cards can't?

A. Cards inherit a batch-clearing legacy from the 1970s. UPI was designed in 2016 on top of RBI's IMPS rails, which are 24×7 real-time gross settlement. So the settlement happens synchronously as part of the transaction, not overnight. This is why UPI's chargeback timelines and fraud model differ meaningfully from card networks.

Q. What's a chargeback vs a refund?

A. A refund is merchant-initiated ("I agree to give the money back"). A chargeback is cardholder-initiated through their issuer ("I dispute this charge as fraudulent or not-as-described"). Chargebacks bypass the merchant entirely; the network claws money back and only then invites the merchant to submit evidence. Losing rate is tracked (Visa allows up to 0.9%) — go over and you get fined or dropped.

Q. How do you handle a network partition mid-transaction?

A. Every payment has a definite issuer response — approved, declined, or timeout. On timeout, the safe default is to reverse locally and mark the transaction "unknown; do not retry automatically." A reconciliation job the next day matches against issuer records to determine truth and posts a correcting entry. The point is: no state stays ambiguous past 24 hours; either the reconciliation confirms it or you create a compensating entry.

🗣️ Interview cheat sheet

30-second pitch: "A payment system moves money between a payer and a payee via a 4-party model — cardholder, merchant, acquirer, issuer — with a PSP fronting the API and a card network switching between banks. The core primitives are idempotency keys so retries never double-charge, an append-only double-entry ledger so every balance is derivable and every batch sums to zero, and separation of authorization (real-time hold) from capture and settlement (nightly batch or real-time in UPI). Fraud sits inline under 100 ms; 3DS shifts chargeback liability; the outbox pattern makes downstream integrations reliable; and daily reconciliation with bank files closes the loop. Everything PCI-scoped is behind a tokenization vault so the rest of the system never touches raw card numbers."

Numbers to quote

  • UPI: 20 billion txns/month (Aug 2025), ~7,500 avg TPS, peak 10× that.
  • Auth latency budget: < 2 s end-to-end, fraud < 100 ms.
  • Idempotency window: ≥ 24 hours.
  • PCI DSS Level 1: > 6M card txns/year.
  • Merchant payout: T+1 / T+2 for cards; near-real-time for UPI.
  • Chargeback threshold: 0.9% (Visa) before penalties.

5 talking points that win the round

  • Name the 4-party model + PSP + network explicitly on the whiteboard. Interviewers love it.
  • Draw the outbox pattern — it separates "ledger is truth" from "webhooks are eventually consistent."
  • Show a ledger table with DR + CR rows summing to zero. This is the single most senior-signal thing you can draw.
  • Distinguish auth / capture / settlement — they run on different clocks and different rails.
  • Call out reconciliation as a first-class subsystem, not an afterthought. It's what makes the system self-healing.
