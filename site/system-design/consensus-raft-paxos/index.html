🎯 Real-world scenario

Getting unreliable machines to agree on one truth

Consensus is the problem of getting a group of machines to agree on a single value — the next command in a replicated log, who the current leader is, whether a lock is held — even though machines crash, restart, and messages are delayed or lost. It's the beating heart of every reliable distributed system: it's how etcd and ZooKeeper store the source-of-truth config that Kubernetes and Kafka depend on, how CockroachDB and Spanner keep replicas of your data identical, and how distributed commit escapes 2PC's blocking flaw by replicating the coordinator.

Interviewers love consensus because it forces you to reason about the hardest part of distributed systems: failure without a global clock. The FLP impossibility result proved that in a fully asynchronous network you cannot guarantee consensus if even one node can fail — so practical algorithms (Paxos, Raft) sidestep it with randomized timeouts and the assumption of eventual synchrony. If you can explain how a cluster elects a leader and commits a log entry with a majority, you've demonstrated the core competency.

🧑‍🎓 In laymen terms

Five friends trying to pick a restaurant on WhatsApp

Picture five friends on a WhatsApp group deciding where to eat tonight. Everyone types at once, everyone has opinions, and — worst of all — some phones are on airplane mode, some people are stuck in traffic, and one friend's battery just died. And yet the group needs to land on exactly one restaurant. Not two. Not "maybe Pizza Hut or maybe KFC." One place, everyone shows up there. That is the essence of the problem computers face when a group of servers has to agree on something.

The clever trick used by real systems is: elect a captain. One friend — usually whoever spoke up first — becomes the captain for tonight. From now on, only the captain proposes restaurants. Anyone else who has an idea whispers it to the captain, and the captain broadcasts it to the group. This stops the chaos of everyone shouting at once.

When the captain suggests "let's do Pizza Hut," they don't just declare it. They wait until more than half the group replies "yes, sounds good." Three yeses out of five is enough. Once they hit that majority, it's locked in — even if the two silent friends were on airplane mode, they'll see the decision the moment their phones reconnect, and they must go along with it. This "more than half" rule is why the group survives even when a couple of phones are dead.

What if the captain's own battery dies? The group waits a few seconds — no captain message means "he's gone" — and then they hold a quick vote for a new captain. Whoever most people vote for takes over. That's how systems like the one behind Google Docs, or the config store powering Kubernetes, keep working even when servers randomly crash. There is always one captain, majority approval, and instant re-election when the captain vanishes.

📋 What a consensus algorithm must guarantee

The safety and liveness properties

PropertyMeaning
Agreement (safety)No two correct nodes decide different values. If node A applies command X as the 5th log entry, no node ever applies a different 5th entry.
Validity / IntegrityThe value decided was actually proposed by some node — no values invented out of thin air.
Termination (liveness)Every correct node eventually decides — as long as a majority is up and can communicate.
Fault toleranceTolerates f crash failures with 2f + 1 nodes (a majority must survive). Byzantine variants tolerate f malicious nodes with 3f + 1.

The magic number is the majority quorum. A 5-node cluster survives 2 failures (needs 3 to agree); a 3-node cluster survives 1. If it can't reach a majority, a correct system halts rather than returning a wrong answer — "it will never return an incorrect result." That's the consistency-over-availability (CP) posture from the CAP theorem.

🏗️ Raft — consensus you can actually understand

Raft: leader-based replicated log

Raft was designed for one goal Paxos famously lacked: understandability. It's "equivalent to Paxos in fault-tolerance and performance" but decomposes the problem into three digestible subproblems — leader election, log replication, and safety. Every server is in one of three states: Follower, Candidate, or Leader. Time is divided into terms — monotonically increasing integers that act as a logical clock; each term has at most one leader.

state transitions

Leader election

Followers expect periodic heartbeats from the leader. If a follower hears nothing before its randomized election timeout (e.g. 150–300 ms) fires, it becomes a Candidate, increments the term, votes for itself, and sends RequestVote RPCs to all peers. A server grants its vote at most once per term, and only to a candidate whose log is at least as up-to-date as its own. If the candidate collects votes from a majority, it becomes Leader and immediately sends heartbeats to suppress other elections. The randomized timeouts make split votes rare and self-correcting — that's Raft's elegant trick against FLP.

Log replication

All client writes go to the leader. It appends the command to its log and sends AppendEntries RPCs to followers. Once a majority has persisted the entry, the leader marks it committed, applies it to its state machine, and returns success to the client; followers apply it too as they learn the commit index. Because a leader only commits entries from its current term (and only after a majority has them), Raft guarantees the committed prefix of every log is identical everywhere — "no other state machine will ever apply a different nth command."

🔍 Deep-dive: why it's safe

Terms are a logical clock

Every RPC carries the sender's term. If a node ever sees a term higher than its own, it immediately reverts to Follower and adopts that term. This single rule cleanly resolves stale leaders: a leader that was network-partitioned and comes back with an old term is instantly demoted the moment it sees a newer one, preventing two active leaders (split-brain) from both committing.

The commit rule and log matching

An entry is committed only when stored on a majority of servers. The Log Matching Property guarantees that if two logs contain an entry with the same index and term, the logs are identical in all preceding entries. The leader forces followers to reconcile by walking back until logs agree, then overwriting the follower's conflicting suffix. This is how a follower that missed entries (or has stale ones from a dead leader) gets repaired.

The partition scenario (the interview favorite)

Split a 5-node cluster into a 3-node side and a 2-node side. The 3-node side has a majority: it can elect a leader and keep committing. The 2-node side cannot form a majority — its leader (if it had one) steps down and it stops accepting writes. When the partition heals, the minority side sees the higher term, reverts to followers, and its state machine catches up from the leader's log. No divergence, no data loss for committed entries — availability was sacrificed on the minority side (CP).

5 nodes, partition 3 | 2: [ N1 N2 N3 ] ← majority (3) → elects leader, COMMITS ✓ ‖ partition ‖ [ N4 N5 ] ← minority (2) → no quorum, STALLS ✗ heal → N4,N5 see higher term → become followers → catch up from log
🧮 Paxos, quorums & the math

Paxos, and the theory underneath

Paxos (Lamport, 1998) is the original crash-fault-tolerant consensus algorithm and is provably correct, but notoriously hard to understand and to implement completely — real systems use Multi-Paxos to amortize the cost across a stream of values. Its single-decree form runs two phases with roles Proposer, Acceptor, Learner:

PhasePaxos stepPurpose
1a/1b (Prepare/Promise)Proposer picks a ballot number n, asks acceptors to promise not to accept anything lower; they reply with any value already accepted.Establish leadership for ballot n and learn prior values.
2a/2b (Accept/Accepted)Proposer sends accept(n, v); acceptors accept if they haven't promised a higher ballot.A value chosen by a majority is permanent.

Raft is essentially a Multi-Paxos with a strong leader and a more restrictive log structure, chosen precisely to be teachable. Both rely on the same foundational idea: any two majorities of a set overlap in at least one member. That overlapping node "remembers" the previously chosen value, which is what makes it impossible for two different values to both win.

💻 C# implementation — Raft leader election core

This captures the heart of Raft election: term handling, the "grant vote at most once per term to an up-to-date log" rule, and stepping down on a higher term. It's deliberately trimmed to the logic an interviewer wants to see, not a production RPC stack.

enum Role { Follower, Candidate, Leader }

sealed class RaftNode
{
    public long CurrentTerm;
    public int? VotedFor;          // candidate id we voted for THIS term (once)
    public Role Role = Role.Follower;
    public readonly int Id;
    public List<LogEntry> Log = new();

    // --- Handle an incoming RequestVote RPC from a candidate ---
    public VoteReply OnRequestVote(VoteRequest req)
    {
        // Rule 1: a newer term always wins — adopt it and step down.
        if (req.Term > CurrentTerm)
        {
            CurrentTerm = req.Term;
            VotedFor    = null;
            Role        = Role.Follower;
        }

        // Reject stale-term candidates outright.
        if (req.Term < CurrentTerm)
            return new VoteReply(CurrentTerm, granted: false);

        // Grant only if we haven't voted this term AND the candidate's log is
        // at least as up-to-date as ours (compare last term, then last index).
        bool free   = VotedFor is null || VotedFor == req.CandidateId;
        bool upToDate = CandidateLogIsCurrent(req.LastLogTerm, req.LastLogIndex);

        if (free && upToDate)
        {
            VotedFor = req.CandidateId;              // one vote per term
            return new VoteReply(CurrentTerm, granted: true);
        }
        return new VoteReply(CurrentTerm, granted: false);
    }

    // Candidate wins iff it collects votes from a MAJORITY of the cluster.
    public bool HasQuorum(int votes, int clusterSize) => votes > clusterSize / 2;

    bool CandidateLogIsCurrent(long lastTerm, int lastIdx)
    {
        var myLastTerm = Log.Count > 0 ? Log[^1].Term : 0;
        if (lastTerm != myLastTerm) return lastTerm > myLastTerm;
        return lastIdx >= Log.Count;          // same term ⇒ longer/equal log wins
    }
}

In production you'd never hand-roll this: use a battle-tested library/service — etcd or Consul (Raft) for coordination, ZooKeeper (Zab), or in .NET the dotNext.Net.Cluster Raft implementation. The value of writing it out is being able to explain term handling and the vote rule under interview pressure.

⚖️ Trade-offs, bottlenecks & likely follow-ups

"Why is Raft preferred over Paxos?"

Same fault tolerance and performance, but far more understandable and implementable. Raft has a strong single leader and a clean log structure; basic Paxos is subtle and Multi-Paxos leaves many implementation details unspecified, which historically led to buggy implementations.

"How many nodes should a cluster have?"

An odd number — typically 3 or 5 — spread across AZs. N = 2f+1 tolerates f crashes. Odd is better because 4 nodes tolerate the same 1 failure as 3 but need a bigger quorum, hurting latency and availability.

"What's the throughput bottleneck?"

The leader — all writes funnel through it, and every commit needs a majority round-trip (bounded by the slowest node in the quorum). Scale by sharding into many Raft groups (one per key range), as CockroachDB/Spanner do, rather than one giant group.

"What about FLP impossibility?"

FLP says no deterministic consensus is guaranteed to terminate in a fully async network with one faulty node. Raft/Paxos remain safe always, and achieve liveness by assuming partial synchrony plus randomized timeouts — they can theoretically livelock on repeated split votes, but randomization makes that vanishingly unlikely.

"How do you do a linearizable read?"

Don't read from a follower blindly (it may be stale). Either route reads through the leader with a lease, or have the leader confirm it's still leader via a quorum heartbeat before answering (ReadIndex). Otherwise you can read stale data from a deposed leader.

🗣️ Interview cheat sheet

30-second pitch: "Consensus makes a cluster agree on one value despite crashes and message loss — it's how etcd, ZooKeeper, Kafka, and Spanner stay consistent. Raft, the understandable version of Paxos, splits it into leader election, log replication, and safety. Time is divided into terms with one leader each; a follower whose heartbeat times out becomes a candidate, wins if a majority votes for it, then replicates log entries and commits them once a majority persists them. With 2f+1 nodes you tolerate f failures; the minority side of a partition stalls rather than diverging — that's the CP choice."
  • Three states: Follower → Candidate → Leader. Terms are the logical clock; higher term always wins → step down.
  • Election: randomized timeout → request votes → majority ⇒ leader → heartbeats suppress others.
  • Commit rule: entry committed once a majority stores it; then applied to state machine.
  • N = 2f + 1 for crash faults; 3f + 1 for Byzantine. Use odd sizes across 3 AZs.
  • Raft vs Paxos: same guarantees, Raft wins on understandability & correct implementations.
  • Scale by sharding into many Raft groups; the leader is the throughput bottleneck.
