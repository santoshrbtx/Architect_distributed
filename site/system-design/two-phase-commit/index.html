Two-Phase & Three-Phase Commit

How independent nodes agree to commit one atomic transaction — the classic distributed-commit protocols, their blocking flaw, and what replaced them.

🎯 Real-world scenario

The atomic-commit problem across machines

Imagine transferring $100 from an account in the Payments database to a balance in the Ledger database — two separate systems on two separate machines. You need all-or-nothing: either both the debit and the credit happen, or neither does. If the debit commits and the credit is lost, money vanishes. On a single database an ACID transaction handles this trivially, but the moment the data spans multiple independent nodes there is no shared transaction log to lean on. This is the distributed atomic commit problem, and Two-Phase Commit (2PC) is its textbook solution.

You'll meet 2PC anywhere multiple resource managers must commit together: the XA transaction standard used by Java/JEE app servers and message brokers, distributed SQL engines, and older microservice designs that spanned several databases in one request. Interviewers ask about it because it is the cleanest way to test whether you understand coordination under failure — and because its famous blocking weakness motivates everything that came after (Paxos, Raft, and the Saga pattern).

🧑‍🎓 In laymen terms

The friends who want to swap concert seats — but only if everyone agrees

You and five friends are at a concert, but you all got seated in random spots. You want to swap so you can sit together. The catch — a swap only works if every single one of the five is willing to move, at the same time. If even one person refuses, everybody stays put.

So one of you becomes the "organiser." The organiser walks around the row and asks each friend, one by one, "Are you ready to move if I tell you to?" Each friend thinks about it — packs up their popcorn, folds their jacket, and says either "yes, ready" or "no, my knee hurts, I'm not moving." The organiser writes down every answer on a piece of paper. This is phase one.

Once everyone has said "yes," the organiser walks around a second time and says "OK, move now." Everyone gets up together, swaps seats, and it's done. If even one person had said no, the organiser would instead say "abort, sit back down" — and everyone unpacks their popcorn again. This is phase two. That is basically how banks, airlines, and old-style databases used to make sure two computer systems don't get out of sync when they need to change something together.

Here is the ugly part. Suppose after everyone said "yes," the organiser goes to the bathroom and never comes back. Now the five friends are stuck. They've all promised they will move — they can't sit down, they can't get more popcorn, they can't even leave — because the organiser might return any moment with the "move now" signal. They are frozen, waiting. That is the famous blocking problem: one bad moment for the organiser, and the whole row is paralysed. This is why modern apps like WhatsApp or Uber avoid this dance — they use a gentler pattern called a Saga, where each step can be undone later, so nobody has to freeze and wait.

📋 What atomic commit must guarantee

Requirements of a distributed-commit protocol

PropertyMeaning
AgreementAll participants that decide, decide the same outcome (all commit or all abort). No split decisions.
ValidityIf any participant votes No, the outcome is abort. If all vote Yes and no failures, the outcome is commit.
Stability / DurabilityOnce a node commits or aborts, that decision is final and survives crashes (written to a stable log).
TerminationNon-faulty nodes eventually reach a decision. This is the one 2PC fails to guarantee — hence its blocking problem.

The roles: one coordinator (the transaction manager) drives the decision; multiple participants (also called cohorts or resource managers) each own a slice of the data and vote. Both sides write to a durable log so they can recover their state after a crash.

🏗️ The 2PC protocol

Two phases: vote, then decide

Phase 1 is prepare/voting: the coordinator asks everyone "can you commit?" Each participant does all the work up to the commit point — acquires locks, writes undo/redo log records — and replies Yes (I'm ready and I promise I can commit) or No. Crucially, a Yes vote is a binding promise: the participant must now be able to commit even if it crashes and restarts, so it forces its prepare record to stable storage first. Phase 2 is commit/completion: if all voted Yes, the coordinator writes a commit record and broadcasts COMMIT; if anyone voted No (or timed out), it broadcasts ABORT. Participants act, release their locks, and acknowledge.

phase 1: prepare + vote phase 2: commit broadcast
  1. Prepare: coordinator logs BEGIN, sends CanCommit? to all participants.
  2. Vote: each participant does the work, force-writes a prepared record, replies Yes/No. After Yes it holds locks and waits — it is now in doubt.
  3. Decide: coordinator force-writes the global decision (commit iff all Yes). This write is the point of no return.
  4. Broadcast & ack: coordinator sends the decision; participants apply it, release locks, and acknowledge. Coordinator completes once all acks arrive.

🔍 Failure modes — where 2PC hurts

The blocking problem (the answer they're fishing for): Its greatest disadvantage is that 2PC is a blocking protocol. After a participant votes Yes it is "in doubt" — it holds its locks and cannot unilaterally decide. If the coordinator crashes after collecting votes but before broadcasting the decision, the in-doubt participants must block indefinitely, holding locks, until the coordinator recovers. There is no safe timeout: the participant can't abort (maybe others already committed) and can't commit (maybe others aborted).

Why a participant can't just guess

Suppose P2 voted Yes, then loses contact with both the coordinator and its peers. It cannot ask "did we commit?" If it assumes commit and the real decision was abort, it diverges; if it assumes abort and the decision was commit, it diverges the other way. Safety forces it to wait. This is precisely the tension the CAP theorem predicts: 2PC chooses Consistency over Availability — it would rather freeze than risk divergence, making it a CP protocol.

Other costs

  • Latency & disk: multiple forced log flushes to stable storage and two network round-trips per commit.
  • Locks held across the round-trip hurt throughput and create contention/deadlock risk.
  • Coordinator is a single point of failure unless it is itself replicated (which is what real systems do — replicate the coordinator via Paxos/Raft).
  • Optimizations — presumed abort, presumed commit, tree/dynamic 2PC — cut messages and logging but never remove blocking.
🧮 3PC and the modern alternatives

Three-Phase Commit and what actually replaced 2PC

Three-Phase Commit (3PC) attacks the blocking flaw by inserting a pre-commit phase between voting and committing, and by giving every state a timeout. The extra phase guarantees that before anyone commits, everyone knows the decision will be commit. So if the coordinator dies, a surviving participant can look at its own state and safely time out toward the right outcome: if it reached pre-commit, it commits; if not, it aborts. This makes 3PC non-blocking under a single fail-stop failure.

2PC: [Prepare/Vote] ────────────────▶ [Commit] │ ▲ └── coordinator dies here → IN-DOUBT, BLOCKS ✗ 3PC: [Prepare/Vote] ─▶ [Pre-Commit] ─▶ [Commit] │ ▲ └ everyone knows outcome → timeout is SAFE ✓

The catch — and the reason 3PC is a textbook curiosity rather than a workhorse — is that it assumes synchronous timing and fail-stop crashes. Under network partitions, 3PC can still produce inconsistent decisions: two sides can time out toward different outcomes. It also adds a whole extra round-trip, worsening latency. Real systems therefore skipped 3PC and went to consensus.

The two winners you must name

Consensus-backed commit: instead of one fragile coordinator, replicate the commit decision through Raft/Paxos so a majority agreeing is the decision — the coordinator can fail and a new one takes over from the replicated log. Google Spanner and CockroachDB run 2PC on top of Paxos/Raft groups, getting atomic commit without the single-coordinator block. See the consensus page.

Saga pattern: for microservices, avoid distributed locks entirely. Break the transaction into a sequence of local commits, each with a compensating action to undo it. If step 3 fails, run the compensations for steps 2 and 1 (refund, restock). You trade atomic isolation for availability and eventual consistency — the AP-style answer to the same problem.

The interview-grade takeaway: in a cloud microservices world you almost never run true 2PC across services — you use Step Functions Sagas plus the Outbox pattern and idempotency to get correctness without the blocking coordinator.

💻 C# implementation — a 2PC coordinator

This models the coordinator driving prepare → decide, and shows exactly where a Yes vote makes a participant "in doubt." Note the durable log writes at the decision point — that record is what lets recovery finish the transaction after a crash.

public enum Vote { Yes, No }

public interface IParticipant
{
    // Phase 1: do the work, force-write a 'prepared' record, then vote.
    Task<Vote> PrepareAsync(Guid txId);
    Task CommitAsync(Guid txId);   // phase 2
    Task AbortAsync(Guid txId);    // phase 2
}

public sealed class Coordinator
{
    private readonly IReadOnlyList<IParticipant> _parts;
    private readonly ITxLog _log;   // durable, survives crashes

    public Coordinator(IReadOnlyList<IParticipant> parts, ITxLog log)
        => (_parts, _log) = (parts, log);

    public async Task<bool> RunAsync(Guid txId, TimeSpan voteTimeout)
    {
        await _log.WriteAsync(txId, "BEGIN");

        // ---- PHASE 1: collect votes (with a timeout -> treat as No) ----
        Vote[] votes;
        try
        {
            var tasks = _parts.Select(p => p.PrepareAsync(txId));
            votes = await Task.WhenAll(tasks).WaitAsync(voteTimeout);
        }
        catch (TimeoutException)
        {
            await BroadcastAbort(txId);   // a missing vote is a No
            return false;
        }

        bool commit = votes.All(v => v == Vote.Yes);

        // ---- DECISION: this durable write is the point of no return ----
        await _log.WriteAsync(txId, commit ? "COMMIT" : "ABORT");

        // ---- PHASE 2: broadcast + retry until every participant acks ----
        foreach (var p in _parts)
            await RetryForever(() => commit ? p.CommitAsync(txId) : p.AbortAsync(txId));
        // If the coordinator crashes BEFORE this loop, prepared participants
        // are IN-DOUBT and BLOCK until it recovers and replays the log. <- the flaw.
        return commit;
    }
}

In the real world you rarely hand-roll this: .NET exposes System.Transactions.TransactionScope and MSDTC for XA-style 2PC, Java uses JTA/XA, and distributed databases embed it. For microservices, prefer a Saga library (e.g. MassTransit sagas, or AWS Step Functions) over rolling your own coordinator.

⚖️ Trade-offs, bottlenecks & likely follow-ups

"What is the single biggest problem with 2PC?"

It blocks. If the coordinator fails after participants vote Yes, they're stuck holding locks in an in-doubt state until it recovers — it can't guarantee termination. That's the answer to lead with.

"Does 3PC fully fix it?"

Only under synchronous timing and fail-stop crashes. Under network partitions 3PC can still make inconsistent decisions, and it costs an extra round-trip. That's why production systems use consensus (Raft/Paxos) instead.

"How do real databases get atomic commit without blocking?"

They replicate the coordinator/decision through consensus. Spanner and CockroachDB run 2PC over Paxos/Raft groups, so any single coordinator failure is recovered from the replicated log by a new leader — no permanent block.

"When would you use a Saga instead?"

Across microservices/services with separate databases, where holding distributed locks is unacceptable. Sagas do local commits with compensating transactions and accept eventual consistency — trading isolation for availability.

"Is 2PC consistent or available (CAP)?"

Consistent — it's a CP protocol. It sacrifices availability by blocking rather than risk two participants diverging.

🗣️ Interview cheat sheet

30-second pitch: "Two-Phase Commit gives atomic all-or-nothing commit across nodes. Phase 1 the coordinator asks everyone to prepare and vote; a Yes is a durable promise. Phase 2 it commits iff everyone voted Yes. The fatal flaw is blocking: if the coordinator dies after the votes, prepared participants are in-doubt and freeze holding locks. Three-Phase Commit adds a pre-commit phase to become non-blocking, but breaks under partitions — so real systems use consensus like Raft, or the Saga pattern for microservices."
  • Two phases: Prepare/Vote → Commit/Complete. Yes vote = binding, durable promise.
  • Blocking problem: coordinator crash after votes ⇒ in-doubt participants block, holding locks. No safe timeout.
  • 2PC is CP: prefers consistency, sacrifices availability.
  • 3PC = +pre-commit phase ⇒ non-blocking under fail-stop, but unsafe under partitions.
  • Modern answer: consensus-backed commit (Spanner/CockroachDB on Paxos/Raft) or Sagas (compensating transactions) for microservices.
  • AWS: Step Functions Sagas + transactional Outbox + idempotency, not cross-service 2PC.
