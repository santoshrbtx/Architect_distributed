🎯 Real-world scenario

What we're building & where it's used

We're designing a global user-generated video platform: a creator uploads a large file once, and viewers on every device and every network watch a version that looks great and starts instantly. This is the archetype for YouTube, and closely related to TikTok, Instagram Reels, Vimeo, Netflix (though Netflix is a curated library, not user uploads), Twitch (live streaming variant), and Facebook Watch. The interview version usually asks for on-demand video only — live streaming is a follow-up.

Companies build this because video is the dominant medium of the internet and every large platform eventually needs a media pipeline: a social product wants shortform, an e-commerce site wants product videos, a corporate LMS wants training content. The design touches every heavyweight concept in a distributed systems curriculum: large blob storage, asynchronous processing at massive fan-out, CDN and edge caching, adaptive bitrate streaming, and ranked recommendation at billion-item scale. That's why it's a favorite interview question — a good answer proves you can reason about the entire stack, not just one layer.

🧑‍🎓 In laymen terms

Explained without any tech words

The everyday problem. Imagine you shoot a 15-minute video on your phone — a birthday, a cooking tutorial, whatever. You want to share it, and you want any random person on any device to be able to watch it without waiting, without buffering, and without it looking blurry on their new TV or eating up their mobile data. That is genuinely hard, and it's what YouTube quietly does for you.

The story of one video. When you press "Upload," your phone doesn't try to send the whole video in one go — it slices it into small pieces, like tearing a long letter into pages, and sends each page separately. If your connection drops halfway, only the missing pages get re-sent, not the whole thing. Once all the pages arrive at YouTube's building, an army of "converter workers" takes your original video and makes many copies of it — a small blurry one for old phones, a medium one for laptops, a big sharp one for TVs, all at the same time. Each copy is then chopped into tiny 4- to 6-second clips and stored in a giant warehouse of hard drives spread across many cities.

Where things live. When someone in Tokyo taps play, YouTube doesn't fetch your video from a warehouse in California — that would be slow. Instead, YouTube keeps copies of popular videos in mini-warehouses close to viewers, kind of like how supermarkets keep the popular snacks up front and the odd stuff in a back room. Those little clips arrive one after another, so playback starts in a second or two even if the whole video is an hour long.

The hard part. The tricky bit is that hundreds of millions of people press play every hour, and each person is on a different network, a different device, and speaks a different language. YouTube also has to guess which video you'll enjoy next — otherwise you'd close the app after the first video. So on top of the storage and delivery, there's a giant "recommendation kitchen" running in the background, tasting billions of viewing patterns to serve you a personalized playlist. The magic is that almost none of this happens while you wait — the heavy work was done long before you tapped play.

📋 Requirements & scale

Requirements and back-of-the-envelope math

Functional requirements

  • Upload a video (large file, resumable, chunked).
  • Transcode the source into multiple resolutions (144p → 4K) and codecs (H.264, VP9, AV1) for adaptive playback.
  • Stream to any device with adaptive bitrate (HLS/DASH) so quality follows the network.
  • Search by title, tags, channel.
  • Recommend a personalized "up next" list and homepage feed.
  • Social: likes, comments, subscriptions, views.

Non-functional requirements

  • Availability > strong consistency — a stale view count is fine; a broken play button is not.
  • Low startup latency: playback should begin in < 2 seconds worldwide.
  • Durability of the master copy: ~11 nines. Losing an uploaded video is catastrophic for creators.
  • Global reach: viewers on every continent, on cellular to fiber.
  • Read-heavy: watches vastly outnumber uploads (roughly 100 : 1).

The two dominant costs are storage (durable master + many renditions) and egress bandwidth (billions of hours streamed). That's why the entire design orients around doing the heavy work once, offline, and serving the results from cache close to the viewer.

🏗️ High-level architecture

How the pieces fit together

The whole system splits cleanly into two flows that meet only at the metadata database. The upload/ingest flow is offline and heavy — it moves bytes into durable object storage, generates renditions and thumbnails, extracts metadata, and publishes the video as "ready." The read/watch flow is online and light — the player fetches a manifest, then pulls small segments from the CDN and adapts quality as bandwidth changes. Recommendations run alongside, feeding two-stage ranked lists into the homepage and up-next slots.

Synchronous request flow Asynchronous processing

Two subtle points that separate a good answer from a great one: the master copy in the raw object store is precious — everything else can be regenerated from it, and durability requirements are highest here. And the CDN is not a single layer — there's an edge PoP close to the viewer, a regional mid-tier that fills cold edges without hammering origin, and only then origin storage. This tiering keeps origin egress bandwidth manageable even at YouTube scale.

🔍 Component deep-dive

1. Upload flow — resumable, chunked, direct-to-blob

The client asks the upload service for a signed pre-authorized URL (S3 pre-signed URL or the equivalent) and then uploads directly to object storage, bypassing the application server. Uploads use the tus.io resumable protocol (or S3 multipart): the file is split into ~5–10 MB parts, each part is uploaded independently, and if the connection dies only the missing parts are retried. When all parts land, the client calls completeUpload and the server assembles them into a single object. Two crucial properties: (a) the application tier never becomes a bandwidth bottleneck, and (b) resumability turns "phone died in a tunnel" from a fatal error into a five-second retry. On completion, the service writes a videos row with status=UPLOADED and enqueues a transcode job — the API returns 200 immediately, and everything downstream is asynchronous.

2. Transcoding — the biggest asynchronous fan-out in the stack

A single source has to become many outputs: multiple resolutions (144p, 240p, 360p, 480p, 720p, 1080p, 1440p, 2160p) × multiple codecs (H.264 for compatibility, VP9 and AV1 for bandwidth savings on modern devices) × segmented into 4–6-second chunks × plus a thumbnail sheet, captions, and content-ID fingerprint. To make this tractable at 500 hours-uploaded-per-minute, transcoding is chunk-parallelized: the source is split into scene-cut boundaries or fixed intervals, each interval is transcoded on a different worker (MapReduce-style), then reassembled. YouTube built a custom silicon accelerator called the VCU (Video Coding Unit) that gave them 20-33× the compute efficiency of CPU transcoding — proof of how much this workload dominates infrastructure. In an interview, describe it as "a Kafka topic per resolution, workers pull chunks, and a reducer stitches segments and writes the manifest."

3. Streaming — HLS/DASH adaptive bitrate

Every stored video is not one file — it is a manifest (M3U8 for HLS, MPD for DASH) plus many 4–6-second segments, one segment per (resolution, codec) combination. When the player opens a video, it downloads the manifest first (a few KB), then requests segments one at a time. The player measures its own bandwidth and buffer level and switches quality between segments — so a viewer whose Wi-Fi drops from strong to weak sees the next segment come down in 480p instead of stalling. This is why segments are short: shorter segments mean faster quality adaptation, at the cost of more manifest overhead. YouTube typically uses ~5-second segments as the sweet spot.

4. Storage tiers — cheap-and-cold, fast-and-hot

Videos are stored in three logical tiers. The master original lives in a durable, replicated blob store (S3 Standard / GCS) — this is the source of truth from which any lost rendition can be regenerated. The renditions and segments live in the same blob store but are the objects actually served. Cold videos — anything not watched in, say, 90 days — are migrated to a cheaper storage class (S3 Glacier Instant Retrieval or similar) with slightly higher retrieval cost. Metadata (title, uploader, view count, likes) lives in a sharded relational store (Vitess on MySQL for YouTube itself); high-volume denormalized reads (comments, subscription feeds) sit in a wide-column store like Bigtable/Cassandra.

5. CDN strategy — three tiers to keep origin quiet

The CDN is the only reason this design is affordable. It has three tiers: the edge PoP is the closest to the user (Google Global Cache lives in ISP networks and inside universities; CloudFront lives in ~600 PoPs globally); the regional mid-tier aggregates edge misses so origin sees only unique content; and the origin is the blob store of last resort. Hot content is promoted into the CDN by popularity — a video that starts trending is pulled up from origin into the edge on its first miss. A cold long-tail video may never be cached, and that's fine: for a video watched twice a month, cache miss cost is trivial. Interviewers love hearing you say "we don't cache the long tail because the working-set is Zipf-distributed — 90% of watches are on 10% of videos."

6. Recommendations — two-stage funnel

The recommendation problem is "from ~1 billion videos, pick 10 for this user right now." You cannot rank a billion candidates per request — the classic solution, publicly described in the 2016 paper "Deep Neural Networks for YouTube Recommendations", is a two-stage architecture. Stage 1 — candidate generation — takes user embeddings and does approximate nearest-neighbor lookup over item embeddings to return a few hundred candidates in milliseconds. Stage 2 — ranking — takes those hundreds of candidates plus rich features (watch time, freshness, session context, user demographics) and applies a deep model to produce a final ordered list. Real-time signals (this session's watches) feed both stages so the feed adapts as you scroll. Training data comes from the analytics pipeline: every play, pause, and skip is logged to Kafka, processed by Spark, and lands in BigQuery for offline model training.

7. View counting & social — write-behind, eventually consistent

View counts do not go through a synchronous DB write. Every play emits an event to Kafka, a stream job aggregates counts per window, and the count DB is updated in batches — a viewer refreshing the page might see the counter go from 1,203 to 1,847 in one jump. This is deliberate: strong consistency on a global counter would require distributed locking, which is impossible at YouTube throughput. The same pattern applies to likes and watch history — write to a durable log first, materialize the aggregate later.

🧮 The DSA behind it

Data structures & algorithms

YouTube looks like it's "all infrastructure," but under every subsystem is a specific algorithm doing the heavy lifting. Naming them in an interview signals depth.

ComponentStructure / algorithmWhy chosen & complexity
Video ID generation Snowflake-style 64-bit IDs Time-sortable, globally unique without coordination. O(1) per ID.
Upload chunk assembly Content-addressable hashing (SHA-256 per part) Detect corruption & dedupe re-uploads. O(n) over bytes; parallelizable.
Transcode fan-out MapReduce / DAG of chunks Split source at scene cuts, transcode chunks in parallel, reduce (stitch). Wall-clock shrinks from O(video length) to O(chunk length).
Segment lookup on watch Hash-partitioned key-value on (videoId, resolution, segmentIndex) O(1) get from blob store; CDN caches by URL.
Adaptive bitrate selection Rate-based EWMA on client bandwidth + PID-style buffer controller Smooths quality switches; O(1) per segment on the client.
Search index Inverted index + TF-IDF / BM25 (Elasticsearch/Lucene) O(k + log N) per term over posting lists.
Candidate generation (recs) Approximate nearest neighbor over embeddings (HNSW, ScaNN) Sub-linear (~O(log N)) over a billion items vs O(N) brute force.
Ranking (recs) Deep neural net over hundreds of features O(F × depth × candidates); candidates capped at a few hundred, so bounded.
Trending / view aggregation Count-min sketch, HyperLogLog for unique viewers Sub-linear memory, approximate but tight bounds; O(1) update.
Hot content in CDN LRU-K / LFU with admission (TinyLFU) Keeps hot working-set (Zipf head) in edge cache. O(1) amortized.
Duplicate / Content-ID detection Perceptual hashing + Bloom filter pre-filter Bloom rules out obvious non-matches in O(1); full pHash comparison only on candidates.

The one to remember: MapReduce for transcoding. It's the reason a 3-hour 4K upload doesn't take 3 hours to become watchable — chunks encode in parallel and the reducer stitches them.

Deployment story: services run on EKS across three Availability Zones per region, with active-active multi-region for user-facing APIs. S3 uses Cross-Region Replication so a whole region can go down without losing the master copy. CloudFront's global anycast means viewers in Tokyo, São Paulo, and Frankfurt all get their nearest edge automatically. The recommender's online serving lives in every region behind an ALB; the offline training is a single global pipeline that publishes updated embeddings and model weights on a daily cadence.

💻 C# implementation

Two representative pieces of the design in C#: (1) the upload service returning a pre-signed S3 URL and publishing a transcode job, and (2) a transcoder worker that pulls a job, launches FFmpeg for one rendition, and writes segments back to S3. Both are deliberately compact — the point in an interview is to show you can code the seam between service tiers, not to write a full media server.

Upload service (ASP.NET Core minimal API)

using Amazon.S3;
using Amazon.S3.Model;
using Amazon.SQS;
using Amazon.SQS.Model;
using System.Text.Json;

var builder = WebApplication.CreateBuilder(args);
builder.Services.AddAWSService<IAmazonS3>();
builder.Services.AddAWSService<IAmazonSQS>();
var app = builder.Build();

const string RawBucket   = "yt-raw-uploads";
const string QueueUrl    = "https://sqs.us-east-2.amazonaws.com/000000000000/yt-transcode";

// 1. Client asks for an upload URL. We mint an S3 multipart pre-signed URL
//    (valid ~15 min) and return it. Bytes never touch our app tier.
app.MapPost("/uploads/init", (InitUploadReq req, IAmazonS3 s3) =>
{
    var videoId = SnowflakeId.NewId();          // 64-bit sortable ID
    var key     = $"raw/{videoId}/source.{req.Ext}";

    var url = s3.GetPreSignedURL(new GetPreSignedUrlRequest {
        BucketName = RawBucket,
        Key        = key,
        Verb       = HttpVerb.PUT,
        Expires    = DateTime.UtcNow.AddMinutes(15),
        ContentType= req.ContentType
    });

    // Persist a videos row: status = UPLOADING. (DB call omitted for brevity.)
    return Results.Ok(new { videoId, uploadUrl = url, key });
});

// 2. Client calls /uploads/complete once S3 confirms all parts.
//    We flip status → UPLOADED and enqueue a transcode job per resolution.
app.MapPost("/uploads/complete", async (CompleteReq req, IAmazonSQS sqs) =>
{
    string[] resolutions = { "240p", "480p", "720p", "1080p" };

    // Fan out one message per rendition — workers pick these up in parallel.
    foreach (var res in resolutions)
    {
        var body = JsonSerializer.Serialize(new {
            videoId    = req.VideoId,
            sourceKey  = req.Key,
            resolution = res,
            codec      = "h264"
        });
        await sqs.SendMessageAsync(new SendMessageRequest {
            QueueUrl    = QueueUrl,
            MessageBody = body,
            MessageGroupId = req.VideoId   // keep same video's jobs ordered
        });
    }

    return Results.Ok(new { req.VideoId, status = "QUEUED" });
});

app.Run();

Transcoder worker (background service)

public class TranscoderWorker : BackgroundService
{
    private readonly IAmazonSQS _sqs;
    private readonly IAmazonS3  _s3;
    private const string Renditions = "yt-renditions";
    private const string Queue      = "https://sqs..../yt-transcode";

    protected override async Task ExecuteAsync(CancellationToken ct)
    {
        while (!ct.IsCancellationRequested)
        {
            var msgs = await _sqs.ReceiveMessageAsync(new ReceiveMessageRequest {
                QueueUrl        = Queue,
                MaxNumberOfMessages = 1,
                WaitTimeSeconds = 20            // long-poll
            }, ct);

            foreach (var m in msgs.Messages)
            {
                var job = JsonSerializer.Deserialize<TranscodeJob>(m.Body)!;
                await TranscodeAsync(job, ct);
                await _sqs.DeleteMessageAsync(Queue, m.ReceiptHandle, ct);
            }
        }
    }

    private async Task TranscodeAsync(TranscodeJob job, CancellationToken ct)
    {
        // 1) Pull master to local disk (streamed, no full buffer in memory).
        var src = $"/tmp/{job.VideoId}.src";
        await using (var obj = await _s3.GetObjectAsync("yt-raw-uploads", job.SourceKey, ct))
        await using (var fs = File.Create(src))
            await obj.ResponseStream.CopyToAsync(fs, ct);

        // 2) Transcode + HLS-segment via FFmpeg — 6-second GOP-aligned chunks.
        var outDir = $"/tmp/{job.VideoId}/{job.Resolution}";
        Directory.CreateDirectory(outDir);
        var h = ResolutionHeight(job.Resolution);
        var args = $"-i {src} -vf scale=-2:{h} -c:v libx264 -c:a aac " +
                   $"-hls_time 6 -hls_playlist_type vod " +
                   $"-hls_segment_filename {outDir}/seg_%03d.ts {outDir}/index.m3u8";
        await RunProcess("ffmpeg", args, ct);

        // 3) Upload segments + playlist back to S3 under a stable prefix.
        foreach (var file in Directory.EnumerateFiles(outDir))
        {
            var key = $"renditions/{job.VideoId}/{job.Resolution}/{Path.GetFileName(file)}";
            await _s3.PutObjectAsync(new PutObjectRequest {
                BucketName = Renditions, Key = key, FilePath = file
            }, ct);
        }

        // 4) Flip DB: rendition ready. Once all resolutions done, mark video PUBLIC
        //    and invalidate CDN if a re-encode.
    }

    private static int ResolutionHeight(string r) => r switch {
        "240p" => 240, "480p" => 480,
        "720p" => 720, "1080p" => 1080,
        _ => throw new ArgumentException(r)
    };

    private static async Task RunProcess(string exe, string args, CancellationToken ct)
    {
        using var p = System.Diagnostics.Process.Start(new System.Diagnostics.ProcessStartInfo {
            FileName = exe, Arguments = args, RedirectStandardError = true, UseShellExecute = false
        })!;
        await p.WaitForExitAsync(ct);
        if (p.ExitCode != 0) throw new InvalidOperationException("ffmpeg failed");
    }
}

public record TranscodeJob(string VideoId, string SourceKey, string Resolution, string Codec);

Note the shape: the upload API is synchronous but tiny (mint URL, enqueue jobs, return); the transcode worker is long-running and CPU-bound, running FFmpeg out of process. In production, chunked parallel transcoding would split the source further (say, one worker per 30-second scene) and a reducer would stitch segments — this is where the MapReduce framing shines. For AWS-first answers, replace the FFmpeg worker entirely with Elemental MediaConvert — same conceptual pipeline, but managed.

⚖️ Trade-offs, bottlenecks & likely follow-ups

Where it can break

The three real bottlenecks are egress bandwidth cost, transcoder throughput during viral upload bursts, and metadata DB hot shards (e.g., a global superstar channel writing a video that every user queries). Egress is handled by aggressive CDN caching and by tiered edge → regional → origin fill; transcoder bursts absorb into the queue and workers autoscale; hot shards are handled by caching the item view (Redis) and by write-behind counters. A subtle failure mode: a coding bug that produces a corrupted rendition — you never want to lose the master, so renditions are re-derivable and can be blown away and regenerated.

Consistency vs availability

Everything user-facing chooses availability. View counts, likes, subscriber counts are all eventually consistent — a viewer refreshing may see the count jump. Payment/monetization data (revenue share, ad billing) is the exception and lives in a separate strongly-consistent system with 2PC/Saga semantics. In an interview, name that split explicitly: "read paths are AP; the money path is CP."

Likely follow-up questions (with crisp answers)

How do you keep viral videos from melting origin?

Origin Shield / regional mid-tier collapses cache-fill traffic to one request per region per edge miss. Plus request coalescing: if 10 000 edges miss simultaneously, only one goes to origin.

Why 4–6-second HLS segments? Why not 2s or 10s?

Shorter segments = faster ABR adaptation and lower live latency but more manifest overhead and more HTTP requests. Longer = fewer requests, better cache hit ratio, but slow to react to bandwidth changes. 4–6s is the industry-standard sweet spot.

How do you handle "fresh upload not yet transcoded, viewers hitting play"?

Show a "processing" state until the first watchable rendition (typically 360p) is ready — this is why 360p is transcoded first. Once ready, unlock playback and continue transcoding higher resolutions in the background.

How does the recommender avoid stale results after a viral event?

Real-time signals feed both stages: recent watches are added to the user's session vector and to a real-time popularity index. Candidate ANN over embeddings uses the fresh session embedding; ranking uses live features. Full model retraining is daily; feature freshness is minutes.

Why not gRPC everywhere instead of HTTP for streaming?

Video segments are big and cacheable — HTTP is a first-class citizen of every CDN, browser, and mobile player, and HTTP/2 already multiplexes. gRPC gives you nothing on the segment path and would kill CDN-ability. Use gRPC on the control plane (upload service ↔ metadata) and HTTP on the data plane.

How would you extend this to live streaming?

RTMP/SRT ingest, real-time transcode to LL-HLS or CMAF chunked-transfer, edge CDN with same three-tier fill. End-to-end latency ~2–5s (versus 15–30s classic HLS). YouTube Live uses this pattern.

How do you prevent unauthorized downloads / DRM?

Encrypt segments (AES-128 in CMAF) with per-user session keys handed out through DRM licensing (Widevine / FairPlay / PlayReady). Pre-signed segment URLs with short TTLs prevent link-sharing.

🗣️ Interview cheat sheet

"YouTube is fundamentally a write-once, read-a-billion-times problem. The uploader hits a chunked, resumable upload that lands the master file in blob storage. That triggers an asynchronous, MapReduce-style transcode into 6–8 resolutions × 2–3 codecs × 4-second HLS segments. Viewers hit a three-tier CDN — edge PoP, regional cache, origin — and the player switches quality between segments as bandwidth changes. Metadata lives in sharded MySQL; comments in a wide-column store; views ride a Kafka pipeline into an aggregate. Recommendations are a two-stage funnel: ANN over embeddings picks a few hundred candidates from a billion, then a deep ranker scores them. Choose availability over consistency everywhere except billing."

Numbers to quote

  • 500 hours uploaded/minute (public YouTube figure).
  • ~1 B DAU, ~5 B views/day, read:write ratio ~100:1.
  • ~2 PB/day raw upload growth, ~1 EB/year with renditions.
  • 4–6 second HLS/DASH segments.
  • <2 s startup latency target; 11 nines durability on the master.

Five talking points that win the round

  • "The client uploads bytes directly to S3 via a pre-signed URL — my app never becomes the bandwidth bottleneck."
  • "Transcoding is MapReduce: split at scene cuts, transcode chunks in parallel, reduce back into segments + a manifest."
  • "Three-tier CDN — edge PoP, regional, origin — because a single-tier CDN can't survive a viral event; regional collapses fill traffic."
  • "Player-side adaptive bitrate using the same manifest — the server has one job, the player has all the intelligence."
  • "Recs are a two-stage funnel: ANN over embeddings for recall (billions → hundreds), deep ranker for precision (hundreds → 10)."
