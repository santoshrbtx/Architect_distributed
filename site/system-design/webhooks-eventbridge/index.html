Webhooks & AWS EventBridge

Two ways to move an event from "something just happened over here" to "now do X over there" — one is an open-web HTTP contract, the other is a managed AWS event bus. Same shape, different blast radius.

🎯 Real-world scenario

What we're building & where it's used

Every non-trivial modern product has to solve the same problem: something happened in system A, and system B needs to react to it. A customer's card charged in Stripe → your dashboard updates. A build finished in GitHub Actions → Slack pings the channel. An order status changed in your warehouse → 3 downstream systems (billing, notifications, analytics) all need to know within seconds. Polling APIs every few seconds is wasteful, slow, and doesn't scale, so we need push-based, event-driven plumbing.

Two dominant designs solve this. A webhook is the open-web version: the producer signs an HTTPS POST and fires it at a public URL you control — used by Stripe, GitHub, Shopify, Twilio, Slack, PayPal, HubSpot, SendGrid, and virtually every SaaS API. AWS EventBridge is the same idea implemented as a managed service inside AWS: a serverless event bus where producers PutEvents, rules pattern-match on JSON, and matched events fan out to up to 5 targets per rule (Lambda, SQS, Step Functions, another AWS account, or even a webhook via API Destinations). Same conceptual shape — event → route → deliver → retry — but one is public-HTTP and the other is AWS IAM-secured.

Interviewers love this pair because it forces you to talk about at-least-once delivery, signing, idempotency, retries with backoff, dead-letter queues, ordering guarantees and the classic dual-write problem. If you can compare the two crisply, you demonstrate you understand event-driven architecture in general, not just one vendor's implementation.

🧑‍🎓 In laymen terms

The whole thing, explained without any tech words

Imagine you order a pizza online. You could stand at the window every 30 seconds and shout "Is it here yet? Is it here yet?" — that's what a computer program does when it "polls" a website. Annoying, wasteful, and if the pizza shows up a second after you last checked, you don't know for a whole 30 seconds. A better idea is that the delivery driver rings your doorbell when the pizza actually arrives. That doorbell ring is a webhook.

A webhook is just a "please ring my doorbell when X happens" arrangement between two companies. When you shop online, the shop tells the payment company (say, Stripe): "here's my doorbell address — myshop.com/paid — please ring it the instant a customer's card goes through." Then the shop can put you on their thank-you page and email your receipt immediately. Nobody had to keep asking. This is how Uber knows the driver arrived, how WhatsApp shows your friend's typing dots, how Slack pops up a notification the moment someone messages you, and how GitHub tells your build system "hey, someone just pushed code."

Now imagine you're a huge apartment building with hundreds of tenants — the pizza guy, the mailman, the plumber, the neighbour returning a bowl — all wanting to ring different apartments. You'd get chaos if every visitor had to know every apartment number. Instead, the building has a front desk. Every visitor drops off their delivery there, tells the receptionist what it is, and the receptionist looks at a little book and forwards it to the right tenants. That front desk is what AWS EventBridge is inside Amazon's cloud — a smart receptionist for events. One thing happens, and it can automatically be forwarded to 5 different tenants at once, without the sender needing to know any of them.

The hard parts, in plain words: sometimes the doorbell rings and you're in the shower — the visitor keeps ringing back until you answer, which means you might hear the same news twice ("your pizza's here" x3), so you have to remember what you already knew. Sometimes the pizza arrives before the drink even though they left the shop in the opposite order — things arrive out of order. And someone could pretend to be the pizza guy, so you need a secret handshake to know it's really them. That's basically every hard problem in this whole page, in three sentences.

A tiny example to walk through: you buy a $12 book. Your card gets charged. Stripe fires a "payment succeeded for order #A47" doorbell at the bookshop's server. The bookshop confirms the handshake, writes "order A47 = paid" in its notebook, then sends you the confirmation email. If the doorbell rings twice for the same order (Stripe wasn't sure the first ring got through), the shop looks at its notebook, sees "A47 already paid", and quietly ignores the duplicate. That's the whole thing.

📋 Requirements & scale

Requirements and back-of-the-envelope math

Functional requirements

  • Producer emits an event when something interesting happens (state change, transaction, external trigger).
  • Router matches events to zero or more interested consumers using a pattern/filter (event type, source, JSON body attributes).
  • Delivery pushes the event to consumer(s) with retries on failure.
  • Authenticity — consumer must verify the event really came from the producer (HMAC signature, or IAM).
  • Observability — the sender can see delivery attempts, status, and manually replay failed ones.

Non-functional requirements

  • At-least-once delivery — never drop an event, even if the consumer is briefly down. Consumer must handle duplicates.
  • Low delivery latency — sub-second p50 in-region for EventBridge; a few seconds for public webhooks.
  • Durable retry with backoff — exponential, capped, then dead-letter.
  • Security — signed payloads, HTTPS-only, replay-attack protection.
  • Fan-out — one event → N consumers without the producer knowing them.

If you fan out to 5 targets each, that's 50,000 downstream invocations/sec — enough to fill 100 concurrent Lambdas at 500/sec each. In webhook land, the same 10 k/sec producer would need 10,000 consumer servers if each takes 1 second per POST — which is why the golden rule is "consumer returns 200 in < 200 ms, enqueues, processes async."

🏗️ High-level architecture

How the pieces fit together

Both systems share a five-stage pipeline: Emit → Enrich/Sign → Route → Deliver → Confirm/Retry. The stages are almost identical; the difference is who runs which stage and how it authenticates.

Webhook flow (open-web)

The producer's application writes the event to its own outbox table in the same DB transaction as the state change (this defeats the dual-write bug). A relay worker polls that outbox (or reads a CDC stream), looks up the subscribers registered for that event type, serialises a JSON body, computes HMAC-SHA256(body, endpoint_secret), and fires an HTTPS POST at the consumer's URL. The consumer verifies the signature, drops the payload onto its own internal queue, and returns 200 OK in under a second. If it doesn't respond in time or returns 5xx, the relay schedules an exponential-backoff retry (t+1min, t+5min, t+30min, ..., up to hours or days). After the final failure the event goes to a dead-letter store and is surfaced in a "failed deliveries" UI where an operator can replay.

EventBridge flow (AWS-internal)

An AWS service (or your own app calling PutEvents) drops an event onto an event bus. EventBridge evaluates the JSON event pattern on every rule attached to that bus; a rule may match on source, detail-type, and content filters inside detail (prefix, suffix, numeric ranges, existence, IP-CIDR, wildcards). Every matching rule fans the event out to up to 5 targets — Lambda, SQS, SNS, Step Functions, Kinesis, another event bus (cross-account/cross-region), or an API Destination (a signed HTTPS webhook, so EventBridge itself can play producer). Failed target invocations are retried for up to 24 h with exponential backoff (up to 185 attempts), then routed to a per-target SQS dead-letter queue with a rich error envelope (rule ARN, error code, exhausted-retry reason).

Webhook flow (public HTTPS + HMAC) EventBridge flow (in-AWS + IAM) Retry / DLQ path

Look at the two lanes side by side and notice the shape is identical: producer → durable buffer → pattern match / lookup → deliver → confirm or retry to DLQ. Webhooks bolt each of those stages together with your own outbox, your own relay, your own signing, your own retry scheduler; EventBridge just gives you all of them managed with a single PutEvents call.

🔍 Component deep-dive

1. Event envelope

Both systems standardise on a JSON envelope with three parts: metadata (id, timestamp, type), source (who emitted it), and detail/data (the domain payload). EventBridge names them exactly: id, time, source, detail-type, detail. Stripe uses id, created, type, data.object. GitHub uses X-GitHub-Event + body. The event id is load-bearing: it's what consumers use to deduplicate replays.

2. Durable buffer & the dual-write problem

Neither system lets you skip the "the event must survive if the producer crashes" step. Webhook producers solve this with the transactional outbox pattern — write the domain change and the "to-be-delivered event" row in one DB transaction, then a separate relay worker drains the outbox and fires the HTTP POST. EventBridge takes the durability off your plate: once PutEvents returns 200, AWS owns the event's fate. Under the hood the bus is backed by replicated storage per partition (unpublished internal detail, but the API contract is "if you got 200, it's durable"). This is the single biggest operational win of EventBridge over rolling your own webhooks.

3. Routing / matching

Webhook systems typically use a simple subscriptions table: (event_type, endpoint_url, secret). When an event fires, look up matching rows, POST to each. EventBridge uses richer content-based filtering: a rule contains a JSON event pattern that can match by exact value, prefix, suffix, wildcard, numeric range, IP CIDR, existence — all evaluated in-memory against the event's JSON. Pattern size is capped at 2048 chars; you can have 300 rules per bus with up to 30 of them containing wildcards.

4. Signing & authentication

Webhooks live on the open internet, so the consumer must prove the caller is the real producer. The universal answer is HMAC-SHA256 over the raw body plus a timestamp, using a shared secret per endpoint. Stripe puts it in Stripe-Signature: t=<unix>,v1=<hex>. The consumer recomputes and compares in constant time. The timestamp defends against replay — reject if |now − t| > 5 min. EventBridge doesn't need any of this inside AWS: the producer's identity is authenticated by IAM SigV4 signing of the PutEvents call, and each target grants EventBridge permission via a resource policy. When EventBridge fires outbound to an API Destination it becomes the webhook producer and does the signing itself (via Connections with Basic / OAuth / API Key / Bearer credentials stored in Secrets Manager).

5. Retries, backoff, DLQ

Both systems retry on 5xx / timeouts and eventually give up. Stripe retries for 3 days on live mode with exponential backoff. EventBridge retries for 24 hours by default, with exponential backoff up to 185 attempts; you can set MaximumEventAgeInSeconds (60–86400) and MaximumRetryAttempts (0–185) per target. When retries are exhausted (or on non-retryable errors like NO_PERMISSIONS, NO_RESOURCE) the event is routed to the target's SQS DLQ with RULE_ARN, ERROR_CODE, EXHAUSTED_RETRY_CONDITION, and RETRY_ATTEMPTS in message attributes. Replay is a manual operation from the DLQ, or from EventBridge's built-in Archive + Replay (store all matching events for N days, then StartReplay to re-fire them at a rule).

6. Idempotency at the consumer

Because delivery is at-least-once, every consumer MUST be idempotent. The pattern is: on receipt, look up the event id in a seen-events store (Redis SET with TTL, or a DynamoDB item with the id as PK), and if it's already there, return 200 without re-processing. This is the number-one interview trap — candidates who forget this get grilled on "what happens if the same event is delivered twice while your first invocation is mid-flight?" The answer is: dedupe by event id, and use conditional writes so the second delivery loses the race.

7. Ordering

Neither system guarantees ordering. Stripe explicitly says events may arrive out of order (e.g. invoice.paid can arrive before invoice.created). EventBridge has the same contract; if you need ordering, use an SQS FIFO queue as your target and derive a group id from a business key (customer id, order id). Alternatively, embed a monotonically-increasing sequence number in your payload and let the consumer discard stale events by comparing against the last-seen sequence for that entity.

🆚 Combined view — same architecture, different chassis

The reason to talk about both in one page is that they solve the exact same problem, just in different environments. If you understand the shape, you can pick the right chassis for the job. Here's the mapping stage by stage:

Put simply: if your consumer is in another company, you're building a webhook; if it's in your own AWS account, use EventBridge. The most powerful production setups combine both — EventBridge routes events internally, and one of the targets is an API Destination that lets EventBridge itself fire the webhook out to customers, with Connections handling the auth. That way you get the managed durability and pattern-matching of EventBridge plus the "reach the outside world" of webhooks in one pipeline.

Webhook mental model

"I own both the producer and the delivery mechanism. I'm going to POST HTTPS with a signed body across the internet, retry when it fails, and give the customer a portal to see failed deliveries."

EventBridge mental model

"I emit an event to a bus and let AWS route it. I don't know or care which team's Lambda picks it up. New consumers just add a rule — I never change my code."

🧮 The DSA behind it

Data structures & algorithms

Under the covers, this is a very DSA-heavy space. The interviewer often probes: "how does EventBridge match 300 rules against every event without becoming O(n) slow?" Here are the key ones.

The most interesting one is rule matching. Naively evaluating 300 patterns × 5 fields each on every one of 10 000 events/sec is 15 million comparisons/sec — do-able but wasteful. EventBridge (and open-source alternatives like AWS's own Ruler library) uses a tokenised prefix trie where common prefixes (e.g. source = "aws.s3") are evaluated once and shared across all rules that mention them. That reduces the expected work from O(rules × depth) to closer to O(depth) plus the number of rules that actually matched.

Deployment C: The hybrid pattern (recommended for anything customer-facing)

This gives you EventBridge's managed durability + observability internally, and webhook reach externally, without having to build a relay worker. This is the modern AWS answer to "we need to send webhooks to our customers".

💻 C# implementation

Three short, idiomatic pieces of C#. First, a Stripe-style HMAC verifier that a webhook consumer needs. Second, a minimal PutEvents call to EventBridge from ASP.NET. Third, the exact idempotency guard pattern using DynamoDB conditional writes that stops duplicate deliveries from double-processing.

1. Verify an inbound webhook signature (Stripe-style)

// Called from your ASP.NET Core webhook endpoint. Rejects if:
//   - Signature header missing / malformed
//   - Timestamp older than 5 min (replay defence)
//   - HMAC doesn't match (constant-time compare)
using System.Security.Cryptography;
using System.Text;

public static class WebhookVerifier
{
    private static readonly TimeSpan Tolerance = TimeSpan.FromMinutes(5);

    public static bool Verify(string rawBody, string header, string secret)
    {
        // header is: "t=1710000000,v1=<hex>"
        var parts = header.Split(',')
            .Select(p => p.Split('=', 2))
            .Where(kv => kv.Length == 2)
            .ToDictionary(kv => kv[0].Trim(), kv => kv[1].Trim());

        if (!parts.TryGetValue("t", out var tStr) || !parts.TryGetValue("v1", out var v1))
            return false;
        if (!long.TryParse(tStr, out var ts)) return false;

        var when = DateTimeOffset.FromUnixTimeSeconds(ts);
        if ((DateTimeOffset.UtcNow - when).Duration() > Tolerance)
            return false; // stale / replay

        var signedPayload = $"{ts}.{rawBody}";
        using var hmac = new HMACSHA256(Encoding.UTF8.GetBytes(secret));
        var expected = Convert.ToHexString(hmac.ComputeHash(Encoding.UTF8.GetBytes(signedPayload))).ToLowerInvariant();

        // Constant-time compare so an attacker can't time-leak the secret.
        return CryptographicOperations.FixedTimeEquals(
            Encoding.ASCII.GetBytes(expected),
            Encoding.ASCII.GetBytes(v1));
    }
}

2. Publish an event to EventBridge from ASP.NET

using Amazon.EventBridge;
using Amazon.EventBridge.Model;
using System.Text.Json;

public sealed class OrderEventPublisher
{
    private readonly IAmazonEventBridge _eb;
    public OrderEventPublisher(IAmazonEventBridge eb) => _eb = eb;

    public async Task PublishPaidAsync(string orderId, decimal amount, string currency)
    {
        var resp = await _eb.PutEventsAsync(new PutEventsRequest
        {
            Entries = [new PutEventsRequestEntry
            {
                EventBusName = "orders",
                Source       = "orders.service",
                DetailType   = "OrderPaid",
                Detail       = JsonSerializer.Serialize(new { orderId, amount, currency }),
                Time         = DateTime.UtcNow
            }]
        });
        // Any failed entry is reported in FailedEntryCount - retry those.
        if (resp.FailedEntryCount > 0)
            throw new InvalidOperationException("EventBridge PutEvents partial failure");
    }
}

3. Idempotency guard on the consumer (DynamoDB conditional put)

// Runs at the top of every webhook / target invocation.
// Returns false the second time the same event id arrives → skip processing.
using Amazon.DynamoDBv2;
using Amazon.DynamoDBv2.Model;

public sealed class IdempotencyStore
{
    private readonly IAmazonDynamoDB _db;
    public IdempotencyStore(IAmazonDynamoDB db) => _db = db;

    public async Task<bool> TryClaimAsync(string eventId, TimeSpan ttl)
    {
        try
        {
            await _db.PutItemAsync(new PutItemRequest
            {
                TableName = "processed_events",
                Item = new()
                {
                    ["event_id"] = new(eventId),
                    ["expires_at"] = new { N = DateTimeOffset.UtcNow.Add(ttl).ToUnixTimeSeconds().ToString() }
                },
                ConditionExpression = "attribute_not_exists(event_id)"
            });
            return true;   // first time — go process it
        }
        catch (ConditionalCheckFailedException)
        {
            return false;  // duplicate — skip
        }
    }
}

Together these three snippets are the whole spine: verify the sender, publish reliably, dedupe on receipt. Every real production system layers monitoring, poison-message handling and business logic on top, but the load-bearing correctness is right here.

⚖️ Trade-offs, bottlenecks & likely follow-ups

Trade-offs

Push vs pull. Push (webhook/EventBridge) gives near-real-time delivery and eliminates polling waste, but forces every consumer to run a public endpoint or a listener. Pull (polling APIs, Kafka consumer groups) is simpler for the consumer but wastes cycles when nothing is happening. Rule of thumb: use push when events are irregular and time-sensitive; use pull when they're continuous and the consumer sets its own pace.

At-least-once vs exactly-once. Both webhooks and EventBridge are at-least-once — no exceptions. The industry has largely accepted that exactly-once delivery is a lie; instead you get exactly-once processing by making the consumer idempotent. Idempotency-by-id is the only correct answer.

Ordering vs throughput. Guaranteeing ordering across a stream requires either a single partition (bottleneck) or per-key partitioning (SQS FIFO groups, Kinesis shard keys). Neither system does this by default because most workloads don't need it.

Push to customers: DIY webhook infra vs API Destinations. Rolling your own gives you full control (custom auth schemes, custom retry policies, custom UI). But it's a huge amount of undifferentiated heavy lifting — a whole product's worth of features. API Destinations get you 80% of it for cents per million events.

Bottlenecks & failure modes

  • Slow consumer — one tenant's endpoint takes 30 s to respond, retries pile up, connection pool exhausts. Fix: per-endpoint circuit breaker + rate limit; drop to DLQ fast when latency is bad.
  • Thundering herd on outage recovery — a consumer recovers and gets 24 h of retries in one minute. Fix: jittered backoff + concurrency cap per endpoint.
  • Schema drift — you add a field, an old consumer breaks. Fix: additive-only changes, versioned event types, Schema Registry.
  • Signature secret leak — rotate immediately, support dual secrets during rotation, keep a rotation cadence.
  • Poison message — a payload that crashes every consumer. Fix: retry cap + DLQ + alarm; process from DLQ manually.
  • EventBridge PutEvents throttling — 10 k/sec cap in us-east-1 (raiseable). Batch entries in one call (up to 10 entries × 256 KB = 2.56 MB per call), or shard across buses.
  • Duplicate at boundary — the consumer processes the event, dies before returning 200; producer retries. Fix: idempotency guard at the top of the handler, before the side effect.

Likely interviewer follow-ups (with crisp answers)

Why not just use SNS?

SNS is a pub/sub topic with a simpler filter language and no rules-with-multiple-targets abstraction, no archive/replay, no schema registry, and no API destinations. EventBridge is a superset for event-driven use cases. SNS wins on raw throughput and price for simple broadcast; EventBridge wins on routing complexity, observability, and cross-account/cross-region.

Why is HMAC signing better than a shared bearer token?

A bearer token proves possession but says nothing about the payload — if it leaks, everything is compromised, and a MITM can tamper with the body. HMAC over the body means an attacker who steals the header can't reuse it for a different payload, and rotating the secret invalidates all replays.

How do you handle a consumer that's been down for a day?

EventBridge retries up to 24 h with exponential backoff; after that the events land in the target's SQS DLQ with error metadata. When the consumer recovers, drain the DLQ into it (via a Lambda pointed at the DLQ). For longer outages, use Archive + Replay — you can replay any event received in the last N days by time range and pattern.

Ordering matters for my domain — what do I do?

Send events to an SQS FIFO queue as the target, with MessageGroupId derived from a business key (e.g. order id). FIFO groups guarantee ordering within a group and parallelism across groups. Alternatively, embed a sequence number and drop stale events on the consumer.

How do you prevent replay attacks on a webhook?

Sign the timestamp along with the body, and reject anything more than ~5 minutes old on the consumer side. Also dedupe by event id in a store with a TTL longer than your retry window.

How would you send webhooks to 100 000 different customer endpoints?

Central EventBridge bus in your account; one rule with an API Destination target per customer, or one shared API Destination and a Lambda that looks up the right endpoint. Store per-customer secrets in Secrets Manager (Connections). This scales because EventBridge handles the durability, retries, and DLQs — you just wire config.

What if the same event triggers two rules — do I get duplicate delivery?

Yes, that's fan-out: two rules × their targets. You either accept it (each consumer is idempotent) or restructure your patterns so rules are mutually exclusive.

Webhooks are the open-web version: HTTPS POST, HMAC signature, you own the outbox, relay, and DLQ.   • Fan-out: 5 targets/rule × 300 rules/bus.
  • Retries: default 24 h max age, up to 185 attempts, exponential backoff.
  • Stripe retries: 3 days with exponential backoff on live mode.
  • Consumer must return 2xx within seconds — enqueue and process async.
  • Idempotency dedupe TTL should be ≥ retry window (so ≥ 24 h for EventBridge, ≥ 3 d for Stripe).
  • Replay-attack tolerance: 5 minutes.

Five talking points that win the round

  • At-least-once forces idempotency — event id + conditional put + TTL. Say it out loud.
  • Signing goes over the body + timestamp, not just the body — protects against replay.
  • Dual-write problem — solve it with the transactional outbox (or let EventBridge own durability via PutEvents).
  • Fan-out is a rule-per-consumer pattern; consumers add rules, producer never changes.
  • DLQ + replay is not optional — it's how you keep a spike from turning into permanent data loss.
