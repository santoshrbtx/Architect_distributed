How Amazon DynamoDB Works

🎯 What it is & where it's used

A serverless key-value store that never slows down

DynamoDB is AWS's fully-managed NoSQL database that delivers consistent single-digit-millisecond latency at any scale — whether your table holds a kilobyte or a petabyte, whether you do ten requests a second or twenty million. It descends from the 2007 Dynamo paper (the internal Amazon key-value store born from a Christmas-shopping outage), but the original Dynamo was disliked internally because teams still had to run and scale it themselves. DynamoDB, launched in January 2012, kept Dynamo's core ideas (consistent hashing, replication, tunable consistency) and removed all the operational burden — no servers, no manual sharding, no patching.

It powers the shopping cart and many core services at Amazon.com, and externally backs things like Snapchat's Memories, Disney+ watchlists, Lyft ride state, and countless session/leaderboard/IoT workloads. Interviewers ask about it because it forces you to think differently: unlike a relational database, DynamoDB makes you design your table around your access patterns first — get that wrong and no amount of scaling saves you. Explaining why it stays fast (the partitioning + replication model) and how to model data for it is the whole game.

🧑‍🎓 In laymen terms

A library with a million drawers and a magic sorting hat

Imagine the world's largest library, but instead of shelves it has a million tiny drawers lined up along a giant wall. When you walk in with a book to file — say a note about your grandmother's birthday — you don't wander around looking for space. You hand the book to a little assistant at the door who wears a magic sorting hat. The hat looks at the title of your book, whispers a number, and tells you "drawer number seven-hundred-and-forty-two, second row." You put the book in, done. Someone else walks in with a completely different book — the hat tells them "drawer number two-thousand-nine-hundred-and-eleven." Nobody ever has to fight over the same drawer.

Now here's the beautiful part. Because everyone gets sent to a different drawer, a thousand people can file books at the same time without bumping into each other. If suddenly ten million people show up, the library just builds more drawers along the wall and the hat starts pointing to those too. Nothing slows down. This is how Amazon's giant online store keeps working even during a huge Black Friday sale — every shopping cart is filed in its own little drawer.

To make sure nothing is ever lost, the library secretly makes three copies of every drawer, kept in three different buildings across the city. If one building burns down, your book is still safe in the other two. And when you go back to fetch your grandmother's birthday note, you again walk in the door, tell the hat the title, and it whispers the drawer number — you walk straight to it in one step. No searching, no wandering.

The one big rule is: you have to tell the hat in advance what titles you'll be searching by. If you file a book under "birthday note" but later try to find it by "grandmother's name," the hat shrugs — it wasn't told to remember that. This is why people who use DynamoDB spend a lot of time thinking about how they'll search before they store anything.

📋 Guarantees, and the numbers to quote

What DynamoDB promises — and its hard limits

The value proposition is predictable performance: latency stays flat as you scale because data is spread across many small partitions, each independently served. Durability comes from synchronous replication across three Availability Zones; availability targets are 99.99% (single region) and 99.999% (global tables).

UnitDefinition
RCU (Read Capacity Unit)1 strongly-consistent read/sec, OR 2 eventually-consistent reads/sec, for items up to 4 KB. (Transactional reads cost 2×.)
WCU (Write Capacity Unit)1 write/sec for items up to 1 KB. (Transactional writes cost 2×.)

These three per-partition ceilings — 10 GB, 3,000 RCU, 1,000 WCU — are the key mental model: whenever a partition crosses any of them, DynamoDB automatically splits it into more partitions. That's the whole scaling story in one sentence.

🏗️ Architecture

Request routers, partitions, and Paxos replication

A request flows through two tiers. First it hits a stateless request router — it authenticates, checks the metadata service to learn which partition owns your key, verifies you have capacity (admission control), and forwards the request. Second, it lands on the right storage node. Every partition is replicated to three storage nodes across three AZs, forming a replication group. One replica is the leader, elected via Multi-Paxos; the leader handles all writes and strongly-consistent reads, then replicates the write to the peers. A write is acknowledged once a quorum (the leader + at least one peer) has durably persisted it to its write-ahead log.

request path Paxos replication metadata lookup

Behind the scenes there are also log replicas (write-ahead-log-only nodes) that let DynamoDB restore a full replica quickly after a failure without a heavy copy, and an auto-admin service that continuously splits/moves partitions. The genius is that every layer is horizontally scalable and stateless where possible — routers scale out freely, storage scales by adding partitions.

🔍 Component deep-dive

Partition keys and how data is placed

Every item has a partition key (PK), optionally plus a sort key (SK). DynamoDB runs the partition key through an internal hash function; the hash determines which partition stores the item. Items sharing a PK live together on one partition, sorted by SK — which is what makes Query (fetch all items for one PK, optionally a SK range) so fast: it's a B-tree lookup within a single partition. Choosing a high-cardinality, evenly-accessed partition key is the single most important design decision, because it spreads load uniformly.

Consistency options

Reads come in two flavors. A strongly consistent read goes to the leader and always returns the latest write. An eventually consistent read (the default, and half the cost) hits any of the three replicas at random — so there's roughly a "1-in-3 chance" of reading a value from a replica that hasn't yet received the very latest write during the brief replication window. This is the CAP/PACELC dial in action: DynamoDB is PA/EL — it favors availability and low latency, offering strong consistency only as an opt-in, single-region choice.

Hot partitions & adaptive capacity

The classic failure mode is a hot partition: a bad key (e.g., partitioning by "today's date" so all writes hit one partition) concentrates traffic and throttles even though the table has spare capacity. DynamoDB fights this with adaptive capacity (instantly borrows unused capacity for hot partitions), burst capacity (banks up to 5 minutes of unused throughput), and split-for-heat (isolates a hot key onto its own partition). But you should still design keys to avoid hotspots — write sharding (append a random suffix #1..#N to the PK) is the standard fix for an unavoidably hot key.

Secondary indexes

To query by an attribute that isn't the primary key, you add an index. A Local Secondary Index (LSI) keeps the same PK but a different SK (must be created at table creation, shares the partition's throughput). A Global Secondary Index (GSI) has a completely different PK/SK and is effectively a separate, asynchronously-maintained copy of the table (eventually consistent, its own capacity). A "log propagator" keeps indexes updated. Because each index adds write cost, a table is capped at a handful — favor GSIs for flexible query patterns.

Capacity math worked example

Say you store 6 KB items and need 500 strongly-consistent reads/sec. Each read rounds up to ceil(6/4) = 2 RCU, so you need 500 × 2 = 1,000 RCU. For 200 writes/sec of 2 KB items: ceil(2/1) = 2 WCU each ⇒ 400 WCU. Being able to do this arithmetic out loud is a common interview checkpoint.

💻 C# implementation — single-table design in action

The idiomatic DynamoDB pattern is single-table design: store multiple entity types in one table using overloaded, prefixed keys so related items co-locate under one partition key and one Query fetches an entire aggregate. Here's modeling a user and their orders, then reading them in a single round-trip with the AWS SDK for .NET.

// Single-table design: PK groups an aggregate, SK distinguishes entity type.
//   PK = "USER#123"   SK = "PROFILE"        -> the user row
//   PK = "USER#123"   SK = "ORDER#2024-..."  -> that user's orders
var client = new AmazonDynamoDBClient();

// --- Write a profile and an order atomically (all-or-nothing) ---
await client.TransactWriteItemsAsync(new TransactWriteItemsRequest
{
    TransactItems = new()
    {
        new() { Put = new Put {
            TableName = "AppTable",
            Item = new() {
                ["PK"] = new("USER#123"),
                ["SK"] = new("PROFILE"),
                ["name"] = new("Santosh")
            }}},
        new() { Put = new Put {
            TableName = "AppTable",
            Item = new() {
                ["PK"] = new("USER#123"),
                ["SK"] = new("ORDER#2024-06-01#a91"),
                ["total"] = new AttributeValue { N = "42.00" }
            }}}
    }
});

// --- One Query returns the profile AND every order in a single partition read ---
var resp = await client.QueryAsync(new QueryRequest
{
    TableName = "AppTable",
    KeyConditionExpression = "PK = :pk",        // hits exactly one partition -> fast
    ExpressionAttributeValues = new() { [":pk"] = new("USER#123") },
    ConsistentRead = false                        // eventually consistent = cheaper (½ RCU)
});
// resp.Items now holds the PROFILE row + all ORDER# rows, sorted by SK.

Alternatives worth naming: the higher-level DynamoDBContext (object mapper) for simple CRUD, PynamoDB/boto3 in Python, and PartiQL (SQL-like syntax) if the team prefers SQL. But the key skill is the data model, not the SDK — design keys around your access patterns before writing any code.

⚖️ Trade-offs, bottlenecks & likely follow-ups

"How does DynamoDB scale to millions of requests?"

By partitioning: data is hashed across many partitions, each capped at 10 GB / 3,000 RCU / 1,000 WCU. Cross any ceiling and it auto-splits. Stateless request routers scale out independently. Latency stays flat because each partition is small and independently served.

"What's a hot partition and how do you fix it?"

A poorly-chosen key concentrates traffic on one partition and throttles despite spare table capacity. Fixes: choose a high-cardinality key, write-shard by appending a random suffix, or lean on adaptive capacity + split-for-heat. Prevention via key design beats relying on the automatic mitigations.

"Strongly vs eventually consistent reads?"

Strong reads go to the Paxos leader and always see the latest write, cost 1 RCU/4 KB, single-region. Eventual reads hit any replica, cost ½ RCU, and may briefly return stale data during the replication window. Default is eventual.

"When is DynamoDB the wrong choice?"

When you need ad-hoc queries, complex joins, or flexible analytics — its access patterns must be known up front. For those, use Aurora/RDS, or stream to Redshift/OpenSearch/S3+Athena for analytics.

"LSI vs GSI?"

LSI = same partition key, alternate sort key, shares partition throughput, must exist at table creation, supports strong reads. GSI = entirely different key, async/eventually consistent, own capacity, can be added anytime. Prefer GSIs for flexibility.

🗣️ Interview cheat sheet

30-second pitch: "DynamoDB is a serverless NoSQL store giving single-digit-ms latency at any scale. Items are hashed by partition key across many partitions, each capped at 10 GB, 3,000 RCU, 1,000 WCU — cross a ceiling and it auto-splits. Each partition is replicated across 3 AZs with a Multi-Paxos leader that handles writes and strong reads; writes ACK on a quorum. It's a PA/EL system — availability and low latency first, strong consistency as an opt-in. You design the table around access patterns using single-table design and pick a high-cardinality key to avoid hot partitions."
  • Numbers: 400 KB item, 10 GB/partition, 3,000 RCU & 1,000 WCU/partition, 3 AZs. RCU = 4 KB strong read; WCU = 1 KB write.
  • Placement: hash(PK) → partition; items with same PK sorted by SK ⇒ fast Query.
  • Replication: 3 AZ replicas, Multi-Paxos leader, quorum write ACK, log replicas for fast recovery.
  • Consistency: eventual (default, ½ cost, any replica) vs strong (leader). CAP = PA/EL.
  • Hot partition = the #1 pitfall → high-cardinality key + write sharding.
  • Toolkit: on-demand vs provisioned, Streams→Lambda, Global Tables, DAX, TTL, transactions, single-table design.
