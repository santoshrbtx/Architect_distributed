Database Bottleneck — What Are Your Options?

Your database is dying under load. This is the classic senior-engineer interview question — walk the six-lane highway of options in order, pick with reasons, and know the cost of each turn.

🎯 Real-world scenario

The database is the bottleneck — now what?

Every non-trivial system eventually hits the same wall: one relational database is doing all the reads, all the writes, all the reporting, and all the joins for millions of users, and it starts to buckle. Query latency creeps up, CPU pegs at 100%, connection pools exhaust, replication falls behind, and pages start timing out. Every senior-engineer interview asks some flavour of this: "assume you have a monolith and a single Postgres instance and traffic keeps growing — what do you do?"

Real products live this journey every day. Instagram famously started on a single Postgres box and evolved through read replicas, memcached, and ultimately application-level sharding on Postgres. Shopify runs "pod" architecture where each shard is a self-contained MySQL cluster. Uber moved from Postgres to their in-house Schemaless (built on MySQL, sharded by trip id). Twitter pushed CQRS to the limit with a write path (tweets) totally separate from the read path (fan-out timelines in Redis). Netflix and Amazon switched database technology outright — DynamoDB and Cassandra — because sharding a relational database further would have cost more than moving off it.

The interviewer wants to hear you traverse the options tree in the right order: cheap and reversible first, expensive and one-way last. Read replicas and caching before you touch the schema; partitioning inside one node before you shard across nodes; CQRS before event sourcing; event sourcing before ripping out Postgres for DynamoDB. Anyone can name "sharding" — the signal is knowing when not to.

🧑‍🎓 In laymen terms

The library that ran out of librarians

Imagine a small town library with one librarian at the front desk. She looks up books for you, stamps them out, puts them back on the shelves, files new arrivals, and answers phone calls. When ten people a day come in, she's fine. When five hundred people a day show up, there's a queue out the door and nothing gets done.

That single librarian is your database. Everything the app wants to know — "what's my order status?", "show me my inbox", "how many likes did this post get?" — goes to her. Everything anyone changes — a new comment, a payment, a like — also goes to her. Eventually she can't keep up. This is what engineers mean when they say "the database is the bottleneck."

You have six honest ways to fix it, and you try them roughly in this order:

1. Hire helpers who can only look things up (Read Replicas). Most people just want to read the catalogue. Hire cheap assistants who each have a photocopy of the catalogue and can answer look-up questions. Only the head librarian still handles new arrivals. Boom — throughput goes up 10× for the cost of a few photocopiers.

2. Put a sticky note on the door with the popular answers (Caching). Ninety percent of people ask "is Harry Potter available?" Just write "yes, aisle 3" on a sticky note out front. Nobody has to bother the librarian at all. This is what Redis and Memcached do — remember the answer and hand it out instantly.

3. Split the library into branches by ZIP code (Sharding). One building can only hold so many books. Open five branches — one per district — and each holds a chunk of the collection. Now no single building is overwhelmed. This is powerful but painful: if someone from District A wants a District E book, someone has to figure out where to send them.

4. Two different desks: "borrow" and "return" (CQRS). Reading books out ("borrow", write-heavy work like recording who has what) is a totally different job than showing people the catalogue ("return", read-heavy work). Split them into two counters with two different systems tuned for each. Faster and cleaner.

5. Keep a journal of every action ever taken (Event Sourcing). Instead of just knowing "Amit has 3 books", write down every borrow-and-return in a permanent journal. If a computer glitches, you can replay the journal and rebuild reality. Great for audit and rewind, but the journal grows forever.

6. Rip out the old desk and put in something built for scale (Database Technology Change). Sometimes the little library building physically cannot grow anymore. Move into a mega-warehouse designed for millions of visitors (DynamoDB, Cassandra, MongoDB). Painful move, but if you're Netflix or Amazon, worth it.

The mark of a good engineer isn't picking the fanciest option. It's trying the cheap and reversible fixes first — hiring look-up assistants and sticking notes on the door — before you split the library into five branches or move buildings.

🔍 Deep-dive on each option

This is where interviews get won. For each option, know exactly what it solves, what it costs, and when it's the wrong answer. Below are the six honest tools, in the order you should reach for them.

1. Read Replicas — the first, cheapest lever

The primary keeps handling writes; you spin up 1–15 asynchronous replicas that stream the WAL/binlog and serve read-only queries. On AWS RDS you can attach up to 15 read replicas per primary (5 on Oracle), across AZs or even cross-region. Aurora goes further — replicas share the same storage layer, so lag is measured in milliseconds. This is the most cost-effective and reversible option, and it should always be your first move for a read-heavy workload.

The catch is replication lag. Replicas are eventually consistent — a user who writes then immediately reads may see stale data. You have three answers to that: (a) send critical read-after-write to the primary; (b) use synchronous replicas at higher latency cost; (c) accept the staleness and design the UX to hide it (e.g., optimistic UI). Replicas do not help write-bound workloads and can make writes marginally slower (WAL shipping overhead).

✅ Reach for it when: read:write ratio ≥ 5:1, DB CPU dominated by SELECT, reporting queries choking OLTP traffic.
⚠ Avoid when: writes are the bottleneck, or business needs strong read-your-writes on the primary path.

2. Caching — absorb the hot reads before they hit the DB

Put a fast in-memory store (Redis, Memcached, ElastiCache, DynamoDB DAX) between the app and the database. Every layer of the stack can cache: CDN (CloudFront) for static + edge-cacheable API responses, application-level in-process cache for tiny lookups, and a distributed cache (Redis) for shared hot data. A well-placed Redis instance handles ~100k–1M ops/sec at sub-millisecond latency and can drop DB load by 80–95% on read-skewed workloads.

There are four honest caching strategies. Cache-aside (a.k.a. lazy loading): the app checks Redis; on miss, fetches from DB, writes it back. Simple, robust, but the first read on a miss is slow and stale data can linger until TTL expires. Write-through: every write goes to Redis and DB in the same operation. Cache is always fresh, but writes are slower and you pay to cache data that may never be read. Write-behind (write-back): write to Redis, async flush to DB — fast writes, but risk of data loss on cache crash. Read-through: cache library owns the fetch; app just calls the cache. The Aurora integrated cache is a form of read-through.

The classic bugs are cache stampede (a hot key expires and a million requests hammer the DB simultaneously — mitigate with probabilistic early refresh or request coalescing), thundering herd on cold start (empty cache after a restart — mitigate with warm-up scripts), and the dual-write consistency problem (DB and cache diverge — mitigate by writing to DB first, invalidating cache, or using CDC to keep them in sync).

✅ Reach for it when: a small set of hot keys serves most traffic (Zipf distribution — very common). Session data, user profiles, product catalogues, feature flags, top-N lists.
⚠ Avoid when: reads are broad and uniformly distributed, or the business can't tolerate any staleness.

3. Partitioning / Sharding — split the data itself

Once even a beefy primary can't hold the working set in memory or absorb the write rate, you have to split the data across nodes. Partitioning and sharding are related but distinct: partitioning divides a table into pieces (usually on the same node — Postgres declarative partitioning, MySQL range partitions); sharding puts each partition on a different physical node. Sharding wins throughput linearly and expands storage without bound, at the cost of large architectural pain.

Two flavours matter in an interview. Range-based sharding keeps data ordered — user IDs 0–1M on Shard A, 1M–2M on Shard B — which makes range queries efficient but is prone to hotspots (newest users on the newest shard = hot). Hash-based sharding hashes the shard key and mods by shard count — even distribution, no hotspots, but range scans have to fan out to every shard and resharding is nightmarish because the mod changes.

The shard key is the single most important decision. It needs (a) high cardinality so you have many possible partitions, and (b) even frequency so no one value dominates. Uber shards trip data by trip_id; Slack shards messages by workspace_id; Instagram shards photos by user_id. Cross-shard queries (joins, group-bys spanning users) become router+scatter+gather operations — slow, and often the reason people give up on sharding and change database technology.

Modern systems avoid the mod-N trap with consistent hashing — the shard key hashes to a point on a ring, and you own the arc up to the next node. Adding a shard only moves ~1/N of the data instead of nearly all of it. DynamoDB, Cassandra, and modern distributed caches all use this approach.

✅ Reach for it when: single-node write ceiling reached, working set no longer fits in RAM, storage nearing physical limits.
⚠ Avoid when: you haven't first exhausted replicas + caching + partitioning within a single node. Sharding is one-way and expensive.

4. CQRS — one model for writes, a different one for reads

Command Query Responsibility Segregation (Greg Young's term, popularised by Martin Fowler) is the idea that the model you use to change data doesn't have to be the same as the model you use to read it. In the small this is a code split — separate command objects from query objects. In the large it becomes two physical stores: a normalised transactional DB for writes (Postgres with FK constraints, ACID transactions) and one or more denormalised read stores optimised per query pattern (Elasticsearch for full-text, Redis for leaderboards, a materialised view in DynamoDB for hot API endpoints).

The two sides stay in sync via domain events: every write publishes an event, projection workers consume the event stream, and each read store rebuilds its slice of the world. The read stores are effectively materialised views — cheap to query, expensive to maintain. This shifts consistency from strong to eventual: after a write, the read side lags by milliseconds to seconds. Fowler is deliberately cautious about CQRS — he says "for most systems CQRS adds risky complexity" and it should live inside a single bounded context, not across a whole system.

Twitter's timeline is the canonical example: writing a tweet inserts one row into the tweets table (write model), then fans out to precomputed Redis timelines per follower (read model). Reading your feed hits the Redis timeline directly — never joins the tweet, follower, and follows tables at read time. That's why the read latency is single-digit milliseconds for a query that would otherwise be a nightmare.

✅ Reach for it when: read and write patterns are fundamentally different (heavy denormalisation on the read side, complex commands on the write side), or read scale needs to grow independently of write scale.
⚠ Avoid when: a simple CRUD app can serve both — CQRS doubles your moving parts.

5. Event Sourcing — the source of truth is a log, not a table

Event sourcing flips the usual persistence model. Instead of storing current state (Order.balance = 42), you store the sequence of events that produced it (OrderCreated, ItemAdded, PaymentReceived, ItemShipped). Current state is a fold over the event log. This gives you three superpowers: a permanent audit trail, the ability to reconstruct any past state (temporal queries), and the ability to replay events to build new projections you didn't design for at first.

Why it helps a database bottleneck: the write path becomes an append-only log — the fastest possible workload for a storage engine. Kafka, Kinesis, EventStoreDB, and DynamoDB Streams handle millions of appends per second because they never seek. Read models are then projections rebuilt by consumers off the log; if a read store gets slow, add another projection or rebuild it with a different schema. In effect, event sourcing lifts the "one true schema" constraint off your database.

The costs are real. Every side effect on external systems (send email, charge card, call third-party API) must be idempotent because events may replay. Business rules that change over time need careful versioning ("bi-temporal" behaviour). Snapshotting is needed so you don't replay millions of events on every read. And developers new to the pattern find it genuinely hard to reason about.

✅ Reach for it when: audit is a requirement (banking, healthcare, accounting), you need to replay history, or write throughput needs to be extreme.
⚠ Avoid when: you have a CRUD app with no audit needs — event sourcing is one of the fastest ways to get in over your head.

6. Database technology change — the nuclear option

Sometimes the honest answer is that the shape of your workload doesn't fit a relational database. Move it. If your data is a huge collection of small, independent key-value writes with predictable access patterns, DynamoDB or Cassandra will handle 100k+ writes/sec at consistent single-digit ms latency out of the box. If it's time-series (metrics, IoT), Timestream or InfluxDB. Graph (social, fraud): Neptune or Neo4j. Full-text: OpenSearch. Analytics: Redshift, Snowflake, or BigQuery.

You lose ACID transactions, joins, and the flexibility of SQL. You gain horizontal scale that Postgres would need a decade of engineering to match. Amazon famously migrated Prime Day workloads off Oracle onto DynamoDB — hundreds of services, over years. Netflix's core viewing history sits in Cassandra; their metadata is in EVCache (Memcached). Discord migrated messages from Cassandra to ScyllaDB. This is not a decision to take in a Slack thread — it's often a multi-quarter project involving dual-writes, back-fills, and shadow reads.

✅ Reach for it when: the workload shape (write-heavy, non-relational, geographically distributed) genuinely doesn't fit SQL, and you've exhausted the earlier options.
⚠ Avoid when: you haven't first proven that no combination of the previous five works. Interviewers penalise "just switch to Mongo" answers.

A mature, scaled service on AWS typically layers several of these at once: CloudFront in front of API Gateway for edge caching; an ALB fronting stateless ECS/Lambda services; ElastiCache Redis for hot data + session; Aurora with read replicas as the OLTP store; DynamoDB for the highest-throughput write paths; Kinesis or MSK carrying domain events between them; and OpenSearch for read-heavy full-text queries. The interviewer wants to hear you assemble those pieces with reasons, not name-drop them.

💻 C# — a cache-aside + read-replica pattern

Here is a small but realistic implementation of the two lowest-cost fixes in the tree: cache-aside on top of a primary + read-replica split. It's the code you'd write on day one of your scaling journey, and it's what an interviewer would ask you to whiteboard for a "just make it faster" question. Idiomatic C# — IDbConnection, IDatabase (StackExchange.Redis), async all the way.

using System;
using System.Data;
using System.Text.Json;
using System.Threading.Tasks;
using Dapper;
using StackExchange.Redis;

// Two connections: one for writes (primary), one for reads (replica).
// The DI container decides which is which. In AWS you'd point at the RDS
// writer endpoint and the RDS reader endpoint respectively.
public interface ISqlConnections {
    IDbConnection Primary();     // RDS writer endpoint
    IDbConnection Replica();     // RDS reader endpoint (round-robin)
}

public class UserRepository {
    private readonly ISqlConnections _sql;
    private readonly IDatabase _cache;      // ElastiCache Redis
    private static readonly TimeSpan Ttl = TimeSpan.FromMinutes(5);
    private static readonly TimeSpan Jitter = TimeSpan.FromSeconds(30);

    public UserRepository(ISqlConnections sql, IConnectionMultiplexer redis) {
        _sql = sql;
        _cache = redis.GetDatabase();
    }

    // ── READ PATH ────────────────────────────────────────────────
    // 1. Try Redis.
    // 2. On miss, hit the read replica (NOT the primary — save it for writes).
    // 3. Back-fill the cache with a TTL + jitter to avoid stampede.
    public async Task<User?> GetAsync(long userId) {
        var key = $"user:{userId}";

        RedisValue cached = await _cache.StringGetAsync(key);
        if (cached.HasValue)
            return JsonSerializer.Deserialize<User>(cached!);

        using var replica = _sql.Replica();
        var user = await replica.QuerySingleOrDefaultAsync<User>(
            "SELECT id, name, email FROM users WHERE id = @id",
            new { id = userId });

        if (user != null) {
            // TTL with jitter (± 30 s) so the cache doesn't expire all at once
            // → thundering herd on the DB when hot keys expire together.
            var ttl = Ttl + TimeSpan.FromSeconds(Random.Shared.Next(-30, 30));
            await _cache.StringSetAsync(key, JsonSerializer.Serialize(user), ttl);
        }
        return user;
    }

    // ── WRITE PATH ───────────────────────────────────────────────
    // 1. Write to the primary. This is the source of truth.
    // 2. Invalidate the cache (write-around, not write-through) — safer
    //    than writing to both because if the DB commit fails, we don't
    //    leave a stale cache entry claiming success.
    public async Task UpdateEmailAsync(long userId, string email) {
        using var primary = _sql.Primary();
        await primary.ExecuteAsync(
            "UPDATE users SET email = @email WHERE id = @id",
            new { id = userId, email });

        // Delete AFTER the DB commit. Any concurrent reader that pulled
        // the stale value between commit and delete will re-cache stale
        // data; a short TTL is our belt-and-braces.
        await _cache.KeyDeleteAsync($"user:{userId}");
    }
}

public record User(long Id, string Name, string Email);

Notice the deliberate small details that impress: write to the primary, read from the replica; cache TTL with jitter to prevent stampede; delete-after-write instead of write-through (write-around) so a failed commit doesn't leave lies in the cache; connection scoping so we don't leak; async everywhere so we don't block threads waiting on IO. Every one of those is a follow-up question waiting to happen.

⚖️ Trade-offs, bottlenecks & likely follow-ups

The technique itself isn't the answer; the answer is the reasoning about when to use which, and the follow-ups an interviewer will fire at you. Here are the questions that reliably come up:

"How do you handle read-your-writes when using replicas?"

Three options, in increasing order of complexity. (1) Route reads that immediately follow a write to the primary — session-sticky "read-your-writes" routing based on a last-write timestamp. (2) Track the WAL/GTID position of your write and only serve replica reads that have caught up past it. (3) Use synchronous replication for that path and accept the write-latency hit. Aurora's ~10 ms lag makes option 1 the pragmatic default.

"What if a cache node fails?"

A cache should be an optimisation, not a dependency. If Redis dies, the app must transparently fall back to the DB — degraded latency, but not an outage. This is one reason cache-aside beats write-behind: write-behind means data lives only in Redis until the flush, and a node crash is data loss. Deploy Redis in cluster mode with replicas + AZ redundancy, and make sure your app tolerates the failover gap.

"How do you pick a shard key?"

Two properties: high cardinality (many possible values) and even frequency (no single value dominates). user_id is usually good; timestamp is usually bad (all new writes hit the newest shard). If your dominant query is "get everything for user X", shard by user_id and every request hits exactly one shard. If your dominant query crosses users, you'll have scatter-gather forever.

"How do you rebalance shards without downtime?"

Consistent hashing minimises data movement to ~1/N when adding a shard. On top of that, do it online: (1) start dual-writing to old and new shards for the moving range; (2) copy historical data with a back-fill job; (3) verify checksums; (4) flip reads over; (5) drop the old copy. Vitess, Citus, and Dynamo/DynamoDB all implement variants of this.

"What are the failure modes of CQRS + event sourcing?"

Three classic ones. (1) Projection lag — the read side is stale, users see old data. Mitigation: monitor lag and back-pressure writes if it grows. (2) Poison messages — an event that always crashes the consumer blocks the whole log. Mitigation: dead-letter queues and idempotent consumers. (3) Schema evolution — old events in a different format than the code now expects. Mitigation: version events and keep the reader backwards-compatible forever.

"When would you actually change database technology?"

When the workload shape is a bad fit for relational: mostly independent writes, no joins in the hot path, extreme write throughput, or a data model that fights the schema (nested documents, graphs, time-series). Also when operating cost of scaling the current DB exceeds migrating. Amazon moved Prime Day off Oracle onto DynamoDB precisely for this — the operational cost of scaling Oracle further was multiples of the migration cost.

"How do you detect the database is the actual bottleneck?"

Look at the four signals in order: (1) DB CPU + IOPS saturated in metrics; (2) query latency p99 rising; (3) connection pool exhausted; (4) replication lag. If the DB is idle and the app tier is CPU-pegged, the problem is elsewhere. If a specific query dominates, first fix the query (index, rewrite, denormalise) before you touch architecture — an added index has bought many teams a year of runway.

🗣️ Interview cheat sheet

"Databases become the bottleneck in every serious system. I'd traverse the options cheap-to-expensive: read replicas and caching first — reversible, days of work, huge wins on read-heavy load. If writes are the problem, partitioning inside the box, then sharding across nodes with a shard key that has high cardinality and even distribution. If read and write patterns diverge, CQRS — separate stores tuned per query. If audit or replay is a requirement, event sourcing — the source of truth becomes an append-only log. And only if none of the shapes fit relational, change database technology — DynamoDB, Cassandra, or the right specialised store."

Say-this bullets that win the round

  • Prove it's the DB first. "Before I touch architecture, I'd confirm DB CPU is saturated and identify the top-N slow queries. An added index has saved more teams than every framework combined."
  • Read-heavy vs write-heavy. "The whole tree branches on read:write ratio. Replicas + cache dominate the ≥5:1 case; sharding and CQRS dominate the write-bound case."
  • Cheap and reversible first. "I go up the ladder — days of work before quarters of work, reversible before one-way. Sharding is architectural surgery; I'd exhaust simpler options first."
  • Shard keys: cardinality + distribution. "user_id is usually good, timestamp usually bad — the shard key must give you many, evenly-populated partitions."
  • Consistency is a design choice. "Replicas, cache, CQRS, and event sourcing all shift you from strong to eventual consistency. That's not a bug — it's the mechanism that makes them fast. Design the UX to hide the lag."
  • The nuclear option is real. "Sometimes the honest answer is to change database technology. Amazon migrated off Oracle onto DynamoDB for Prime Day. That's not a Slack-thread decision, but it's a valid endpoint."
