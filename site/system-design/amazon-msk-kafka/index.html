🎯 Real-world scenario

What we're building & where it's used

Amazon MSK (Managed Streaming for Apache Kafka) is a fully managed service that runs open-source Apache Kafka for you. So "how MSK works internally" is really two layers: (1) Kafka's internals — the distributed, replicated, append-only commit log that is the heart of the system — and (2) the managed layer MSK wraps around it — provisioning brokers across Availability Zones, running the ZooKeeper/KRaft metadata quorum, auto-recovering failed brokers, encryption, IAM auth, and tiered storage. MSK owns the control plane (create/update/delete clusters); you use the standard Kafka data plane (produce/consume) unchanged.

Kafka is the backbone of event-driven and streaming architectures: it's the durable, replayable "central nervous system" that decouples producers from consumers. Real uses everywhere — LinkedIn (where Kafka was born) for activity streams; Uber and Netflix for trillions of events/day of telemetry and real-time pipelines; log aggregation, change-data-capture, metrics, stream processing, and microservice event buses. Companies pick MSK specifically to avoid the operational pain of self-managing Kafka + ZooKeeper — patching, broker replacement, AZ-aware placement, and scaling storage.

Why it's a great interview topic: Kafka forces you to reason about the single most important primitive in distributed systems — the replicated log — plus partitioning for scale, the ISR replication protocol for durability, consumer groups for parallel consumption, and the clever throughput tricks (sequential disk I/O, page cache, zero-copy) that let one cluster push millions of messages per second on commodity disks.

🧑‍🎓 In laymen terms

The busy restaurant with a never-ending ticket rail

Imagine a huge, busy restaurant kitchen. Waiters keep taking orders and the cooks keep cooking. If a waiter had to walk each order over to the exact cook, hand it to them, and wait — the whole kitchen would grind to a halt. So instead, they use a long metal rail above the pass: waiters just clip the order tickets onto the rail in the order they took them, and cooks grab the next ticket whenever they're free. Everyone works at their own speed, and nobody blocks anybody else. That rail is exactly what this system is.

Now imagine the restaurant is so busy that one rail isn't enough. So they put up many rails side by side, and every table is assigned to a specific rail — table 7's tickets always go on rail 3, table 12's on rail 5. This way many cooks can work in parallel, and the tickets for any one table still stay in the right order.

Two more clever ideas make it magical. First, the rail never forgets a ticket — even after the food is served, a copy stays on the rail for a week. If a new cook joins tomorrow, they can look back and see everything that happened. Second, every ticket is photocopied onto two backup rails in two other buildings. If one whole building loses power, the kitchen keeps going without missing an order.

This is how LinkedIn, Uber, and Netflix move billions of little events every day — every tap, click, ride request, or "video started" is just another ticket clipped onto the rail, waiting to be picked up by whoever needs it. Amazon's version simply promises: we'll put up the rails, replace them when they break, and you just clip and grab.

📋 Requirements & scale

Requirements and back-of-the-envelope math

Functional requirements

  • Publish/subscribe — producers append messages to topics; many consumers read independently at their own position.
  • Durable & replayable — messages persist by retention policy (time/size) regardless of consumption; consumers can rewind to any offset.
  • Ordered within a partition — strict order per partition (not across a topic).
  • Horizontal scale — add partitions and brokers to scale throughput; add consumers to scale reads.
  • At-least-once / exactly-once delivery semantics, configurable.

Non-functional requirements (the ones that shape the design)

  • High throughput — millions of messages/sec on modest hardware via sequential I/O + batching.
  • Durability — survive broker and whole-AZ failure via replication (typically RF=3 across 3 AZs).
  • Low, predictable latency — single-digit ms produce/consume from the page cache.
  • Elastic storage — retain data for days to forever without over-provisioning brokers (tiered storage).
  • Availability — automatic broker recovery; no single point of failure in the data path.

🏗️ High-level architecture

How the pieces fit together

A Kafka cluster is a set of brokers (servers) that store partitions. A topic is a logical stream split into N partitions; each partition is an append-only commit log living on one broker as the leader, with follower replicas on other brokers. Producers append to the tail of a partition; each message gets a monotonically increasing offset. Consumers track their own offset and read forward — reads don't destroy data, so many independent consumers can replay the same log. A metadata quorum (ZooKeeper in older versions, now KRaft — Kafka's built-in Raft controllers) tracks cluster membership, partition leadership, and configuration, and elects the controller.

MSK wraps this: it provisions brokers across AZs (each AZ in its own VPC subnet, minimum one broker per AZ), runs the ZooKeeper/KRaft nodes for you at no extra cost, and auto-detects and replaces failed brokers — reusing the old broker's storage where possible so Kafka has less data to re-replicate, and keeping the same broker endpoints so clients reconnect transparently. You get the open-source Kafka data plane; MSK runs everything around it.

Produce (append to leader) Replication to followers (ISR) Consume (read by offset) Metadata / control

The produce path

  1. Producer picks a partition (by key hash, or round-robin) and sends a batch of messages to that partition's leader broker.
  2. Leader appends the batch to its log segment (sequential write) and assigns offsets.
  3. Followers in the ISR pull the new records and append them to their own logs.
  4. With acks=all, once min.insync.replicas have the data the leader acknowledges the write — now it's durable against AZ loss.

The consume path

  1. A consumer group subscribes; each partition is assigned to exactly one consumer in the group (parallelism = partition count).
  2. The consumer fetches from the partition leader starting at its committed offset.
  3. Broker serves the read straight from the OS page cache via zero-copy — no data loss to userspace.
  4. Consumer periodically commits its offset (to an internal Kafka topic) so it can resume after a restart; it can also rewind to reprocess.

🔍 Component deep-dive

The commit log: partitions, segments & offsets

Everything in Kafka is a partitioned, append-only log. A partition is stored on disk as a series of segment files; new records are only ever appended to the active segment, and each gets a monotonically increasing 64-bit offset. This append-only design is the source of Kafka's speed: writes are sequential disk I/O, which on spinning disks and SSDs alike is dramatically faster than random writes — Kafka "turns a bursty stream of random message writes into linear writes." Old segments are deleted (or compacted) by retention policy — by time or size — completely independent of whether anyone has consumed them. That's what makes Kafka replayable: the log is the source of truth, not a queue that empties on read.

Replication & the ISR (the durability heart)

Each partition has one leader and replication-factor − 1 followers on other brokers. All reads and writes go through the leader; followers continuously fetch to stay caught up. The set of replicas that are fully caught up is the In-Sync Replica set (ISR). Durability is governed by two knobs: producer acks (0 = fire-and-forget, 1 = leader only, all = wait for all ISR) and min.insync.replicas (how many replicas must have the record before an acks=all write is accepted). The classic safe config — RF=3, acks=all, min.insync.replicas=2 — means a write survives losing any one broker/AZ with no data loss. If the leader dies, the controller elects a new leader from the ISR, guaranteeing the new leader has all acknowledged data.

Consumer groups & offset management

A consumer group is how Kafka scales reads and load-balances: partitions are distributed across the group's members, each partition owned by exactly one consumer, so parallelism is capped by partition count. Different groups read the same topic independently at their own offsets (pub/sub), while consumers within one group split the work (queue-like). Kafka uses manual/committed offset management — consumers control when they commit, enabling at-least-once (commit after processing) or at-most-once (commit before) semantics and fine-grained retry/failure handling. Offsets are stored in an internal __consumer_offsets topic. When members join/leave, a rebalance reassigns partitions.

Why Kafka is so fast: page cache, zero-copy, batching

Three tricks give Kafka its throughput. (1) Sequential I/O — append-only logs mean the disk head never seeks. (2) OS page cache — Kafka doesn't maintain its own cache in the JVM heap; it writes to the filesystem and lets the OS page cache serve hot reads, so recently produced data is served from RAM. (3) Zero-copy — on consume, Kafka uses the sendfile syscall to copy bytes straight from the page cache to the network socket, bypassing userspace entirely (no copy into the JVM, no serialization on the read path). Combined with batching and compression of message sets, this is how a single broker handles enormous throughput on commodity hardware.

Metadata: ZooKeeper → KRaft

Kafka needs a consistent place to store cluster metadata — which brokers exist, who leads each partition, topic configs — and to elect a controller. Historically this was Apache ZooKeeper (a separate quorum). Modern Kafka replaces it with KRaft, a built-in Raft-based consensus among a set of Kafka controller nodes, removing the external ZooKeeper dependency, simplifying operations, and scaling to far more partitions. MSK runs whichever your cluster version uses — and manages the controllers for you at no additional cost.

What MSK adds on top

MSK is the control plane: it provisions and patches brokers, spreads them across AZs (each in its own subnet), runs ZooKeeper/KRaft, and auto-recovers failed brokers — replacing an unhealthy broker while reusing its storage to minimize re-replication, and preserving broker endpoints so clients reconnect seamlessly. It adds encryption (at rest via KMS, in transit via TLS), IAM authentication, CloudWatch/Prometheus monitoring, and tiered storage (below). MSK Serverless removes capacity planning entirely — you don't size brokers, MSK manages them and you provision at the cluster level.

Tiered storage (KIP-405)

Normally each broker holds all its partition data on local EBS volumes, so retaining more data means bigger disks or more brokers. MSK tiered storage keeps hot, recent data on a performance-optimized local tier and, once a topic hits its retention limit, automatically moves older log segments to a low-cost remote tier — scaling to virtually unlimited storage with no infra to manage. You still read old data in exact production order through the same Kafka APIs (expect a small latency bump on the first bytes, then near-local speeds streaming sequentially). A big operational win: because cold data lives remotely, partition rebalances and broker recovery are faster — there's far less on-disk data to replicate between brokers.

🧮 The DSA behind it

Data structures & algorithms

Kafka is a beautiful application of a few core structures. The partition is the classic log data structure — an ordered, append-only sequence. Lookups by offset use a sparse index per segment (offset → byte position) for O(log n) seeks. Leader election and metadata use Raft consensus (KRaft). Producer partitioning uses hashing; consumer assignment is a partitioning/bin-packing problem.

💻 C# implementation

The interview-relevant code is the producer with durability settings and a consumer-group consumer with manual offset commit, using the Confluent.Kafka .NET client (works against MSK unchanged). Note the durability knobs (acks=all, idempotence) and the at-least-once commit-after-processing pattern.

using Confluent.Kafka;

// ---- PRODUCER: durable, idempotent, keyed for per-key ordering ----
var producerConfig = new ProducerConfig
{
    BootstrapServers = "b-1.mycluster.kafka.us-east-1.amazonaws.com:9098,...",
    // Durability: wait for ALL in-sync replicas before ack (no data loss on AZ failure).
    Acks = Acks.All,
    EnableIdempotence = true,        // exactly-once append: no duplicates on retry
    MessageSendMaxRetries = 5,
    // Throughput: batch messages before sending (linger + size).
    LingerMs = 10,
    BatchSize = 64 * 1024,
    CompressionType = CompressionType.Lz4,
    // MSK IAM auth over TLS (SASL/OAUTHBEARER via the IAM extension).
    SecurityProtocol = SecurityProtocol.SaslSsl,
    SaslMechanism = SaslMechanism.OAuthBearer
};

using var producer = new ProducerBuilder<string, string>(producerConfig).Build();

// Key = userId ⇒ all events for a user hash to the SAME partition ⇒ ordered.
var result = await producer.ProduceAsync("user-events",
    new Message<string, string> { Key = "user-42", Value = """{"event":"login"}""" });
Console.WriteLine($"→ partition {result.Partition} @ offset {result.Offset}");


// ---- CONSUMER: consumer group + AT-LEAST-ONCE (commit AFTER processing) ----
var consumerConfig = new ConsumerConfig
{
    BootstrapServers = producerConfig.BootstrapServers,
    GroupId = "analytics-workers",         // members split the topic's partitions
    AutoOffsetReset = AutoOffsetReset.Earliest,
    EnableAutoCommit = false,             // we commit manually for correctness
    SecurityProtocol = SecurityProtocol.SaslSsl,
    SaslMechanism = SaslMechanism.OAuthBearer
};

using var consumer = new ConsumerBuilder<string, string>(consumerConfig).Build();
consumer.Subscribe("user-events");

while (!ct.IsCancellationRequested)
{
    var cr = consumer.Consume(ct);       // fetch from partition leader at current offset
    Process(cr.Message.Value);              // do the work FIRST...
    consumer.Commit(cr);                     // ...THEN commit ⇒ at-least-once (never lose a msg)
}
// Commit-before-process would be at-most-once. Exactly-once needs
// transactions (InitTransactions/BeginTransaction/CommitTransaction).

The two takeaways: (1) Acks=All + EnableIdempotence on the producer is the durability contract that pairs with broker-side min.insync.replicas=2; and (2) the order of Process then Commit is exactly what makes delivery at-least-once — commit before processing and a crash loses the message (at-most-once). Because MSK is plain Kafka, this same code runs against any Kafka cluster; only the bootstrap endpoints and the IAM/SASL auth differ.

⚖️ Trade-offs, bottlenecks & likely follow-ups

Partition count is the master dial. More partitions = more parallelism and throughput, but also more open files, more memory, longer leader-election/rebalance times, and more end-to-end latency. You can add partitions but not remove them, and adding them breaks key-ordering guarantees for existing keys (they may re-hash to new partitions). Size partitions for peak consumer parallelism up front.

Ordering is per-partition only. Global ordering across a topic isn't possible with parallelism. If you need ordering, key your messages so related events share a partition — and accept that a single hot key becomes a throughput ceiling.

Durability vs latency. acks=all + min.insync.replicas=2 is safe but slower (waits for replicas); acks=1 is faster but can lose data if the leader dies before followers catch up. Name this trade explicitly.

Consumer lag & rebalances. If consumers can't keep up, lag grows and you risk hitting retention (data deleted before consumed). Monitor consumer lag; scale consumers up to (but not beyond) partition count. Frequent rebalances (from slow processing or churn) stall consumption — tune max.poll.interval.ms and use cooperative rebalancing.

Why not SQS/Kinesis instead? SQS is a queue (no replay, no ordering across the queue, message deleted on ack); Kinesis is AWS-native and simpler but less flexible and with its own limits. Kafka/MSK wins when you need replayable logs, high fan-out to many independent consumers, an open ecosystem (Connect, Streams), and very high throughput.

Q: How does Kafka guarantee no data loss?

Replication factor 3 across AZs, acks=all, and min.insync.replicas=2: a write is only acknowledged once at least two replicas have it, and new leaders are only elected from the in-sync replica set — so the new leader always has every acknowledged record.

Q: What decides consumer parallelism?

Partition count. Within a consumer group each partition is owned by exactly one consumer, so you can't have more active consumers than partitions — extra consumers sit idle. Size partitions for your peak parallelism.

Q: Why is Kafka so fast despite writing to disk?

Append-only logs make writes sequential (no seeks); the OS page cache serves hot reads from RAM; and zero-copy (sendfile) streams bytes from page cache straight to the socket, skipping userspace. Batching and compression amortize per-message overhead.

Q: What does MSK actually manage vs. plain Kafka?

MSK owns the control plane: broker provisioning across AZs, ZooKeeper/KRaft, patching, automatic broker recovery (reusing storage to cut re-replication), encryption, IAM auth, monitoring, and tiered storage. The data plane is unmodified open-source Kafka, so your clients and tooling work unchanged.
