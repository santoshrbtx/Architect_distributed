Consistency Models

From linearizability down to eventual consistency — the ladder of promises a distributed store can make about what your reads will see.

🎯 Real-world scenario

What exactly does a "read" promise?

When you write a value to a replicated store and then read it back — maybe from a different replica — what are you guaranteed to see? The latest value? Some past value? Your own last write but not others'? A consistency model is the precise contract that answers this. It's a safety property that "declares what a system can do" — which orderings of reads and writes the system is allowed to expose to clients. Choosing one is choosing where you sit on the spectrum between correctness and performance/availability.

This is a top-tier interview topic because it's where the CAP and PACELC trade-offs become concrete. Strong models (linearizability) give you a simple mental model — the system behaves like a single machine — but cost coordination latency and availability. Weak models (eventual consistency) are fast and always-on but force your application to tolerate stale and out-of-order reads. Naming the model you need, and why, is what separates a candidate who has "used a database" from one who can design one.

🧑‍🎓 In laymen terms

You change your address at the bank — how fast does everyone know?

Suppose you walk into the bank on Monday morning and update your home address. The bank has many branches, many tellers, and many little screens. The interesting question is — how fast does the new address show up everywhere? That is what consistency is really about. It's a promise the bank makes about how quickly your update becomes visible to the next person who looks.

At the strictest end, the bank could freeze the whole world for a second and shout your new address across every branch before anyone else does anything. Every single teller in every city would see the new address the instant you finished writing it. This is beautiful for correctness — nobody is ever confused — but it's slow, and if one branch's phone line is down, the whole bank has to wait. Your ATM balance works like this. It has to.

At the loosest end, the bank just whispers your new address to the branch you visited, and lets it slowly trickle to the other branches over the next few minutes. If your husband walks into a different branch two seconds later, the teller there might still see the old address. It'll catch up eventually — usually within a blink — but for a brief moment different tellers see different things. This is fast, always-on, and never freezes. It's how Instagram like counts or YouTube view counts work — you don't really care if the number lags for a few seconds.

In between are gentler promises. "You will always see your own latest change, even if others still see the old one" (this is why your own tweet appears instantly to you). Or "If A happened before B, everyone will see them in that order" (this is why replies never appear before the message they reply to). Choosing the right promise means asking: how much staleness would upset a user? A bank balance — zero. A like count — nobody notices.

📋 Two families of guarantee

Single-object models vs transactional isolation

A crucial distinction interviewers test: "consistency" means two related-but-different things.

FamilyGovernsExamples
Consistency models (this page's focus)The ordering of individual reads/writes on single objects across replicas.Linearizability, sequential, causal, eventual.
Transaction isolation levelsHow concurrent multi-operation transactions interleave (a database/ACID concern).Serializable, snapshot isolation, read committed.

They meet at the top: strict serializability = serializability (transactions) + linearizability (real-time order) — the strongest guarantee a distributed database can offer. Isolation levels are often defined by which anomalies ("phenomena") they forbid — dirty reads, non-repeatable reads, phantoms, write skew — formalized as forbidden cycles in a dependency graph.

🏗️ The hierarchy

The ladder from strongest to weakest

Consistency models form a partial order: a stronger model forbids everything a weaker one does, plus more. Stronger = easier to reason about, more coordination, worse latency/availability. Here's the mental ladder to draw on the whiteboard:

The most important boundary is causal consistency: it's the strongest model that can still be achieved while remaining available during a partition (the CAP-available frontier). Everything above it (sequential, linearizable) requires giving up availability under partition.

🔍 Each model, with a one-line litmus test

Linearizability (a.k.a. strong / atomic / external consistency)

Every operation appears to take effect instantaneously at some point between its call and its return, and this order respects real time: if write W finishes before read R begins (wall-clock), R must see W or something newer. The system behaves as if there's a single copy of the data. This is CAP's "C". Litmus: once anyone sees the new value, everyone does, immediately. Cost: a coordination round-trip (consensus/quorum) per op. Used by etcd, ZooKeeper, Spanner.

Sequential consistency

There exists one global order of all operations that respects each process's own program order — but it need not match real time. So two clients agree on an order, but a read may return a stale value as long as everyone sees the same "story." Weaker than linearizable (drops the real-time constraint), stronger than causal. Rare as a database offering; more a theoretical rung and a memory-model concept.

Causal consistency

Operations that are causally related (this write happened-before that one, à la Lamport) are seen in the same order by everyone; concurrent (unrelated) operations may be seen in different orders on different replicas. This preserves intuitive cause-and-effect — you never see a reply before the message it answers — without global coordination. It's the sweet spot for available systems (e.g., COPS, MongoDB causal sessions). Litmus: "if I saw your answer, I must also see the question."

Session guarantees (client-centric)

  • Read-Your-Writes: you always see your own prior writes (e.g., you post a comment and it's there on refresh). Prevents the "did my save work?" confusion.
  • Monotonic Reads: once you've read a value, later reads never return an older one — no going back in time.
  • Monotonic Writes: your writes are applied in the order you issued them.
  • Writes-Follow-Reads: a write after a read is ordered after the write that read observed.

These are cheap, per-client promises usually implemented with sticky sessions or version tokens, and they dramatically improve UX on top of an otherwise eventually-consistent store.

Eventual consistency

The only promise: if writes stop, all replicas eventually converge to the same value. No ordering guarantee in the meantime — reads can be stale, out of order, or flap between values. Cheapest and most available (AP). Used by DNS, Cassandra/DynamoDB (default), S3 cross-region. Convergence needs a conflict-resolution rule: last-write-wins, vector clocks, or CRDTs.

💻 C# — read-your-writes over an eventual store

A common real pattern: the backing store is eventually consistent, but you must guarantee a user sees their own write. You attach a monotonic version token to the session and refuse to serve a replica read that's behind it — upgrading to the primary if needed.

// Read-your-writes on top of an eventually-consistent replica set,
// using a per-session "last write version" token.
public sealed class SessionConsistentStore
{
    private readonly IReplica _primary;
    private readonly IReadOnlyList<IReplica> _replicas;

    public WriteToken Write(string key, string value)
    {
        long version = _primary.Put(key, value);   // monotonically increasing
        return new WriteToken(key, version);        // hand back to the session
    }

    public string Read(string key, WriteToken? session)
    {
        // If the caller has seen version V, only trust a replica that's caught up.
        if (session is { } tok && tok.Key == key)
        {
            var fresh = _replicas.FirstOrDefault(r => r.VersionOf(key) >= tok.Version);
            // Fall back to the primary if no replica has caught up yet.
            return (fresh ?? _primary).Get(key);   // guarantees read-your-writes
        }
        // No session constraint -> cheapest eventual read from any replica.
        return _replicas[0].Get(key);
    }
}
// This is monotonic-reads + read-your-writes WITHOUT paying for global
// linearizability — the pragmatic middle of the ladder most apps actually want.

Real systems express these knobs declaratively: Cassandra's ConsistencyLevel, MongoDB's readConcern/writeConcern and causal-consistent sessions, and DynamoDB's ConsistentRead flag are all this idea productized.

⚖️ Trade-offs, bottlenecks & likely follow-ups

"Difference between linearizability and serializability?"

Linearizability is a single-object, real-time ordering guarantee (recency). Serializability is a multi-object transaction guarantee (some serial equivalent), with no real-time requirement. Combine both and you get strict serializability — the strongest.

"What's the strongest model you can have while staying available under a partition?"

Causal consistency (and the session guarantees below it). Anything stronger — sequential, linearizable — requires coordination that a partitioned minority can't do, so it must sacrifice availability.

"Give me a concrete eventual-consistency bug."

You update your profile photo, the write lands on replica A, then your next request hits replica B which hasn't replicated yet and shows the old photo — violating read-your-writes. Fix with sticky sessions or a version token, as in the code above.

"What is write skew?"

The signature anomaly snapshot isolation allows: two transactions read an overlapping snapshot, update disjoint rows, and both commit, together breaking an invariant neither would break alone. Only serializable prevents it.

"How do eventually-consistent stores resolve conflicts?"

Last-write-wins (timestamp — simple but can lose data), vector clocks (detect concurrency, surface conflicts to the app), or CRDTs (data types that merge deterministically regardless of order).

🗣️ Interview cheat sheet

30-second pitch: "A consistency model is the contract for what a read can return. The ladder runs from linearizable — every read sees the latest write in real-time order, like a single machine, but needs coordination — down through sequential, causal, session guarantees, to eventual, which only promises replicas converge someday. Causal is the strongest model you can keep while staying available under a partition. For transactions the parallel ladder is read-committed → snapshot isolation → serializable → strict serializable. I pick the weakest model that still meets the UX: strong for money and your-own-edits, eventual for global feeds."
  • Ladder: Strict-serializable → Linearizable → Sequential → Causal → Session (RYW/monotonic) → Eventual.
  • Linearizable = recency + real-time, single object = CAP's "C". Serializable = transaction ordering.
  • Causal = the available-under-partition frontier; preserves cause→effect without global sync.
  • Session guarantees (read-your-writes, monotonic reads) are cheap wins on eventual stores.
  • Snapshot isolation ≠ serializable — it allows write skew.
  • Eventual converges via LWW / vector clocks / CRDTs.
