🎯 Real-world scenario

What we're modelling & why it's an interview classic

You type https://app.example.com/orders/42 in your browser and half a second later a rendered page appears. In between, your packet crosses a dozen trust boundaries — the browser cache, the operating system's stub resolver, your ISP's recursive DNS, the DNS root and the .com TLD, Cloudflare's authoritative name servers, Cloudflare's Anycast edge (which is also your CDN, WAF, and DDoS shield), the public internet, an AWS Application Load Balancer, a Kubernetes / ECS ingress, and finally your application pod. Every hop makes a decision: cache, block, route, terminate TLS, add a header, or forward.

This is one of the most-asked foundational interview scenarios because every senior candidate is expected to sketch it on a whiteboard without hesitation. Real products that live exactly on this pattern: Shopify, Discord, and DoorDash (Cloudflare → AWS), Netflix (Open Connect + AWS ALB), Airbnb and Stripe (multi-CDN in front of AWS/GCP). Companies build it this way because it lets them scale reads at the edge, absorb DDoS before it reaches their VPC, roll out TLS certs centrally, and keep origin IPs private.

🧑‍🎓 In laymen terms

How this works if you've never heard the words "server" or "load balancer"

Imagine you're sending a letter to a friend in a huge apartment complex. You know their name — "Priya at Sunrise Towers" — but not their exact flat number. The postal system has to figure that out for you.

When you type example.com in your browser, the same thing happens under the hood. The name example.com is friendly for humans, but computers on the internet only talk to each other using numbers, like 203.0.113.42. So the first job is to look up the number for the name — like flipping open a phonebook. That's what DNS does. Your computer asks its neighbour ("your ISP" — the company that gives you internet) if it knows the number. If not, the ISP asks the internet's master directory, which walks up the tree: "who owns .com? Who owns example.com inside .com?" Bit by bit it finds the answer.

Now here's a twist. The number your computer gets back doesn't lead to your friend's flat directly. It leads to a front desk — a huge one, run by a company like Cloudflare, with copies sitting in every major city in the world. That front desk does three things before your letter goes any further. First, it looks in a drawer of common replies — if someone else just asked for the same page (say, the homepage), it hands over the copy immediately without disturbing the office at the back. That's called a cache. Second, it reads your letter for anything suspicious — a burglar disguised as a customer, a stack of forged requests — and throws those away. That's the firewall (WAF). Third, if the letter is real and not cached, it forwards it to the real office at the back — hidden behind a locked door only the front desk knows about.

The real office isn't one person either. It's a team of clerks, and a supervisor at the door decides which clerk takes each letter based on how busy they are. That supervisor is the load balancer. The clerk that finally handles your letter is the application. When they write the reply, it goes back the same way — clerk → supervisor → front desk → your computer.

Why is this hard? Because millions of people send letters at the same second, and the front desk has to reject fake ones, cache smart, translate languages, and never lose a real letter. Every household name — Instagram, Uber, Netflix, WhatsApp — works this way. When you tap "like" on Instagram, your tap runs through this exact chain in under 200 milliseconds.

Tiny concrete example: Ravi in Bangalore types zomato.com. His phone asks Airtel (his ISP), which asks the internet directory tree, and gets back an Anycast number owned by Cloudflare. His packet hits Cloudflare's Mumbai front desk. Cloudflare has a fresh copy of Zomato's homepage in its drawer — 3 milliseconds later, Ravi sees the page. Zomato's actual servers in AWS never even knew Ravi asked.

📋 Requirements & scale

What the system has to do, and how fast

Functional

  • Resolve a hostname (domain + subdomain) to an IP — for every request that isn't already in a cache.
  • Terminate TLS at the edge (Cloudflare) and, optionally, re-encrypt end-to-end to the origin ALB.
  • Cache eligible responses close to the user; serve stale-while-revalidate on origin failure.
  • Filter malicious traffic (OWASP Top-10, bot, rate-limit, DDoS) before it costs origin CPU.
  • Load-balance surviving requests across a fleet of healthy application instances.
  • Route by host (which app), path (which service), and sometimes header/cookie.

Back-of-the-envelope

A medium SaaS like Notion handles ~1M requests/sec at peak. If Cloudflare caches 70% at the edge, only ~300k RPS actually touch AWS. The ALB fans that across ~500 pods, so ~600 RPS/pod — perfectly reasonable for a well-tuned service. DNS TTL of 60 seconds on the apex means at most ~17k DNS queries/sec against the authoritative servers even at that peak — trivial for Anycast.

🏗️ High-level architecture

The end-to-end picture

The flow is best thought of as five trust boundaries, each of which can decide unilaterally to short-circuit the request. Read the diagram left-to-right; the labels above the arrows are the wire protocol on that hop.

Two flows are actually happening: a DNS control-plane flow (top row, UDP/53, ~50 ms cold, sub-millisecond warm) that gives the browser an IP to connect to, and a data-plane flow (bottom row, HTTPS over TCP/443) that carries the actual HTTP request. The DNS flow returns an Anycast IP owned by Cloudflare — the browser has no idea AWS is behind it. This is what lets Cloudflare hide origin IPs and absorb DDoS.

🔍 Component deep-dive — every trust boundary, examined

1. Domain, subdomain, and DNS delegation

A fully-qualified name like app.example.com. reads right-to-left. The trailing dot is the root zone. Then comes com — the TLD, whose zone is operated by Verisign. Then example — a domain registered to your company. Finally app — a subdomain, which can either live in the same zone as example.com or be delegated to a different set of name servers via NS records. Delegation is how a startup can run its marketing site on Vercel (example.com) while pointing api.example.com at AWS Route 53 — the two subtrees literally sit in different infrastructure. The key record types you'll be quizzed on:

TTL is the contract with every downstream cache: the record must not be re-used past this many seconds. Long TTLs (86400) reduce query load; short TTLs (60) enable fast failover. That's why load balancer DNS entries typically use 60 s.

2. The ISP recursive resolver

Your ISP (Airtel, Comcast, or a public one like Cloudflare's 1.1.1.1) runs a recursive resolver. It doesn't own any records — it walks the tree on your behalf and caches everything it sees. From a cold cache, it makes three UDP queries: to a root server (Anycast, ~10–30 ms), to a .com TLD server (Anycast, ~20 ms), and to Cloudflare's authoritative NS (~20 ms), totalling ~50–100 ms. On a warm resolver, the answer is served from cache in under 5 ms. This is the single biggest reason DNS feels instant most of the time. The resolver also enforces DNSSEC validation if configured — it walks the signature chain from the root's KSK down to the record's RRSIG and returns SERVFAIL on any tampering.

3. Cloudflare authoritative DNS + Anycast

When your domain is on Cloudflare, its authoritative name servers respond to the recursive resolver — but not with the origin IP. For any record marked "proxied" (the orange cloud in the dashboard), Cloudflare returns an Anycast IP it owns. Anycast means the same IP is announced via BGP from every Cloudflare PoP (330+ cities as of 2024). The internet's routing fabric naturally sends the client packet to the nearest one. This gives Cloudflare three superpowers at once: it hides the origin IP (attackers can't target AWS directly), it puts a caching/security layer within ~10 ms of every internet user on earth, and it absorbs DDoS at the edge — a 388 Tbps combined capacity, larger than any single origin could survive.

4. TLS termination at the edge

The browser opens a TCP handshake to the Anycast IP, then negotiates TLS. In TLS 1.3 the handshake is 1 RTT (or 0-RTT with a Pre-Shared Key). The client sends a Server Name Indication (SNI) extension — server_name = app.example.com — which lets Cloudflare's edge (a single IP hosting thousands of domains) pick the right certificate. Cloudflare then either serves the response entirely from cache or opens a second TLS connection to the origin ALB. That origin-side connection uses the customer's own cert on the ALB, giving genuine end-to-end encryption. Encrypted Client Hello (ECH) is the newer version that also encrypts the SNI so an on-path observer can't even see which site you're visiting.

5. WAF and bot management

The WAF sits inline with the TLS-terminated request stream. Cloudflare's WAF ships with the OWASP Core Rule Set (SQLi, XSS, RCE, path traversal, LFI) plus its own Managed Ruleset tuned from patterns Cloudflare sees across ~20% of all internet traffic. Rules fire on request headers, URI, body, and JA3/JA4 TLS fingerprints for bot detection. Rate-limiting (per IP, per session, per header) rejects abusive clients before they cost origin CPU. Anything the WAF blocks never reaches your VPC — this is the primary cost-saver at scale.

6. CDN caching decisions

For every request Cloudflare hits your origin for, the response is potentially cached at the edge. The cache key defaults to (host, scheme, path, query) and can be customised. Static assets (images, JS bundles) hit almost 100%. HTML with dynamic personalisation typically doesn't cache, but you can use Edge Side Includes or stale-while-revalidate to soften origin load spikes. Cache-Control and CDN-Cache-Control headers from your origin decide freshness; Vary headers key on things like Accept-Encoding or Accept-Language.

7. The origin AWS Application Load Balancer

Cloudflare's PoP now opens a TLS connection to your ALB's public DNS name (something like my-alb-123.us-east-1.elb.amazonaws.com). The ALB operates at OSI Layer 7 — it can read the HTTP request. Its behavior is defined by listeners (a port + protocol like 443/HTTPS) and rules. Each rule is condition → action: for example, Host = app.example.com AND Path = /orders/* → forward to target group "orders-api". Target groups map to registered targets (EC2, ECS tasks, EKS pods, IPs, or Lambda), and the ALB does per-target health checks so an unhealthy pod stops receiving traffic within seconds. TLS termination on the ALB uses an ACM cert; SNI lets one ALB host many domains. Optionally an AWS WAF WebACL attaches to the ALB as a defense-in-depth layer.

8. Kubernetes ingress / service mesh

If the target group is an EKS cluster, the ALB forwards to the Ingress Controller (nginx, AWS Load Balancer Controller, or Envoy). The ingress applies its own path/host rules, terminates or passes through mTLS if you run Istio/Linkerd, adds tracing headers (traceparent, x-request-id), and routes to the right Service. kube-proxy or the service mesh sidecar picks a healthy pod IP via iptables/eBPF and forwards the connection.

9. The application layer

Finally the request reaches your ASP.NET Core / Spring Boot / Node process. The framework's routing table matches the method+path, runs middleware (auth, CORS, logging), invokes the controller, hits a database or cache, and returns a response. Same path — pod → ingress → ALB → Cloudflare → browser — for the reply.

Typical deployment. Register the domain with Route 53. Delegate the whole zone to Cloudflare (change NS records at the registrar). Set an A-proxied record on app.example.com pointing at your ALB's DNS name (Cloudflare will resolve and serve traffic behind its Anycast IP). Provision an ALB with two listeners — 80 → 443 redirect, 443 with an ACM certificate — and rules that split by host/path into target groups. Each target group points at an EKS node group or ECS service. Slap an AWS WAF WebACL on the ALB for defense-in-depth. Route 53 private hosted zones handle internal service discovery. Done.

💻 C# implementation — a minimal end-to-end pipeline

Below is a compact ASP.NET Core setup showing what the application-side of this flow actually looks like: forwarded-header handling (so HttpContext.Connection.RemoteIpAddress is the real client, not the ALB), CORS for the browser origin, health endpoints for ALB target-group probes, and a route that reads a header the WAF/Ingress added. This is what a Principal Engineer would show at a whiteboard.

// Program.cs — ASP.NET Core 8. Behind Cloudflare → AWS WAF → ALB → Ingress.
using Microsoft.AspNetCore.HttpOverrides;

var builder = WebApplication.CreateBuilder(args);

builder.Services.Configure<ForwardedHeadersOptions>(o =>
{
    // ALB/Ingress set X-Forwarded-For and X-Forwarded-Proto.
    // Cloudflare adds CF-Connecting-IP with the real client IP.
    o.ForwardedHeaders = ForwardedHeaders.XForwardedFor | ForwardedHeaders.XForwardedProto;
    o.KnownNetworks.Clear();  // trust the ingress/ALB CIDR only
    o.KnownProxies.Clear();
});

builder.Services.AddCors(p => p.AddDefaultPolicy(b =>
    b.WithOrigins("https://app.example.com").AllowAnyHeader().AllowAnyMethod()));

builder.Services.AddHealthChecks();       // /healthz for ALB target group
builder.Services.AddControllers();

var app = builder.Build();

app.UseForwardedHeaders();                // must run BEFORE anything reads RemoteIpAddress
app.UseHttpsRedirection();
app.UseCors();

// Cheap liveness probe — used by ALB target group + k8s liveness.
app.MapGet("/healthz", () => Results.Ok(new { status = "ok" }));

// Real endpoint — reads the client IP the reverse proxies preserved for us.
app.MapGet("/orders/{id:int}", (int id, HttpContext ctx) =>
{
    var clientIp = ctx.Request.Headers["CF-Connecting-IP"].FirstOrDefault()
                ?? ctx.Connection.RemoteIpAddress?.ToString();
    var traceId = ctx.Request.Headers["traceparent"].FirstOrDefault();

    // Auth, DB, business logic ...
    return Results.Ok(new { orderId = id, servedTo = clientIp, trace = traceId });
});

app.MapControllers();
app.Run();

Key things this snippet gets right that most demos get wrong: (1) UseForwardedHeaders runs before any middleware that inspects RemoteIpAddress; (2) it reads Cloudflare's CF-Connecting-IP header, which is the only reliable client IP when Cloudflare is in front; (3) a cheap /healthz endpoint lets the ALB target-group probe pass without touching a database.

⚖️ Trade-offs, bottlenecks & likely follow-ups

Where it actually breaks

DNS is the silent single point of failure. If your authoritative NS goes down (Dyn 2016, AWS us-east-1 Route 53 2019), no client can find your site — even if the origin is perfectly healthy. Mitigation: use two DNS providers in secondary mode. Long TTLs kill failover. A 3600 s TTL on an A record means it takes an hour for the internet to notice a new IP. Use 60 s for anything that can move. Cloudflare origin exposure — if your origin IP leaks (misconfigured DNS record, cert transparency logs, email headers), attackers bypass the CDN entirely. Fix: restrict the origin security group to accept traffic only from Cloudflare's published IP ranges.

Consistency & caching

CDN caching is eventually consistent — a purge doesn't hit all 330 PoPs at the exact same instant. If you need strict freshness, cache-bust via versioned URLs (app.abc123.js) instead of relying on purge. Personalised responses (auth'd APIs) usually mark themselves Cache-Control: private, no-store and never sit at the edge — which means the origin sees full traffic on those paths.

TLS & certs

Certs expire. Automate with ACM or Let's Encrypt, and monitor days-to-expiry. Watch out for cipher-suite mismatches between Cloudflare and your ALB — you need at least one overlap or the origin fetch fails at handshake. TLS 1.3 negotiates in 1 RTT (down from 2 in TLS 1.2), which is a measurable page-load win.

Interviewer follow-ups (with crisp answers)

Q. Why is example.com often an A record but www.example.com a CNAME?

A. A CNAME can't sit at the zone apex because DNS forbids a CNAME to coexist with the required SOA/NS records. So the apex needs a real A/AAAA — or a vendor-specific ALIAS/ANAME record that the DNS provider resolves internally.

Q. What's Anycast vs Unicast?

A. Unicast is one IP → one server. Anycast is one IP announced by many servers via BGP; internet routing picks the topologically nearest one. It's how Cloudflare has 388 Tbps of edge capacity behind a handful of IPs, and how the DNS root servers scale.

Q. Where does TLS actually terminate?

A. Twice, usually. Once at Cloudflare (client → edge) so the WAF and cache can read the request. A second, separate TLS connection from Cloudflare to the origin ALB, using the customer's ACM cert. That's why "end-to-end encryption" here doesn't mean "no one in the middle sees plaintext" — it means every hop is encrypted, but Cloudflare deliberately sees plaintext to do its job.

Q. How does the ALB pick a pod?

A. Listener rules match host/path/header conditions in priority order. The winning rule points at a target group. Within the target group, the algorithm is round-robin by default or "least outstanding requests"; unhealthy targets are removed via health checks. If sticky sessions are needed, an ALB-generated cookie pins subsequent requests to the same target.

Q. ALB or NLB — when do I pick which?

A. ALB for HTTP/HTTPS/gRPC — anything where you want to route on path/headers or terminate TLS. NLB for TCP/UDP, extreme RPS, or when you need a static IP the client can whitelist. Many real deployments put an NLB in front of the ALB just to get the static IP.

Q. What happens on a total Cloudflare outage?

A. Your users hit an error. Mitigation: multi-CDN setups (Cloudflare + Fastly or Akamai) with DNS-based failover, and — critically — you must be able to bypass the CDN and go direct to origin, which means the origin has to be able to survive the raw traffic and terminate its own certs. Most companies accept the risk and put a runbook in place.

🗣️ Interview cheat sheet

30-second pitch: "A request travels through five trust boundaries. First the browser + OS + ISP resolves the hostname via DNS — root, TLD, then the authoritative NS — and gets an Anycast IP owned by Cloudflare. Second, the packet hits the nearest Cloudflare PoP, where TLS 1.3 terminates and the WAF, DDoS shield, and CDN cache decide whether to answer or forward. Third, the surviving request crosses to the AWS ALB, which uses listener rules to pick a target group by host + path. Fourth, the ingress or service mesh handles internal routing and mTLS. Fifth, the request lands on the application pod. Each hop can short-circuit — the earlier it short-circuits, the more scale we get for free."

Numbers to quote

  • DNS cold-lookup: 40–200 ms. Warm: <5 ms.
  • Cloudflare edge: 330+ cities, 388 Tbps capacity.
  • TLS 1.3 handshake: 1 RTT (0 with PSK).
  • Edge cache hit ratio target: >80% static, >50% API.
  • ALB is Layer-7; NLB is Layer-4.
  • Route 53 SLA: 100%.

5 talking points that win the round

  • Draw the two flows separately — DNS control-plane (UDP/53) and data-plane (HTTPS). Interviewers love this.
  • Name the record types (A, AAAA, CNAME, ALIAS, NS, SOA, CAA) and explain the CNAME-at-apex rule.
  • Say "Anycast IP" — it's the single word that shows you understand how CDNs actually route.
  • Call out the two TLS terminations (edge and origin) and why they're not a security bug.
  • Discuss what doesn't hit origin — WAF blocks, cache hits, DDoS scrubbing — because that's where the cost story lives.
