🎯 Real-world scenario

What we're building & where it's used

You're building the guts of a ride-sharing platform: a rider opens the app, sees the nearest cars moving on the map in real time, taps Request, and within a second a driver is offered the trip. Behind that one tap is a continuous firehose of GPS pings from millions of moving vehicles, a geospatial index that answers "who is within 2 km of this lat/lng right now?" thousands of times per second, and a matching engine that reserves exactly one driver per rider even when three riders reach for the same car in the same 200 ms window.

This is the canonical real-time marketplace problem, solved in production by Uber, Lyft, Ola, Didi, Grab, Bolt, Careem, and — with variations — by DoorDash, Uber Eats, Instacart, Amazon Flex. It shows up in interviews because it forces you to reason about write-heavy geo-indexing, consistent hashing over stateful services, persistent bidirectional connections, event streaming (Kafka), and partial-failure recovery — all inside a single, tangible product everyone has used.

Real-world grounding: Uber's own dispatch stack introduced this pattern (Supply / Demand / DISCO), built on Ringpop (consistent hashing + SWIM gossip), Google S2 for the live dispatch grid, and H3 (Uber's own hexagonal grid) for supply-and-demand analytics that drive surge pricing.

🧑‍🎓 In laymen terms (for a beginner)

Explain it like I have never opened a code editor

You're standing on a corner, tired, and you want a car to come pick you up. You open an app, it shows you tiny cars moving around on a map, you tap a button, and a minute later a car pulls up. Simple to feel — enormously tricky to build. Let's walk through it like a story.

Every driver's phone is like a tiny lighthouse, blinking its exact position out to a big control room every four seconds. Multiply that by millions of drivers around the world and you get a river of blinks pouring in — millions per second, day and night.

Now the trick: when you tap Request, the app has to find the drivers near you, not sift through every driver on Earth. So the world is chopped up into a huge grid of small tiles — think of the city like a giant honeycomb where each hexagon is about half a kilometer wide. Every driver "lives" in one tile at any moment. When you ask for a ride, the system just looks in your tile and a few neighboring tiles. That turns "search five million drivers" into "look inside a dozen tiles" — the difference between checking every house in a city and just checking your street.

Once a few candidates are found, another service asks: Which one gets to me fastest by road? (Not "shortest as the crow flies" — real streets, one-ways, traffic.) It picks the winner and offers the ride. The tricky part here is that only one driver can get your ride — even if two riders tap at the same instant for the same car, the system has to say "yes" to exactly one and "sorry, try again" to the other. Think of a single bakery ticket: whoever grabs it first, gets the loaf.

And the last bit — surge pricing. At 8pm on New Year's Eve in a downtown block, riders vastly outnumber drivers. The system watches, per hexagon, how many people are asking versus how many cars are free, and if the ratio is bad it nudges the price up. Higher price pulls more drivers into the area, and prices settle back down. It's not greed; it's a thermostat for supply and demand, tuned every few seconds per tile.

Products that work this way: Uber, Lyft, Ola, DoorDash, Instacart. Same shape, different cargo — sometimes it's you, sometimes it's a burrito.

📋 Requirements & scale

Requirements and back-of-the-envelope math

Functional

  • Drivers broadcast their location continuously; the system tracks who is available, on-trip, or offline.
  • Riders can request a ride from a lat/lng; the system returns the best-matched nearby driver within ~1 second.
  • A driver can be matched to at most one active rider — no double-booking.
  • Both apps maintain a live picture: rider sees the ETA and car moving; driver sees turn-by-turn.
  • Surge pricing is computed per geographic cell in near real time.
  • Payments, ratings, notifications, and trip history are stored durably.

Non-functional

  • Latency: match < 1 s p99; location update ingest < 100 ms p99.
  • Availability: five 9s on the match path — a downed matching engine strands entire cities.
  • Consistency: per-driver assignment must be strongly consistent (linearizable CAS). The geo index itself can be eventually consistent — a driver's location being 4 s stale is fine.
  • Scale: tens of millions of DAUs; millions of active drivers globally.

🏗️ High-level architecture

How the pieces fit together

Think of the platform as three planes riding on top of a Kafka firehose. The ingest plane (green) takes the driver-location tsunami and routes each ping to the shard that owns that geographic cell. The match plane (blue) takes a rider request, queries the live geo index for nearby drivers, ranks them by road-network ETA, and atomically reserves the winner. The marketplace plane (amber) consumes the same location stream to run surge pricing and demand prediction, feeding the price back to the rider at request time.

Two protocol choices matter. Drivers push locations over a persistent WebSocket / bidirectional gRPC channel — much cheaper than an HTTPS handshake per ping, and it lets the server push back a match offer instantly. Riders open the same kind of channel while watching cars. Everything else (payments, history) is boring stateless HTTP.

🔍 Component deep-dive

1. Ingesting a million pings per second

Every driver's phone opens a persistent WebSocket (or bidirectional gRPC stream) to the API gateway when it goes online. Roughly every four seconds the app pushes a compact protobuf frame containing driver_id, lat, lng, bearing, speed, and a monotonic seq. Using a persistent channel rather than fresh HTTPS per ping is a huge win: no TLS handshake per update, half-duplex is free, and the same socket carries offer pushes back to the driver.

The gateway does not write to a database. It publishes to Kafka on a topic like location.driver.updates, partitioned by driver_id — that keeps a single driver's updates ordered on one partition. From here two consumers fan out: the Location Service updates the live geo index, and downstream analytics consumers feed surge, ETA training, fraud detection, and long-term storage.

2. The live geospatial index (Location Service)

The Location Service is a stateful, sharded, in-memory fleet. Each node owns a set of geographic cells; the world is chopped by Google S2 (Uber's dispatch grid at level 12, ~3–6 km²) or H3 hexagons at a comparable resolution. The cell_id is the sharding key, and shards are placed on a consistent hash ring managed by Ringpop with the SWIM gossip protocol for membership.

Within a shard, two co-located maps live in memory: cell → List<Driver> and driver_id → Location. Updates within the same cell are O(1) — just mutate the driver's lat/lng. A cell crossing costs three coordinated writes: remove from the old cell's list, add to the new cell's list, update the driver → cell pointer. If the destination cell lives on a different shard, the update is rerouted via the ring.

Nothing is written to disk on the hot path. The index is a throwaway: if a shard dies, the next four seconds of driver pings will fully repopulate it. Location durability lives in Kafka's retained log; the shard is just a fast projection.

3. Finding nearby drivers (the query side)

To find drivers near a rider, the Matching service converts the rider's lat/lng to a cell ID and asks: give me all drivers in this cell and its k-ring neighbors. In H3 that's kRing(cell, 1) — the center hex plus the six surrounding hexes. Why the ring? Because a driver 20 m from the rider could easily be sitting on the other side of a cell boundary; a single-cell lookup would miss them. Cell size is chosen so that k=1 covers the intended match radius (typically 1–2 km).

The candidate set (dozens, not millions) is then filtered — must be available, must have the right vehicle class, must not be on-trip — and passed to the ETA/routing service to compute a road-network arrival time using OSRM (Dijkstra with contraction hierarchies precomputed nightly). The winner is the driver with the smallest ETA.

4. The atomic reservation (the interesting part)

Now the subtle bit: at any instant, three riders may all be reaching for the same driver. The system must reserve the driver for exactly one rider — this is the one place we need linearizability. Uber's DISCO does a compare-and-swap on the driver's status field:

UPDATE drivers
SET status='reserved', reserved_by='rider-42', reserved_at=now()
WHERE driver_id='D7' AND status='available';

Whichever transaction executes first sees 1 row affected and wins. The others see zero and iterate to the next candidate. This is coordinated per driver_id, so total contention is tiny — a hot key would need multiple riders reaching for one specific car. The rest of the system remains eventually consistent.

5. Surge pricing (the marketplace loop)

A stream job (Flink or Spark Streaming) consumes the location firehose plus a parallel ride.requests topic, bucketing both by H3 cell at ~1 km resolution over a rolling 1–5 minute window. For each cell it computes a simple ratio: open requests ÷ available drivers. When that ratio crosses thresholds, a surge_multiplier for that cell is written to a low-latency store (Redis) keyed by H3 cell.

When a rider requests a ride, the matching service reads the surge multiplier for the rider's cell and applies it to the fare estimate. The higher price signals both directions: riders may defer, and drivers in nearby cells are notified — many will migrate toward the surge tile, closing the gap in minutes. The whole loop is fully event-driven: no user asked for a price update; the stream job is always running.

6. Hot cells & the boundary problem

Downtown at 5pm can hold thousands of drivers in one cell — an obvious hotspot. Two mitigations: (a) adaptive resolution — use finer cells in dense zones so no cell is overloaded; and (b) sub-shard splitting — split a hot cell into C#0..C#3 keyed by hash(driver_id), so writes distribute and reads fan out four ways in parallel. Uber's engineering blog notes that hard-coded cell size (level 12) is a known limitation of their earlier S2-based design.

7. Failure & datacenter failover

Because the live index is throwaway, a shard crash is nearly free — the ring rebalances, in-flight requests retry, drivers repopulate the new owner in a single ping cycle. For full datacenter failover, Uber uses a clever trick: DISCO periodically sends the driver's phone an encrypted state digest of any in-progress trip. If the primary DC dies and the secondary DC has no trip state, the driver's next ping uploads the digest back, reconstructing state without cross-DC replication of hot trip data.

Why H3 (hexagons) and not squares?

Hexagons have a single neighbor distance: every one of a hex's six neighbors sits the same distance from its center. Squares have two neighbor distances (edge-shared vs. diagonally-shared), and triangles have three. This matters when you're computing gradients — like "how does surge fall off from the hot cell to its neighbors?" — because a uniform metric means fewer coefficients and much simpler math. H3 also uses 16 resolutions where each level's cells are ~1/7 the area of their parent, and cell IDs pack into a 64-bit int for cheap hashing and set membership.

Why not just a B-tree on (lat, lng)?

Because a 2-D range query lat BETWEEN a AND b AND lng BETWEEN c AND d on a B-tree is a bad fit — the B-tree is 1-D. You either scan a wide range or maintain two indexes and intersect. Grid cells collapse 2-D locality into a 1-D key, and that key doubles as a shard partition.

💻 C# implementation

The core of the system is the geospatial shard plus the atomic reservation. Below is an interview-ready reference: an in-memory geo index keyed by cell, a k-ring nearby-driver query, and a CAS-style reservation. In production you'd back the geo maps with ConcurrentDictionary and per-cell locks; the reservation would be a single-partition Cassandra or DynamoDB conditional update.

// GeoIndexShard.cs — one shard owns a set of H3 cells and keeps drivers in memory.
// The real system uses the H3 library; here we abstract cell resolution behind ICellIndex.

using System.Collections.Concurrent;

public record DriverLocation(
    string DriverId,
    double Lat,
    double Lng,
    ulong CellId,
    DriverStatus Status,
    long Ts);

public enum DriverStatus { Available, Reserved, OnTrip, Offline }

public interface ICellIndex {
    ulong LatLngToCell(double lat, double lng);
    ulong[] KRing(ulong cell, int k);
    double Haversine(double lat1, double lng1, double lat2, double lng2);
}

public class GeoIndexShard {
    // cell → set of drivers currently in that cell
    private readonly ConcurrentDictionary<ulong, ConcurrentDictionary<string, byte>> _byCell = new();
    // driver_id → latest location (for O(1) updates + CAS on status)
    private readonly ConcurrentDictionary<string, DriverLocation> _byDriver = new();
    private readonly ICellIndex _idx;

    public GeoIndexShard(ICellIndex idx) => _idx = idx;

    // Called for every incoming GPS ping (Kafka consumer).
    public void UpsertLocation(string driverId, double lat, double lng, long ts) {
        var newCell = _idx.LatLngToCell(lat, lng);
        _byDriver.AddOrUpdate(driverId,
            _ => { var loc = new DriverLocation(driverId, lat, lng, newCell, DriverStatus.Available, ts);
                    _byCell.GetOrAdd(newCell, _ => new()).TryAdd(driverId, 0);
                    return loc; },
            (_, prev) => {
                if (ts < prev.Ts) return prev;               // out-of-order ping, drop
                if (prev.CellId != newCell) {
                    // cell crossing: remove from old, add to new
                    if (_byCell.TryGetValue(prev.CellId, out var oldSet))
                        oldSet.TryRemove(driverId, out _);
                    _byCell.GetOrAdd(newCell, _ => new()).TryAdd(driverId, 0);
                }
                return prev with { Lat = lat, Lng = lng, CellId = newCell, Ts = ts };
            });
    }

    // Rider request → nearby available drivers ranked by rough distance.
    // The ETA service then re-ranks these with road-network routing.
    public IEnumerable<DriverLocation> Nearby(double lat, double lng, int topN = 10) {
        var center = _idx.LatLngToCell(lat, lng);
        var cells  = _idx.KRing(center, 1);           // center + 6 neighbors

        var candidates = new List<DriverLocation>();
        foreach (var c in cells) {
            if (!_byCell.TryGetValue(c, out var set)) continue;
            foreach (var did in set.Keys) {
                if (_byDriver.TryGetValue(did, out var d) && d.Status == DriverStatus.Available)
                    candidates.Add(d);
            }
        }
        return candidates
            .OrderBy(d => _idx.Haversine(lat, lng, d.Lat, d.Lng))
            .Take(topN);
    }

    // Atomic reservation: whichever thread flips Available → Reserved wins.
    // Losers see false and try the next candidate.
    public bool TryReserve(string driverId, string tripId) {
        while (_byDriver.TryGetValue(driverId, out var cur)) {
            if (cur.Status != DriverStatus.Available) return false;
            var next = cur with { Status = DriverStatus.Reserved };
            if (_byDriver.TryUpdate(driverId, next, cur)) return true;
            // lost the race — reload and retry, unless someone else already took it
        }
        return false;
    }
}

A few things to call out in an interview about this code:

  • Out-of-order pings are dropped via ts comparison. Kafka partitions by driver_id already give per-driver ordering, but retries and DC failover can rewind — the timestamp check protects us.
  • Cell crossings are the only moderately expensive path — same-cell updates are just a field mutation.
  • TryReserve is CAS in a loop, mirroring what Cassandra's LWT or DynamoDB's ConditionExpression would give you across nodes. In production this call goes to the durable store, not the in-memory shard.
  • ICellIndex is abstracted so you can plug in H3, S2, or geohash. Uber's own OSS H3 has a .NET binding; S2 does too.

Alternative languages/tools: Uber's own dispatch was Node.js (fits async I/O well); many peers now use Go for the geo shards (better GC for sustained high-throughput). C# / .NET is a perfectly reasonable choice on modern kestrel + ASP.NET Core.

⚖️ Trade-offs, bottlenecks & likely follow-ups

Bottlenecks and mitigations

Hot cells. Downtown is a spatial hot key: one cell with 20K drivers dominates a shard. Mitigate with (a) adaptive resolution — finer cells in dense zones; (b) sub-shard splitting — hash driver_id into cell#0..#3 and scatter-gather queries; (c) shed the oldest queued pings first when a shard's inbox backs up.

Ingest write amplification. Every driver writes every 4 s regardless of whether they moved. Compress: skip pings if displacement < ε and heading unchanged. Batch pings client-side into a single 4-second frame with sub-second sub-samples. Some fleets drop from 4 s → 2 s only when a trip is active.

Cross-cell coordination. A driver near the boundary flips cells often. Add hysteresis: don't reassign until the driver is > ε deep into the new cell.

ETA is the slowest step. Road-network Dijkstra on a big graph is the highest-latency piece per match. Contraction hierarchies + memoized ETAs per (origin cell, destination cell) at coarse resolution take it from 100 ms to a few ms.

Consistency choices

Geo index: eventual. A stale location by 4 seconds means we might offer a driver who is 30 m off — the driver either accepts or the system re-matches. Cheap error.

Driver status: linearizable per key. Double-booking is a product bug we cannot ship. CAS on a single key gives strong consistency where we need it without a global transaction.

Trip state: sequential per trip. Trip transitions (requested → matched → started → ended) are single-key state machines, easy to keep consistent.

Failure modes

Shard death: Ringpop rebalances; the fresh owner rebuilds its slice of the index from the next ping cycle. Match requests in flight retry idempotently.

Kafka outage: The gateway falls back to a smaller regional store or short-lived in-memory buffer; on recovery, the backlog drains. Surge freezes at its last value.

Network partition (rider ↔ driver): WebSocket reconnects with an exponential backoff carrying the trip ID; the server treats the trip as authoritative until a timeout.

DC failover: The encrypted state-digest trick puts a lightweight backup of trip state on the driver's phone — the ultimate distributed backup medium.

Likely interviewer follow-ups

How would you handle a stalled/crashed driver phone?

Timestamp each ping. If a driver's last ping is older than N intervals (say 20 s), mark Offline, remove from the cell's candidate set, and stop offering. When a fresh ping arrives, re-add.

Why not just put every driver in Redis with GEOADD?

Redis geo commands work for small fleets but are single-node bottlenecks at Uber scale — GEOSEARCH on millions of points is slow and Redis doesn't shard by geo naturally. In-memory sharded services with a consistent hash ring scale linearly; Redis geo is fine for a startup, not for a global fleet.

Quadtree vs. geohash vs. H3 vs. S2 — how do you pick?

Fixed grids (H3, S2, geohash) shard trivially by cell ID and give O(1) lookups; use them when driver density is roughly uniform or when you can adapt resolution. Quadtrees adapt to skew automatically — great for extreme non-uniformity — but are harder to shard and rebalance. Uber picked S2 for dispatch (fixed level 12 cells) and H3 for analytics (hexagons for uniform gradients / surge).

How does surge feed back into behavior?

The multiplier is displayed to the rider (deterrent) and pushed to nearby drivers via notification ("busy area — 1.8×"). Over ~2–5 minutes drivers migrate in, the ratio normalizes, and surge decays. It's a slow control loop over Kafka events, not a synchronous negotiation.

Why not a single global geo database?

Because at 1–2 M writes/sec no single-master database keeps up cheaply, and geo locality lets us shard perfectly by cell — same-city queries hit the same shard, cross-region traffic is naturally zero. Splitting is free.

How do you keep matches fair across drivers?

DISCO can factor idle time and recent trip volume when ranking within a candidate set — not purely nearest-ETA. There's also a per-driver rate limiter on how many offers they can decline before de-prioritization.

🗣️ Interview cheat sheet

30-second pitch: "Uber is a real-time marketplace. Drivers stream GPS pings every 4 s over persistent WebSockets into a Kafka firehose — about 1–2 million writes per second at scale. The pings feed an in-memory geospatial index sharded by H3 or S2 cell IDs on a consistent hash ring. When a rider requests a ride, the matching service does a k-ring lookup in the rider's cell, ranks candidates by road-network ETA via OSRM, and reserves the winner with a linearizable CAS on driver status. A parallel stream job aggregates supply and demand per H3 cell every minute for surge pricing. The whole hot path is throwaway state — nothing is persisted on the location-update path — so shards die cheaply and drivers repopulate them in one ping cycle."

  • The five numbers to volunteer: 5M active drivers · GPS every 4 s · ~1.25 M writes/s · ~500 MB in-memory index · sub-second match latency.
  • The five talking points that win: (1) H3 hexagons over squares for uniform neighbor distance; (2) k-ring to handle boundary drivers; (3) OSRM/CH for road-network ETA (not haversine); (4) atomic CAS on driver_id for reservation; (5) throwaway in-memory shards backed by Kafka.
  • Where consistency lives: eventual for locations, linearizable per-key for status — say this out loud.
  • Surge is a control loop: supply/demand ratio per cell → multiplier → decays as drivers migrate. Not a bidding auction.
  • Datacenter failover trick: encrypted state digest on the driver's phone reconstructs trip state — impress the interviewer with this one.
