🎯 Real-world scenario

What we're building & where it's used

Live-video-at-scale is the hardest streaming problem in the industry. Unlike Netflix or YouTube — where each viewer watches a different chunk of a different file — a live sporting event forces every viewer to be on almost the same 2-to-6-second window of the timeline at once. When 20 million phones simultaneously request the same 4-second HLS segment, you have one of two outcomes: your CDN mesh absorbs it, or your origin catches fire. There is no third option.

JioCinema (now merged into JioHotstar) is the platform behind the Indian Premier League (IPL) digital broadcast. It has repeatedly set world records for concurrent live-stream viewership: 20M concurrent during IPL 2023 marquee matches, ramping to 32M during the IPL 2024 final and up to 55M during IPL 2025. The traffic isn't linear either — the arrival rate spikes from 5M to 30M+ inside a few minutes when a star batter walks out or the final over begins.

The same architecture pattern shows up wherever the whole world watches the same thing at the same moment: Disney+ Hotstar for earlier IPL seasons, YouTube Live for the FIFA World Cup, Twitch for esports finals, Amazon Prime Video for Thursday Night Football, Peacock for the Olympics, Twitter/X Spaces for viral live conversations, and Zoom for the largest webinars. In an interview, this problem is the canonical way to demonstrate you understand CDN economics, live packaging, DRM, hot-key handling, elastic autoscaling, and graceful degradation — all at once.

🧑‍🎓 In laymen terms — for a beginner

Imagine a stadium the size of a country

Picture an actual cricket stadium — 50,000 seats, a giant screen, cheerleaders. Now imagine you had to squeeze 400 stadiums' worth of people into one room, each with their own tiny TV, and every single tiny TV has to show the ball being hit at the exact same moment. That is what "20 million concurrent viewers" means on your phone.

Here is what actually happens when you tap Watch Live. A camera at the stadium sends the video over a fibre cable to a big computer (a server). The server chops the match into short 4-second clips — like tearing a movie roll into little strips — and hands each strip to many, many "delivery vans" (called CDN edge servers) parked in cities near you. Your phone doesn't call the stadium; it calls the delivery van in your city and says "give me the next 4-second strip". The van already has it in its glove-box, so it hands it over in a blink. Your phone stitches strip after strip together and you feel like you're watching one smooth video.

The scary bit is what happens when Dhoni comes out to bat. Millions of people who were making tea suddenly rush back to their phones in the same 30 seconds. The system was ready for 5 vans, and now needs 30. If it can't add vans fast enough, the vans get mobbed, delivery slows, and everybody sees a spinning circle. So the engineers do two clever things: (1) they guess ahead of time — using patterns from last year — that Dhoni is likely to walk out around over 15, and pre-park the extra vans hours before the match, and (2) if the surprise is truly a surprise, they switch to "panic mode" — every phone gets the same pre-recorded generic screen from a cheap cardboard box until the vans are ready.

The hard part isn't showing video — YouTube already does that. The hard part is that everybody wants the exact same 4 seconds at the exact same time. This is unlike Netflix (where I'm on episode 3 and you're on episode 7) or Instagram (where I'm scrolling different reels). Live sport is one giant crowd all reaching for the same doorknob together. Solving that is why this system is a marvel — the same challenge faced by Hotstar, YouTube Live, Twitch, and Amazon Prime when you watch a big match or a launch event.

Tiny example: You and 19,999,999 other Indians are watching the IPL final on your phones. It is over 20, ball 5 — the Chennai Super Kings need 6 runs. The camera captures Dhoni's bat swing. That video clip is chopped into a 4-second strip and copied to ~50 delivery vans across India within 300 milliseconds. Your phone in Bengaluru asks the Bengaluru van, my phone in Mumbai asks the Mumbai van, someone else's phone in Guwahati asks the Guwahati van — all in the same second — and each of us gets the same "SIX!" moment without ever bothering the stadium's original computer. That is the magic.

📋 Requirements & scale

Requirements and back-of-the-envelope math

Functional requirements

  • Ingest a live TV feed from the stadium production truck.
  • Package it into adaptive-bitrate segments (multiple resolutions/bitrates).
  • Deliver segments to phones, TVs, and web players with < 1% buffering.
  • Support pause / rewind / seek within a rolling DVR window (last ~1 hour).
  • Insert ads dynamically per user cohort and language feed (SCTE-35 signalled).
  • Serve 12+ language commentary feeds and multi-angle camera views.
  • Show a live concurrent-viewer counter, chat, and interactive polls.
  • Protect the stream with DRM (Widevine, PlayReady, FairPlay).

Non-functional requirements

  • Concurrency: 20M–55M simultaneous playback sessions.
  • Glass-to-glass latency: 3–15 s (LL-HLS target 3–6 s, standard HLS ~10 s).
  • Availability: 99.99% during a match window.
  • Cost: egress bandwidth is the single biggest bill — cache hit ratio must exceed 90%.
  • Elasticity: scale from 1M idle to 30M concurrent in < 5 minutes when a marquee moment lands.

🏗️ High-level architecture

How the pieces fit together

The system splits into three planes: a video plane (contribution feed → transcoder → packager → CDN → player), a control plane (login, entitlements, playback URL signing, DRM licence, ad decisioning), and an observability + failsafe plane (real-time metrics, feature flags, panic-mode toggle). Under load, the video plane is served almost entirely by the CDN mesh, freeing the origin to run cool. The control plane is a set of Kubernetes-hosted microservices behind an API CDN. The failsafe plane exists precisely because the first two will occasionally lie about being fine.

Origin / Encoder CDN Mesh (video) Control plane Video request path Panic-mode fallback

Verbal walkthrough for the whiteboard: The stadium's live SDI feed is contributed over SRT to a live transcoder, which produces an ABR ladder (5–8 rungs from 240p up to 4K HDR). The packager wraps segments into HLS, MPEG-DASH, and CMAF; the DRM system encrypts them and hands keys to the licence server. Segments land on the origin (with an origin shield in front) and fan out to a mesh of external CDNs — Akamai, CloudFront, Cloudflare, plus Jio's own edge — plus embedded caches inside telco ISPs. The player boots by hitting the API CDN for config and playback token, calls the DRM licence server, then asks the multi-CDN optimizer which CDN to pull from. From that moment on, every 4-second segment request goes edge → edge, never touching the origin. Heartbeats flow back through Kafka to compute the real-time concurrency counter, and if crash rate or QoE dips, feature flags flip the client into a degraded mode and the CDN swaps to serving pre-baked "panic-mode" static blobs.

🔍 Component deep-dive

1. Contribution & ingest

The stadium's broadcast truck outputs an SDI signal — think of it as the "raw camera feed after the director cuts". It is contributed to JioCinema's cloud over SRT or Zixi (secure, reliable transport built for lossy internet), often via redundant paths from two ISPs so that a single fibre cut in Mumbai doesn't kill the match. This is a single input flow — ingest is not the scaling problem. Everything hard begins after it.

2. Live transcoder & ABR ladder

A distributed encoding cluster (bare-metal x86 servers with hardware encoders, orchestrated by K8s) transcodes the master feed into an adaptive-bitrate ladder: typically 240p @ 400 kbps, 480p @ 800 kbps, 720p @ 2 Mbps, 1080p @ 4 Mbps, and 4K HDR @ 15 Mbps. Codec is H.264 by default (broadest device compatibility) with HEVC for 4K. The output is chopped into 2–4-second GOP-aligned segments so any player can start on any keyframe.

3. Packager & DRM

The packager (e.g., AWS Elemental MediaPackage, or an in-house Shaka-Packager fleet) wraps segments into three protocol variants — HLS (Apple/iOS), MPEG-DASH (Android/web/smart TV), and CMAF low-latency chunks — so that each device gets its native format from the same source segments (fMP4 unifies the underlying container). It also encrypts segments and generates DRM key IDs for Widevine (Android/Chrome), PlayReady (Windows/Xbox), and FairPlay (iOS/Safari). Keys never leave the licence server; the client fetches a licence per session.

4. Origin, origin shield & the CDN mesh

Segments land on a small origin fleet, which is deliberately kept small — a single-digit number of instances is enough. Directly in front sits an origin shield: a single logical cache that de-duplicates all upstream CDN fetches. Without it, 5 CDNs times 20 regions each requesting the same segment would hammer the origin. With it, each new segment is fetched once, then fanned out. The multi-CDN mesh spans Akamai, Amazon CloudFront, Cloudflare, Jio's in-house CDN, and telco-embedded caches inside Airtel, ACT, and BSNL networks. A custom multi-CDN optimizer service watches every CDN's real-time metrics (error rate, latency, throughput) and hands each player a signed playback URL pointing at the current best CDN. If a CDN degrades, the next segment request goes to a different one — the player never notices.

5. The player

ExoPlayer (Android), AVPlayer (iOS), Shaka Player (web), and Smart TV players all implement ABR selection: measure recent bandwidth (moving average of the last N segment fetches), compare to buffer occupancy, and pick the highest bitrate rung the pipe supports without draining the buffer. Segment fetches are streamed via HTTP/2 or HTTP/3 (QUIC) — QUIC in particular hides packet loss well on mobile radios.

6. Panic mode & graceful degradation

The system's most talked-about trick. Before the match, engineers pre-render static JSON snapshots of the home screen, EPG, and other config endpoints — the "safe defaults". These are pushed to S3 and warmed on the CDN. Under normal load, the CDN routes API requests to the live microservices. If crash rates or latency cross a threshold, an operator (or an automated rule) flips a feature flag; the CDN configuration re-points API paths to the static blob instead. The video plane keeps working because it never depended on the origin, and the non-video experience degrades gracefully — no personalization, no recommendations, but no crashes either.

7. Predictive prescaling & ladder scale-down

Auto-scaling that reacts in 30–60 seconds is far too slow — 20M viewers can arrive in that window. Instead, historical ML models predict peak concurrency by minute across the match window and pre-provision K8s pods hours before the ball is bowled, targeting ~60% CPU headroom. After the match, capacity is stepped down in a "ladder" — e.g., 32M → 25M → 20M — with liveness/readiness gates at each step so the system doesn't collapse under stragglers still watching the post-match presentation.

8. Async pipeline (Kafka)

Anything non-real-time — heartbeats for the live viewer counter, ad-view logs, QoE telemetry — is fired at Kafka. Consumers aggregate concurrency into a per-second global counter, feed real-time dashboards, and materialize views for post-match analytics. If a Kafka broker dies, producers buffer locally and drain later — data loss is bounded by disk on the client, not by uptime of the pipe.

9. Ads (SCTE-35 + SSAI)

The stadium director cues an ad break; a marker is embedded in the broadcast stream using SCTE-35. Segments arriving at the packager carry these markers into the manifest as EMSG events. Server-side ad insertion (SSAI) then rewrites the manifest per user cohort — different phones get different ads, but from a viewer's perspective it looks like one continuous stream with no client-side ad-blocker escape hatch.

💻 C# implementation

Two of the algorithmically interesting pieces are the multi-CDN optimizer (which CDN should this player pull from right now?) and a token-bucket rate limiter guarding the DRM licence service (which must survive a retry storm when a CDN blip flips millions of players into "get me a fresh licence" mode).

// ---- Multi-CDN optimizer ---------------------------------------------
// EWMA-scored health, Power-of-Two-Choices routing.
// Each CDN's rolling score in [0,1]: 1 = perfect, 0 = failing.
// Called on every playback-URL sign; must be O(1) and lock-free.
public sealed class Cdn
{
    public string Name { get; init; } = "";
    private double _score = 1.0;
    private readonly object _lock = new();

    public double Score() { lock (_lock) return _score; }

    // α controls responsiveness: 0.3 reacts in ~10 samples.
    public void Record(bool ok, double latencyMs)
    {
        double sample = ok && latencyMs < 150 ? 1.0
                     : ok ? Math.Max(0, 1 - (latencyMs - 150) / 500)
                     : 0;
        lock (_lock) _score = 0.7 * _score + 0.3 * sample;
    }
}

public sealed class MultiCdnOptimizer
{
    private readonly Cdn[] _cdns;
    private readonly Random _rng = Random.Shared;

    public MultiCdnOptimizer(Cdn[] cdns) => _cdns = cdns;

    // Power-of-two-choices: pick 2 at random, keep the healthier.
    // Avoids a single "current best" from being stampeded.
    public Cdn Pick()
    {
        int i = _rng.Next(_cdns.Length);
        int j = _rng.Next(_cdns.Length);
        return _cdns[i].Score() >= _cdns[j].Score() ? _cdns[i] : _cdns[j];
    }
}

// ---- Token-bucket rate limiter for the DRM licence endpoint ----------
// Wraps the licence-issue path; every user + client-app pair gets a bucket.
// Refill rate = capacity/refillWindow, evaluated lazily to keep O(1).
public sealed class TokenBucket
{
    private readonly double _capacity;
    private readonly double _tokensPerMs;
    private double _tokens;
    private long _lastMs;
    private readonly object _lock = new();

    public TokenBucket(int capacity, TimeSpan refillWindow)
    {
        _capacity = capacity;
        _tokensPerMs = capacity / refillWindow.TotalMilliseconds;
        _tokens = capacity;
        _lastMs = Environment.TickCount64;
    }

    public bool TryConsume(int cost = 1)
    {
        lock (_lock)
        {
            long now = Environment.TickCount64;
            _tokens = Math.Min(_capacity, _tokens + (now - _lastMs) * _tokensPerMs);
            _lastMs = now;
            if (_tokens >= cost) { _tokens -= cost; return true; }
            return false;
        }
    }
}

// ---- Panic-mode gate for the API tier --------------------------------
// A single feature flag decides whether requests go to live services
// or are redirected to the static pre-baked blob in S3/CloudFront.
public interface IFeatureFlags { bool IsOn(string name); }

public sealed class HomeApi
{
    private readonly IFeatureFlags _flags;
    private readonly IHomeService _live;
    private readonly IPanicStore _panic;

    public HomeApi(IFeatureFlags flags, IHomeService live, IPanicStore panic)
    { _flags = flags; _live = live; _panic = panic; }

    public async Task<HomeResponse> GetHomeAsync(string userId, CancellationToken ct)
    {
        if (_flags.IsOn("panic-mode.home"))
            return await _panic.GetStaticHomeAsync(ct);   // served from CDN

        try          { return await _live.GetPersonalizedAsync(userId, ct); }
        catch        { return await _panic.GetStaticHomeAsync(ct); } // belt + braces
    }
}

In real production these components run in ASP.NET Core minimal APIs behind an Envoy sidecar, with the token bucket sharded per-user across a Redis cluster (so a load-balanced fleet enforces a single global limit). The multi-CDN scores are broadcast to all playback-URL signers over Redis Pub/Sub every second so every pod sees the same view.

⚖️ Trade-offs, bottlenecks & likely follow-ups

Where the design will break first

The single most-fragile piece is the origin during a cold cache. If a brand-new segment misses on the shield and 5 CDNs simultaneously try to fetch it, that's a thundering herd. The mitigation is the shield's request-coalescing (only one upstream at a time per key). The second-most fragile is the DRM licence service at match start — every phone in the country wants a licence in a 60-second window. This is why licence issue is rate-limited (token bucket) and licences are cached client-side across the match's DVR window.

Consistency vs availability

Live video is proudly AP: we tolerate a few seconds of drift and even a few seconds of divergence between viewers to keep the stream up. The concurrent-viewer count is eventually consistent (HLL, updated per second). The one place we demand consistency is entitlement — you don't get to keep watching a subscription-only match after you cancel — so entitlement checks hit a strongly-consistent DB and the answer is cached in a short-lived signed JWT.

Latency vs cost

Standard HLS delivers ~10-second glass-to-glass latency; LL-HLS/LL-DASH with 1-second CMAF chunks pushes it to 3–5 seconds but roughly doubles the CDN request rate (chunks are smaller and more frequent). A commercial live-sport operator typically picks 6–10 seconds as the sweet spot: viewers on Twitter learn a wicket fell a few seconds before their neighbour's TV, but bandwidth costs stay sane.

Common interviewer follow-ups

"How would you handle a single-CDN outage during a match?"
The multi-CDN optimizer notices the CDN's EWMA health score dropping (raised error rate, latency spike). Within a few seconds it stops handing out playback URLs for that CDN, and Route 53 latency-based DNS routes to healthy siblings. Players re-fetch the manifest with the new signed URL on the next segment boundary — user impact is a single stall of 1 segment.
"You have 30 seconds before Dhoni comes to bat. What are you doing?"
Nothing — because prescaling based on the match forecast already happened. If the forecast was wrong, we (a) flip on panic mode for non-video APIs, (b) tighten the ABR floor so we serve lower bitrates to marginal networks, (c) lean on ladder scale-up that's been pre-warmed at 20% headroom.
"Why 4-second segments instead of 1-second?"
Latency vs efficiency. 4-second segments mean 4× fewer HTTP requests, higher cache hit rates, and lower CDN cost — at the price of ~10s glass-to-glass. If sub-3s latency mattered (e.g., a live auction), we'd move to CMAF LL-HLS with 200ms chunked-transfer chunks; for IPL it doesn't move the business needle.
"How do you protect the DRM licence server from a retry storm?"
(1) Rate-limit per user via token bucket in Redis; (2) exponential backoff with jitter on the client SDK; (3) long-lived licence with periodic key rotation via key-rotation-in-manifest so most retries are cache hits; (4) a circuit breaker in front of the KMS-backed key vault so a slow KMS response doesn't take the licence server down.
"Why not just autoscale — why prescale?"
Kubernetes cluster autoscale (adding nodes, warming pods, passing readiness probes) takes 30–90 seconds. Concurrency can double in that window. The economics favour paying for spare capacity for a 3-hour match window over risking a 30-second outage that lands on Twitter — Prachi's team explicitly names this trade-off.
"How do you count 20M concurrent viewers accurately?"
Approximately, not exactly. Players heartbeat every ~10 s into Kafka; a consumer bucket them by a 30-second sliding window and merges HyperLogLog sketches per shard, then combines shards. Error is ~1% at 20M cardinality using ~12KB of memory per sketch. The trade-off is you can't answer "which 20M users?", only "how many". The exact set is available in the analytics warehouse post-match.

🗣️ Interview cheat sheet

"Live IPL streaming is a thundering-herd problem: 20 million phones want the same 4-second segment in the same 4 seconds. The trick is a CDN mesh in front of an origin shield that sees ~1 request per new segment, an ABR ladder so every device gets the right bitrate, prescaled Kubernetes capacity because 30-second autoscale is too slow, and a panic-mode static blob so the video plane keeps playing even when the API plane misbehaves."

  • Numbers to volunteer: 20M concurrent × 4 Mbps = 80 Tbps egress; 5M segment req/s; >90% CDN cache hit; ~1000 req/s hit the origin.
  • Name the three planes: video (encoder → packager → CDN), control (auth, DRM, ad, playback URL), failsafe (feature flags, panic-mode blob).
  • Name the CDN mesh: Akamai + CloudFront + Cloudflare + Jio's own edge + telco-embedded caches; picked live by a multi-CDN optimizer.
  • Say "prescale, don't autoscale": ML forecasts per-minute concurrency, pods provisioned hours ahead; ladder scale-down after the match, gated by readiness probes.
  • Explain panic mode: feature-flag flips the CDN to serve pre-baked static snapshots for non-video APIs; video keeps flowing from cache.
  • DSA callouts: HyperLogLog for the viewer counter, token bucket for DRM, consistent hashing for the origin shield, exponential backoff with jitter to prevent retry storms.
