🎯 Real-world scenario

Designing a marketplace that never says "sold out to the wrong person"

Amazon.com is a global marketplace with more than 300 million active customer accounts, ~200 million Prime members, and around 2 billion visits every month. It hosts a catalog of hundreds of millions of products from Amazon itself and roughly ~40% of sales come from ~2M third-party sellers. On Prime Day peaks, the checkout path handles tens of thousands of orders per minute while the browse path handles millions of requests per second against the product catalog and search. Any interview scenario labeled "design Amazon", "design an e-commerce platform", or "design a marketplace" is really asking you to justify how you'd hold this whole system up.

The interesting part isn't the shopping cart — it's the correctness under contention. Two customers click "Buy" on the last iPhone at the same moment. Only one may win. The order must be atomic across inventory, payment, and shipping, and if payment fails after the inventory hold, the hold must release. Prices change during a sale, so the price shown at checkout must match what's charged. Sellers upload millions of products a day; those must appear in search within seconds. The system must be eventually consistent for browsing (some cache lag is fine) but strongly consistent for money, inventory, and orders. This is why it's a favorite Amazon-and-adjacent interview: it tests everything — microservices, caching, sharding, event-driven flows, CAP trade-offs, and payments.

The real Amazon architecture is a case study in service-oriented decomposition: hundreds of independently owned services (catalog, cart, order, inventory, pricing, promotions, search, recommendations, fulfillment, payments, reviews, and dozens more), polyglot persistence (DynamoDB for cart and session state, relational for orders, S3 for images, Elasticsearch/OpenSearch for search, Redis for hot caches), and an event backbone (Kafka / Kinesis / SQS) that keeps everything eventually in sync. The rest of this page walks through it exactly like you'd whiteboard it in an interview.

🧑‍🎓 In laymen terms

Think of the world's biggest shopping mall — but invisible

Imagine the world's largest department store, plus a flea market where anyone can rent a stall, plus a courier company, plus a warehouse the size of a small city — all glued together. That's what Amazon is. You walk in with your phone, wander through millions of aisles, drop things into a basket, hand over your credit card, and a few days later a box appears at your door. Behind that simple experience is a small army of workers, each doing one specialized job.

Here's the story of one order. You type "wireless headphones" into a search box. A search worker (imagine a librarian who's read every book in the store) hands you a list of matches, sorted by what they think you'll like. You tap one — the catalog worker pulls the product page from a giant filing cabinet (a database), and a photo worker serves the images from a nearby locker close to your city so they load fast. You tap "Add to Cart" — a cart clerk writes it on your personal sticky note (your shopping cart) that follows you from your phone to your laptop.

Now the tricky part: checkout. You hit "Buy Now." At that moment, several workers scramble in perfect coordination. The inventory clerk checks the warehouse and puts a "reserved" tag on the last headphones. The payment clerk phones your bank and asks "may we charge $99?" — the bank says yes. The order clerk writes down that you own the headphones. The warehouse robot gets a message: "pick this, pack this, ship this." A few hours later a delivery driver has it. Every one of those steps must succeed together — if the bank declines, the "reserved" tag on the headphones has to be removed so another customer can buy them. This is the hardest part of building Amazon: making sure exactly one customer wins the last item, even if a million people click at the same second.

You've used this exact pattern on Amazon, Flipkart, eBay, Shopify stores, Walmart.com, Etsy, and Alibaba. Even food-delivery apps like DoorDash and Uber Eats work the same way — the "product" is dinner, the "warehouse" is the restaurant, the "delivery driver" is the same. Different names on the storefront; the workers behind the scenes are doing the same jobs.

One tiny example: you're one of a million people buying a $9 book on Prime Day. Even if everyone taps "Buy" in the same second, the store must sell exactly the copies it has, take exactly one payment per copy, and email exactly one order confirmation to each of you. No overselling, no double-charging, no lost orders — that's the promise.

  • Isolation: a failing recommendation service must not break checkout.

🏗️ High-level architecture

Microservices around a strong event backbone

The design breaks the monolith apart along business capabilities: each service owns its own database, its own team, and communicates through a mix of synchronous REST/gRPC (for read paths and checkout) and asynchronous events (for anything that can be eventually consistent). A Client (web / mobile / Alexa) hits CloudFront + Route 53, then the API Gateway / BFF layer (Backend-For-Frontend), which fans out to microservices. Read-heavy services front a Redis / DAX cache. Writes to inventory and orders go through an atomic checkout orchestrator that coordinates the SAGA across payment, inventory, and shipping. Every significant state change publishes an event to Kafka / Kinesis; downstream consumers (search indexer, recommender, notifications, analytics, ledger) subscribe to those events.

🔍 Component deep-dive

1. Catalog Service — the product master

The catalog stores hundreds of millions of products, each with metadata (title, description, category tree, dimensions, seller id, variant options like size/color). Because writes come from ~2M sellers and reads dominate (billions of page views), the catalog uses a hybrid store: a document DB (DynamoDB / MongoDB / Aurora Postgres with JSONB) as the source of truth, fronted by an aggressive Redis cache keyed by product id (SKU) with a TTL of hours. Product images live in S3 and are served from CloudFront so they load fast anywhere in the world. When a seller updates a listing, the write goes to the catalog DB and emits a catalog.updated event; consumers include the search indexer, the pricing service, and the cache invalidator.

2. Search — OpenSearch / Elasticsearch with tuned relevance

Search must return results in under half a second across ~600M documents. Amazon's approach: a separate OpenSearch cluster holds a denormalized document per product (title, keywords, brand, category, price, rating, popularity signal). The inverted index handles keyword matching; ML models re-rank the top-K by clickthrough and purchase probability. Faceted filters (price range, brand, rating) come from aggregations baked into the same query. The indexer is fed by the event backbone — a Kafka consumer tails catalog events and updates OpenSearch within seconds. For very hot queries ("iPhone 16"), the top result set is pre-computed and cached in Redis.

3. Cart Service — the DynamoDB darling

The shopping cart is one of the classic public DynamoDB workloads: low-latency writes, per-user isolation, must never lose your cart. The partition key is user_id; the sort key is added_at. Each cart item is an item in the table. Anonymous users get a browser cookie; logging in triggers a merge cart operation (union anonymous cart into user cart, dedupe by SKU). Cart TTL for anonymous users is 30 days. Multiple devices? Because it's centralized in DynamoDB, adding an item on your phone shows up on your laptop within milliseconds — no local state to sync.

4. Checkout Orchestrator — the SAGA

Placing an order requires changing state in ≥4 services atomically, but a distributed 2-phase commit is impractical at scale. The industry answer is the SAGA pattern: model checkout as a state machine — PENDING → INVENTORY_RESERVED → PAYMENT_AUTHORIZED → ORDER_CREATED → FULFILLMENT_QUEUED → COMPLETE. Each forward step has a compensating action (release inventory, void authorization, cancel order). If payment fails after inventory is reserved, the orchestrator emits release_inventory. The orchestrator can be either choreographed (each service reacts to events; simple but hard to reason about) or orchestrated (a central state machine like AWS Step Functions; easier to observe). Amazon's checkout is orchestrated. Every step is idempotent (same request twice = same result once) via an idempotency key tied to the customer's click.

5. Inventory Service — the "last item" problem

Two customers click "Buy" on the last iPhone at the same millisecond. Only one may win. Options: (a) Optimistic concurrency — check-and-set the row with a version number; the loser retries or fails. (b) Conditional DynamoDB update — UPDATE ... WHERE units_available >= N; DynamoDB atomically fails one of them. (c) Redis + Lua script for extreme QPS — atomically decrement a counter, reject on negative. Amazon's real inventory system is more nuanced: it distinguishes available, reserved, and committed stock, and holds reservations for a short window (say 10 minutes) so payment can finish without another shopper stealing the item. Reservations time out via a TTL sweeper.

6. Payment Service — idempotent and PCI-scoped

Payment is the most dangerous part of the flow because a bug can cost real money. Two rules: idempotency (retrying the same charge must never double-bill — enforced via a customer-generated idempotency key persisted server-side) and PCI scope isolation (raw card data touches only a tokenizer service or the PSP directly — everywhere else uses tokens). The service authorizes now, captures on shipment (industry standard). Chargebacks flow back as separate events into the ledger.

7. Order Service — the immutable ledger of truth

An order is an immutable business fact: order_id, user_id, items, total, currency, status, shipping_address. It lives in a relational store (Aurora Postgres / MySQL) because orders benefit from ACID transactions, foreign keys, and rich queries ("all orders by user X in Q3"). Partitioning is by user_id for read locality, or by order_date for archiving. Old orders (>2 years) tier down to S3 as Parquet for cheap analytical access. The Transactional Outbox pattern ensures every state change writes both the row and an event row in the same DB transaction; a relay ships the events to Kafka — no lost events, no phantom events.

8. Recommendation Service — offline batch + online model

"Customers who bought this also bought" is a collaborative filtering model trained offline on click and purchase logs. Amazon originated the item-to-item collaborative filtering paper (Linden et al., 2003) — for each item, precompute a similarity vector, store it in a key-value store, serve at request time. Modern recommenders are neural (matrix factorization + deep networks), trained on SageMaker and served via a real-time feature store + inference endpoint. Personalized homepage recos are a mix: batch-computed candidate set + online re-rank based on recent session behavior.

9. Notification, Reviews, Fulfillment, Fraud, and dozens more

Every state change fires an event; downstream services subscribe. Notification sends emails (SES), SMS (SNS), push (SNS mobile). Reviews lives in its own service with abuse detection. Fulfillment integrates with WMS (warehouse management), routes to the nearest FC (fulfillment center), and updates shipment status. Fraud & Risk scores every order in real time — a Kinesis stream + a ML model that can hold a suspicious order for manual review. Loyalty (Prime), Pricing, Promotions, and Returns are all their own services with the same shape.

10. Handling Prime Day / Black Friday

Predictable surge → pre-warm capacity. Amazon does weeks of capacity planning, pre-scales ECS/EKS clusters, doubles cache node counts, tests failover to secondary regions, and runs chaos-engineering game days. Non-critical services (recommendation, reviews) can be gracefully degraded — the site still works with a static "popular products" carousel if the recommender falls over. Static assets sit on CloudFront pre-warmed with the deals page. Rate limiters shed bot traffic. Fraud thresholds tighten. Prime Day is a load-testing exercise 364 days a year.

💻 C# implementation — an idempotent checkout SAGA step

The single most important checkout invariant is idempotency: the same "Buy Now" retried after a network blip must produce exactly one order. Below is a compact, production-shaped C# handler that: (1) enforces idempotency via a DynamoDB conditional put, (2) reserves inventory with an atomic conditional update, (3) authorizes payment, (4) writes the order + outbox event in a single transaction, (5) runs compensations if any step fails. This is the shape you'd draw in an interview.

using Amazon.DynamoDBv2;
using Amazon.DynamoDBv2.Model;

public class CheckoutSagaHandler
{
    private readonly IAmazonDynamoDB _db;
    private readonly IInventoryClient _inventory;
    private readonly IPaymentClient   _payment;
    private readonly IOrderRepository _orders;
    private readonly IEventPublisher  _events;

    // Idempotent entry point. The `idemKey` is generated by the client on
    // the "Buy Now" tap; retrying the same click reuses the same key and
    // returns the same OrderId — never a second charge.
    public async Task<CheckoutResult> PlaceOrderAsync(
        CheckoutRequest req, string idemKey, CancellationToken ct)
    {
        // -------- 1. Idempotency guard (DynamoDB conditional put) --------
        // If a record for this idemKey already exists, return its stored result.
        var claim = await TryClaimIdemKeyAsync(idemKey, req.UserId, ct);
        if (claim.AlreadyExisted) return claim.PreviousResult!;

        string? inventoryReservationId = null;
        string? paymentAuthId          = null;

        try
        {
            // -------- 2. Reserve inventory (atomic conditional decrement) --------
            inventoryReservationId = await _inventory.ReserveAsync(
                req.Items, ttl: TimeSpan.FromMinutes(10), ct);

            // -------- 3. Authorize payment (idempotent at PSP too) --------
            paymentAuthId = await _payment.AuthorizeAsync(
                req.UserId, req.PaymentToken, req.TotalAmount,
                idempotencyKey: idemKey, ct);

            // -------- 4. Write order + outbox event in one Aurora transaction --------
            var order = new Order(
                Id: Guid.NewGuid().ToString(),
                UserId: req.UserId,
                Items: req.Items,
                Total: req.TotalAmount,
                InventoryReservationId: inventoryReservationId,
                PaymentAuthId: paymentAuthId,
                Status: OrderStatus.Placed);

            await _orders.SaveWithOutboxAsync(order, new OrderCreatedEvent(order), ct);

            // -------- 5. Publish acknowledgment (best-effort — outbox relay
            //          will re-emit anyway if this Kafka call fails) --------
            _ = _events.PublishAsync(new OrderCreatedEvent(order), ct);

            var result = new CheckoutResult(order.Id, OrderStatus.Placed);
            await StoreIdemResultAsync(idemKey, result, ct);
            return result;
        }
        catch (PaymentDeclinedException)
        {
            // Compensating action: release the inventory hold. Inventory
            // service is idempotent — safe to retry until it succeeds.
            if (inventoryReservationId != null)
                await _inventory.ReleaseAsync(inventoryReservationId, ct);
            throw;
        }
        catch (Exception) when (inventoryReservationId != null)
        {
            // Any downstream failure -> compensate. If payment succeeded but
            // order write failed, void the auth too.
            if (paymentAuthId != null)
                await _payment.VoidAsync(paymentAuthId, ct);
            await _inventory.ReleaseAsync(inventoryReservationId, ct);
            throw;
        }
    }

    // Atomic "first writer wins" on the idempotency key.
    private async Task<IdemClaim> TryClaimIdemKeyAsync(
        string key, string userId, CancellationToken ct)
    {
        try
        {
            await _db.PutItemAsync(new PutItemRequest
            {
                TableName = "IdempotencyKeys",
                Item = new()
                {
                    ["pk"]     = new(key),
                    ["userId"] = new(userId),
                    ["state"]  = new("IN_PROGRESS"),
                    ["ttl"]    = new AttributeValue {
                        N = DateTimeOffset.UtcNow.AddHours(24).ToUnixTimeSeconds().ToString() }
                },
                ConditionExpression = "attribute_not_exists(pk)"
            }, ct);
            return new IdemClaim(AlreadyExisted: false);
        }
        catch (ConditionalCheckFailedException)
        {
            // Someone (probably this same user retrying) got here first.
            // Return the stored result so we don't reprocess.
            var existing = await LoadIdemResultAsync(key, ct);
            return new IdemClaim(AlreadyExisted: true, PreviousResult: existing);
        }
    }
}

Notes: the same pattern is idiomatic in Java (Spring + AWS SDK), Go (channels for compensations), and Python (FastAPI + Boto3). The interesting bit isn't the language — it's the idempotency key + conditional put + compensating actions. In a real system, AWS Step Functions holds the SAGA state machine and each service step is a Lambda or an ECS task; failure retries and compensations are declarative in the state machine ASL.

⚖️ Trade-offs, bottlenecks & likely follow-ups

Consistency: where to be strong vs eventual

Strong consistency is expensive; eventual is cheap. Amazon spends the expense where correctness demands it: inventory, payment, order, ledger. Everything else — reviews, recommendations, search, catalog reads, notifications — is eventually consistent. A review added at 12:00:00 might appear at 12:00:04 in search results. Nobody cares; a wrong order does.

The "two writes" problem — outbox pattern

Every write path that both updates a DB and publishes an event faces the "dual write" problem: DB commit succeeds, Kafka publish fails, event lost. The industry answer is the Transactional Outbox pattern: write the row and an events row in the same DB transaction; a relay (Debezium, MSK Connect) tails the DB WAL and ships events to Kafka. No lost events, no phantom events.

Hot partitions and celebrity SKUs

When Taylor Swift's book drops, one row melts. Options: (a) Split the row — 10 sub-shards, decrement any one, sum in the read path; (b) Redis in front — Lua-scripted atomic decrement; (c) Rate-limit writes to the row; (d) Waiting room UI that queues shoppers.

Region failover

Two active regions with DynamoDB Global Tables and Aurora Global Database. Traffic shifts via Route 53 health checks. RPO ≈ single-digit seconds. Because orders are the source of truth in Aurora, a region failover requires promoting the secondary region — which can take minutes. Read-only browsing degrades to the surviving region instantly.

Cache stampede on trending items

When the deals page invalidates and 100k shoppers hit the same SKU, they all miss the cache and pile onto DynamoDB. Fix: request coalescing at the cache tier (only one miss goes to origin, others wait), stale-while-revalidate, early expiration jitter, or a bloom-filter warmup.

Likely interviewer follow-ups

Q: How do you prevent overselling the last item under peak concurrency?

A: Atomic conditional decrement with the inventory service as the arbiter. DynamoDB conditional update or Redis with a Lua script — the storage engine picks one winner. Losers get a 409/retry. Reservation holds have a short TTL so failed payments release inventory automatically.

Q: Why not use a distributed transaction (2PC) across services?

A: 2PC blocks on the coordinator, doesn't scale to dozens of services, and has terrible availability during partitions. The industry uses SAGA instead — a sequence of local transactions plus compensating actions. Each service stays available; the trade-off is that intermediate states are visible (order can be "payment authorized but inventory released" for a moment).

Q: How do you make sure a duplicate "Buy Now" click doesn't double-charge me?

A: The client generates an idempotency key at click time. The checkout orchestrator does a conditional put on that key in DynamoDB — first click wins, retries reuse the stored result. The payment service also propagates the key to the PSP (Stripe supports this natively).

Q: How does search stay near-real-time when the catalog changes?

A: Catalog writes emit catalog.updated events to Kafka. A dedicated indexer consumer reads those and updates OpenSearch. End-to-end latency is a few seconds. For very hot deals during a sale, the indexer prioritizes the deals topic.

Q: How do recommendations work?

A: A hybrid: offline batch training (item-to-item CF, matrix factorization, GNNs) on SageMaker produces a candidate set per user, stored in DynamoDB. Online re-ranking blends recent session behavior with the candidate set at request time. The homepage carousel is a mix of session, purchase history, and trending items.

Q: Prime Day is coming — how do you make sure the site holds up?

A: Pre-scale ECS + cache node counts based on traffic forecasts. Load-test with real production shape (not synthetic!). Run chaos drills — kill nodes, fail a region — the week before. Turn on aggressive edge caching, tighten fraud thresholds, degrade non-critical services (reviews, recommendations) gracefully. Every year the org publishes a Prime Day post-mortem internally.
